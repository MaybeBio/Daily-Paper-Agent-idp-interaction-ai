## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (preprint, arXiv:2512.16150v1)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, internal consistency, and support for claims as presented in the provided text. No external literature verification or reproduction of results was performed.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors develop a combined coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CGMD) and generalized Flory–Huggins (FH) lattice model framework to predict interfacial energies and multiphase morphologies in DNA nanostar solutions. They report that Janus-like morphologies are ubiquitous in two-component systems, nested morphologies are rare and require large asymmetries in interfacial energies, and that interfacial energies can be tuned discretely via sticky-end distribution or continuously via crosslinker ratio.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text (Sections I–IV), Figures 1–7 (referenced), Equations 1–10, Methods section, references to Supplementary Figures S3, S4, S6–S11, Table S2, and Appendices A–C.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary Information (Appendices A–C, Figures S3–S11, Table S2) was not provided. This includes derivations of the FH model, the Cahn–Hilliard formalism, the diffusive phase-field simulation details, and additional validation data. The absence of these materials limits full verification of the theoretical derivations and the quantitative agreement claims.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a systematic and ambitious computational and theoretical study of multiphase DNA nanostar condensates. The combination of CGMD with a generalized FH lattice model is a sensible and potentially powerful approach. The central findings, that morphology is governed by interfacial energy inequalities and that Janus morphologies dominate while nested ones are rare, are physically plausible and align with existing continuum theories. However, the manuscript's impact is currently limited by the lack of direct experimental validation, the reliance on unprovided supplementary derivations, and some unresolved quantitative discrepancies between theory and simulation. The work is a solid contribution to the field of biomolecular condensates, but its claims of establishing "design principles" would be significantly strengthened by experimental corroboration or more detailed quantitative analysis.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of biomolecular condensates, DNA nanotechnology, soft matter physics, and biophysics. Specifically, those interested in the physical mechanisms governing multiphase morphology, the design of synthetic phase-separating systems, and the development of coarse-grained models for predicting phase behavior. The framework could be of interest to experimentalists working with DNA nanostars as model systems for condensates.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  The study addresses a significant and timely question: how microscopic molecular parameters control mesoscale morphology in multiphase condensates.
    2.  The combination of CGMD simulations with an analytical FH model is a powerful and complementary approach, providing both mechanistic insight and predictive capability.
    3.  The systematic exploration of parameter space (valence, size, flexibility, Debye length, sticky-end distribution) is thorough and provides a comprehensive picture.
    4.  The connection of morphology to interfacial energy inequalities provides a clear, physically intuitive framework for understanding and predicting phase behavior.
    5.  The manuscript is generally well-written and logically structured, making the complex material accessible.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No)
        - **Axis** Experimental Validation
        - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims to establish "design principles for constructing complex condensate architectures directly from microscopic molecular parameters" (Abstract) and that the framework can "predict" multiphase morphologies.
        - **Evidence pointer** All results are based on CGMD simulations and the FH model. No experimental data is presented.
        - **Concern** The entire predictive framework is validated only against the authors' own CGMD simulations. While this is a valid first step, the claim of establishing "design principles" for real-world systems is premature without any experimental verification. DNA nanostars are experimentally tractable, and the authors themselves note the challenges of experimental parameterization. A comparison with existing experimental data on DNA nanostar phase separation (e.g., from the Saleh lab, which is acknowledged) would significantly strengthen the claims.
        - **Why it matters** The central value proposition of the paper is its predictive power for designing experimental systems. Without experimental validation, the framework remains a purely theoretical construct, and its practical utility is unproven. The "design principles" are, at this stage, only simulation-based hypotheses.
        - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) include a direct comparison of their predicted phase diagrams or interfacial energies with existing experimental data on DNA nanostars, or (b) temper their claims of "design principles" and explicitly state that these are predictions awaiting experimental confirmation.
    - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No)
        - **Axis** Quantitative Agreement and Model Parameterization
        - **Claim pointer** The manuscript states that the FH model "closely reproduces the molecular dynamics results" (Section II.3) and shows "good agreement" (Section II.3) for phase diagrams and interfacial energies.
        - **Evidence pointer** Figures 2 and 4 (referenced), Equations 2, 6, and 10. The text mentions "small discrepancies" (Section II.3) and "noticeable discrepancies" (Section II.4) for specific cases.
        - **Concern** The nature and magnitude of the "small" and "noticeable" discrepancies are not quantified. The global fitting parameter C = 0.64 is introduced to account for discrepancies, but its physical basis is unclear. More critically, the FH model's predictive power is compromised if it requires a global fitting parameter that is not derived from first principles. The sensitivity of the model's predictions to this parameter is not discussed. The derivation of the FH model is in the unprovided Appendix A, making it impossible to assess the validity of the approximations made.
        - **Why it matters** The quantitative accuracy of the model is central to its predictive claims. If the model requires ad-hoc fitting parameters and still shows significant deviations, its utility as a predictive tool is diminished. The reader cannot fully evaluate the model's performance without the derivations and a quantitative error analysis.
        - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a quantitative comparison (e.g., percentage error) between theory and simulation for the key quantities in Figures 2 and 4. They should also discuss the physical interpretation of C and perform a sensitivity analysis. The full derivation of the FH model in the main text or a detailed summary in the supplement is essential.
    - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No)
        - **Axis** Morphology Prediction and Verification
        - **Claim pointer** The manuscript predicts morphologies (Janus, nested, etc.) based on interfacial energy inequalities (Section II.4) and presents a "unified morphology spectrum" (Section II.5) from large-scale simulations.
        - **Evidence pointer** Figures 4 and 5 (referenced), Section II.4 and II.5.
        - **Concern** The manuscript states that "large-scale simulations are required to directly capture the equilibrium morphologies" (Section II.4) to rigorously validate the predictions. However, the results of these large-scale simulations are only summarized in Figure 5, and the details are in the unprovided Figures S8–S11. It is unclear if the morphologies observed in these simulations are truly at equilibrium or are kinetically trapped metastable states. The timescales of the simulations (up to 6x10^8 steps) are mentioned, but the physical time this corresponds to is not discussed. The criteria for determining that a configuration has reached a "steady state" are not defined.
        - **Why it matters** The central claim of the paper is the ability to predict morphology. If the validation of these predictions relies on simulations that may not be at equilibrium, the conclusions are weakened. The reader needs to be convinced that the observed morphologies are the thermodynamically stable ones.
        - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the large-scale simulations, including a discussion of equilibration, potential kinetic trapping, and the physical timescales involved. A comparison of the observed morphologies with the phase diagram predictions for a wider range of parameters would be more convincing.
    - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No)
        - **Axis** Model Generalizability and Assumptions
        - **Claim pointer** The manuscript claims the framework can be applied to "an arbitrary number of components" (Section III) and that DNA nanostars are representative of "biomolecular condensates" (Introduction).
        - **Evidence pointer** Section III, Equations 3–5.
        - **Concern** The model assumes a single binding energy for all sticky-end pairs (ΔE_AA = ΔE_BB) and only considers repulsive interactions between different types. The extension to arbitrary numbers of components is stated but not demonstrated. The claim that DNA nanostars are representative of biomolecular condensates is an assumption that is not critically examined. The model does not account for sequence-specific effects beyond the simple A/B distinction, nor does it consider the role of RNA or protein-specific interactions in biological condensates.
        - **Why it matters** The generalizability of the framework is a key part of its appeal. If the model is only valid for a narrow set of conditions, its relevance to the broader field of biomolecular condensates is limited. The assumptions need to be clearly stated and their limitations discussed.
        - **Resolution test** The authors should explicitly discuss the limitations of their model in the context of biological condensates. They should also provide a proof-of-concept example or a more detailed theoretical discussion of how the model would be extended to more than two components.
- **Minor Comments**
    - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity, Affected element: Introduction, Evidence pointer: Section I)
        - **Issue** The introduction is somewhat broad and could be more focused. The connection between the specific problem of DNA nanostars and the general biological relevance could be made more explicit and earlier.
        - **Required correction** Consider restructuring the introduction to more directly motivate the use of DNA nanostars as a model system for understanding general principles of multiphase condensate morphology.
    - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility, Affected element: Methods, Evidence pointer: Section IV.1)
        - **Issue** The CGMD model parameters are described, but the exact values for all parameters (e.g., spring constants, charges, Lennard-Jones parameters if any) are not all given in the main text. They are presumably in Table S2.
        - **Required correction** Ensure that all simulation parameters are explicitly listed in the main text or in a clearly referenced table in the main text, to facilitate reproducibility.
    - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Figure Quality, Affected element: Figures 4 and 5, Evidence pointer: Section II.4, II.5)
        - **Issue** The insets in Figures 4b and 4c are described as illustrating morphologies, but the details are not visible in the text description.
        - **Required correction** Ensure that the inset figures are large enough to be interpretable and that the morphologies are clearly labeled and described in the figure captions.
    - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Terminology, Affected element: Section II.2, Evidence pointer: Figure 2)
        - **Issue** The term "upper critical binding energy" is used. This is an unusual term. Typically, one refers to an upper critical solution temperature (UCST) or a critical interaction strength.
        - **Required correction** Consider rephrasing to "critical binding energy" or "critical interaction strength" for clarity and consistency with standard terminology.
    - **R1-m5** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Discussion, Affected element: Section III, Evidence pointer: Discussion)
        - **Issue** The discussion of the biological implications is relatively brief. The authors mention nucleoli and stress granules but do not deeply connect their findings to specific biological questions or predictions that could be tested in vivo.
        - **Required correction** Expand the discussion to include more specific, testable predictions for biological systems, which would increase the impact and relevance of the work.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the framework establishes "design principles" for constructing complex condensate architectures is not fully supported without experimental validation.
- The claim of "good agreement" between theory and simulation is not quantitatively substantiated in the provided text.
- The prediction of a "unified morphology spectrum" is based on simulation results whose details (equilibration, parameter space) are not fully provided.
- The generalizability of the model to "an arbitrary number of components" is stated but not demonstrated.
- The assumption that DNA nanostars are representative of all biomolecular condensates is an overgeneralization that is not critically examined.
