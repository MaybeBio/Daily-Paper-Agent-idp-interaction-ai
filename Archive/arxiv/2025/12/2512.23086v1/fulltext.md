## Phase transition revealed by eigen microstate entropy

We introduce the eigen microstate entropy ( S EM S_{\text{EM}} ), a novel metric of complexity derived from the probabilities of statistically independent eigen microstates. After establishing its scaling behavior in equilibrium systems and demonstrating its utility in critical phenomena (mean spherical, Ising, and Potts models), we apply S EM S_{\text{EM}} to non-equilibrium complex systems. Our analysis reveals a consistent precursor signal: a significant increase in S EM S_{\text{EM}} precedes major phase transitions. Specifically, we observe this entropy rise before biomolecular condensate formation in liquid-liquid phase separation in living cells and months ahead of El Niño events. These findings position S EM S_{\text{EM}} as a general framework for detecting and interpreting phase transitions in non-equilibrium systems.


## 1 Introduction

As a fundamental concept in physics, entropy has undergone recent advancements, broadening its applications across physical sciences and interdisciplinary fields [ 49 , 11 , 18 , 15 , 7 , 28 , 10 , 39 ] . These advances underscore the effectiveness of entropy as a measure for characterizing phases of complex systems [ 31 , 9 ] . However, traditional entropy measures rely on the probability distribution over a system’s phase space [ 32 ] . While effective for model systems, it becomes intractable for complex systems with multiple components and high-dimensional nonlinear interactions [ 46 ] .

Recent advancements in simulation and observational techniques have yielded extensive phase space data, facilitating the characterization of large-scale behaviors without explicit phase space expressions [ 13 , 3 , 47 ] . These developments have informed various methods related to entropy. Configurational entropy [ 5 ] and the free energy landscape [ 50 ] , for instance, provide insights into how complex systems evolve towards stable states, often by identifying energy minima or calculating energy barriers [ 50 , 5 ] . Curl flux [ 52 , 55 ] and entropy production [ 1 , 48 , 42 ] utilize microscopic state transition probabilities to extract information on system non-equilibrium effects. The computable information density [ 33 ] , as a proxy of entropy, has been applied to capture order and critical behavior in complex systems. While these methods offer valuable perspectives, many are constrained by the requirement of an energy function, specific mathematical formulations, or may not fully account for correlations between sampled configurations. Consequently, we are motivated to develop an entropy measure that addresses these challenges, providing a more versatile approach to understanding complex systems.

In this work, we introduce a data-driven entropy measure, the eigen microstate entropy ( S EM S_{\text{EM}} ). S EM S_{\text{EM}} is based on the probability distribution of eigen microstates, which describes the statistical properties of an ensemble by considering mutually orthogonal eigen microstates within it [ 19 , 58 , 27 , 54 ] . This approach offers a possibility to eliminate the ambiguity in entropy definitions caused by statistical correlations among sampled data. We validate S EM S_{\text{EM}} using representative models, including various Ising and Potts models, and demonstrate its advantages by analyzing challenging non-equilibrium systems and experimental scenarios. Specifically, we apply S EM S_{\text{EM}} to address several long-standing problems, including phase transitions in frustrated Ising systems, liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), and El Niño prediction.

## 4 Eigen microstate entropy in living systems

To demonstrate the applicability of S EM S_{\text{EM}} in real-world systems, we apply it to study LLPS in living cells — a ubiquitous non-equilibrium process where biomolecules assemble into membraneless compartments, playing crucial roles in various cellular functions [ 17 , 44 , 24 , 8 , 53 ] . Using stress granules formation in human osteosarcoma cells (U2OS) expressing EGFP-G3BP1 as a LLPS experiment model [ 56 , 57 ] , we initiate the LLPS by adding sodium arsenite to the cell culture medium, and image the whole process in real time under an inverted spinning-disk confocal microscope, with a total of 5400 s at 1 s intervals (shown in the supplementary data [ 12 ] ). The LLPS process is shown in figure 3 (a): Initially, the cells exhibit a homogeneous fluorescence image; small condensates then appear and coalesce into larger droplets after approximately 1700 s.

Since the fluorescence intensity of each pixel directly reflects the local protein density, we define the microstate as 𝒔 ⁡ ( τ ) = [ I 1 ​ ( τ ) , … , I N ​ ( τ ) ] 𝖳 \bm{s}(\tau)=[I_{1}(\tau),\dots,I_{N}(\tau)]^{\mathsf{T}} , where I i ​ ( τ ) I_{i}(\tau) denotes the fluorescence intensity of pixel i i at time τ \tau , and N N is the pixel count. For the time-series data from empirical measurements, such as 𝒔 ⁡ ( τ ) \bm{s}(\tau) here, we analyze the time evolution of S EM S_{\text{EM}} using a sliding time window. Specifically, we construct an ensemble matrix 𝐀 ⁡ ( τ ) = [ 𝒔 ⁡ ( τ − 150 ) , … , 𝒔 ⁡ ( τ + 149 ) ] \bm{\mathrm{A}}(\tau)=[\bm{s}(\tau-150),\dots,\bm{s}(\tau+149)] over a 300-second window. By decomposing the time-dependent ensemble matrix, we track the entropy evolution, as shown in figure 3 (b). Interestingly, unlike the monotonic behavior observed in equilibrium models, biological LLPS exhibits a non-monotonic S EM S_{\text{EM}} curve characterized by an initial increase peaking around 1700 s, followed by a decrease. This behavior is supported by the derivative of S EM S_{\text{EM}} (inset of figure 3 (b)) and is confirmed to be independent of the time-window length (shown in the supplementary data [ 12 ] ).

The temporal evolution of S EM S_{\text{EM}} provides insight into the complex process of LLPS. The value of S EM S_{\text{EM}} depends on { p I } \{p_{I}\} : a more uniform distribution results in a higher S EM S_{\text{EM}} . Initially, the system is dominated by a homogeneous 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} (figure 3 (c)) with p 1 ≈ 0.9 p_{1}\approx 0.9 , consistent with fluorescent images showing no LLPS droplets (figure 3 (a)). From 0 to 1700 s, p 1 p_{1} decreases while other probabilities like p 2 p_{2} and p 3 p_{3} increase (figure 3 (d)), leading to a more uniform { p I } \{p_{I}\} distribution and resulting in a rising S EM S_{\text{EM}} ; when S EM S_{\text{EM}} reaches its maximum at 1700 s, the homogeneous 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} is replaced by a heterogeneous pattern with small spots (figure 3 (c)), indicating the emergence of a new phase. From 1700 to 5400 s, the gradual rise in new phase ratio, p 1 p_{1} , leads to a reduction in S EM S_{\text{EM}} . This reduction corresponds to the known biological process where EGFP-G3BP1 condensates grow and aggregate into large droplets (shown in the supplementary data [ 12 ] ).

The latter stage of biological LLPS and the cooling process of the Ising model both show a decrease in S EM S_{\text{EM}} , which results from the growth of the new ordered phase. However, contrary to intuition, the intracellular LLPS exhibits an initial increase in S EM S_{\text{EM}} , during which the appearance of small spots in 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} (figure 3 (c)) implies that proteins are beginning to spatially correlate without yet forming observable condensates. This finding is consistent with recent experiments where protein clusters were observed prior to condensations [ 25 , 37 ] , which may facilitate protein condensation and play a crucial role in cellular assembly [ 41 , 38 ] .


## 5 S EM S_{\text{EM}} as a precursor signal of El Niño events

El Niño, in which non-linear interactions play a significant role, is a climate pattern with abnormal warming of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean that influences the global climate system [ 35 ] . It is typically identified by sea surface temperatures at least 0.5 ∘ ​ C 0.5^{\circ}\text{C} above average within the Niño 3.4 region ( 5 ∘ ​ S − 5 ∘ ​ N 5^{\circ}\text{S}-5^{\circ}\text{N} , 170 ∘ ​ W − 120 ∘ ​ W 170^{\circ}\text{W}-120^{\circ}\text{W} ) for a sustained period [ 59 ] . We apply S EM S_{\text{EM}} to study the El Niño events using surface air temperature (SAT) data from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset at a spatial resolution of 1 ∘ × 1 ∘ 1^{\circ}\times 1^{\circ} . We select the extreme 1997/1998 El Niño event, recognized as the strongest Eastern Pacific (EP) El Niño on record [ 40 ] , as a case study. The Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), defined as the 3-month running mean of anomaly temperature in the Niño 3.4 region, is the primary indicator for identifying El Niño events, with values above 0.5 ∘ ​ C 0.5^{\circ}\text{C} typically indicating an El Niño event (see the blue regime in figure 4 (a)) [ 16 ] . As shown in figure 4 (a), S EM S_{\text{EM}} rises sharply about seven months before the onset of El Niño in May 1997, while it then gradually declines before this El Niño event ends in May 1998. The increase in entropy that occurs several months before onset can be considered as a precursor signal.

By analyzing the weights of the dominant eigen microstates, we can understand the behavior of entropy increase. In the early phase of El Niño development (approximately 10 months before onset), the system is dominated by a zonally homogeneous 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} (figure 4 (b)) with p 1 ≈ 0.6 p_{1}\approx 0.6 . As time evolves, p 1 p_{1} decreases significantly, while other probabilities such as p 2 p_{2} and p 3 p_{3} increase (figure 4 (c)), leading to a more uniform distribution of { p I } \{p_{I}\} and consequently an increasing S EM S_{\text{EM}} . When S EM S_{\text{EM}} reaches its peak (in May 1997), the initially homogeneous 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} transitions to a zonally heterogeneous pattern (figure 4 (b)). Thereafter, with the gradual increase of p 1 p_{1} and a decline in S EM S_{\text{EM}} , 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} again becomes dominant, reflecting the system’s transition into a new ordered phase as the El Niño event occurs. The emergence of zonal heterogeneity in 𝑼 1 \bm{U}_{1} likely reflects localized warming anomalies near the South American coast—a distinctive feature of EP El Niño events [ 14 ] . This structural change may be associated with the destabilization of equatorial waves, particularly downwelling Kelvin waves, which is crucial in the development of El Niño by depressing the thermocline and warming the surface waters in the EP [ 34 , 51 ] . Similar behaviors of S EM S_{\text{EM}} have been observed for other significant El Niño events in recent decades (shown in the supplementary data [ 12 ] ).


## 6 Conclusion

We have successfully developed an entropy measure, S EM S_{\text{EM}} , based on statistically independent eigen microstates. Grounded in the concept of statistical ensembles, this entropy measure plays a foundational role for the investigation of general non-equilibrium statistical systems. Because S EM S_{\text{EM}} is not constrained by the definition of an energy function or specific mathematical formulations, it is highly suitable for studying data-driven complex systems. For model systems, we established well-defined FSS behavior of S EM S_{\text{EM}} , verified by exact solutions in the mean spherical model, and applied it to determine the criticality of the frustrated Ising model, a long-standing issue. We have obtained meaningful results in both experimental and empirical systems. In LLPS, the transition from one ordered phase to another involves an intermediate entropy increase. In El Niño events, we observed similar entropy dynamics and demonstrate that S EM S_{\text{EM}} can serve as a precursor signal.

Our entropy definition is based on the concept of eigen microstates and enables the analysis of dominant eigen modes to enhance interpretability beyond entropy signals. It is worth mentioning that the number of eigenvalues of a system is much smaller than the dimension of the phase space, implying the advantage of dimensionality reduction, making it particularly suitable for big-data, high-dimensional complex systems. Moreover, the divergence of relaxation times near a critical point, known as the critical slowing down (CSD), is characterized by a decreasing S EM S_{\text{EM}} , resulting from a more concentrated distribution of { p I } \{p_{I}\} as the system lingers in a smaller subset of states (discussed in detail in supplementary data [ 12 ] ). Applying our method to study CSD in climate tipping points [ 29 , 36 , 6 ] and glassy dynamics represents an intriguing direction for future research.
