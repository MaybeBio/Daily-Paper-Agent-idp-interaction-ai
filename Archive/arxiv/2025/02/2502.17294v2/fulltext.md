## Integrating protein sequence embeddings with structure via graph-based deep learning for the prediction of single-residue properties

Understanding the intertwined contributions of amino acid sequence and spatial structure is essential to explain protein behaviour. Here, we introduce INFUSSE (Integrated Network Framework Unifying Structure and Sequence Embeddings), a Deep Learning framework that combines sequence embeddings, generated by a Large Language Model (LLM), with graph-based representations of protein structures, integrated through a diffusive Graph Convolutional Network (diff-GCN), to predict single-residue properties within proteins. Our approach follows two steps. First, we fine-tune LLM sequence embeddings obtained from bidirectional transformers to make predictions from protein sequence alone. Second, we combine these enriched sequence representations with a geometric graph Laplacian within diff-GCN to refine the initial predictions. This approach leads to improved predictions while allowing us to systematically disentangle the contribution of sequence and structure. We illustrate our framework by applying it to the prediction of local residue flexibility (B-factors) of antibody-antigen complexes, and show that it provides improved performance compared to current Machine Learning (ML) approaches. The addition of structural information via geometric graphs is shown to enhance predictions especially for intrinsically disordered regions, protein-protein interaction sites, and highly variable amino acid positions.

Keywords: Antibody, Deep Learning, Graph-based Learning, Interpretability, Large Language Model, Protein Structure


## 1 Introduction

Protein function is the result of the complex interplay of amino acid sequence leading to a highly attuned physico-chemical spatial structure. The development of sequencing technologies and the proliferation of large-scale repositories have resulted in a wealth of protein sequence data 1 . There is also increasing accessibility to three-dimensional protein structures, available through the Protein Data Bank (PDB) 2 . Bringing together both sources of information remains a critical area of study 3 , specifically determining how and in which contexts structural data can significantly improve the predictions of global or single-residue properties of proteins.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) based on transformer architectures have revolutionised protein modelling and design by harnessing their ability to capture complex patterns and long-range dependencies within protein sequences. Traditionally, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) had struggled with exploding and vanishing gradients, and hence were not useful for long sequences 4 , 5 , 6 , whereas improvements via Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) 7 were still limited to a finite-context window, i.e. , short- and medium-range sequence correlations. In contrast, transformers utilise the self-attention mechanism to process the entire sequence at once, thus capturing long-range dependencies more efficiently 8 . A leading example of this architecture is ProtBERT 9 , which was trained on more than 200 million protein sequences demonstrating high performance in sequence-based predictions 10 , 11 . A key feature of ProtBERT is that it generates sequence embeddings, i.e. , enriched sequence representations that capture biological information and lead to improved predictions relative to one-hot encoding of amino acid types 12 .

LLMs have demonstrated their ability to predict protein properties from sequence data alone, yet the addition of structural information often unlocks deeper insights and enhances performance 13 . Structural information can be effectively represented using graphs, with nodes corresponding to atoms or residues and edges encoding their interactions or geometric relationships. Atomistic graphs have been employed to identify rigid and flexible regions within proteins 14 , 15 , 16 , unravel allosteric mechanisms 17 , 18 , 19 , and deepen our understanding of protein-protein interactions 20 . Coarse-grained protein models at the residue level have also proven effective in various applications 21 , including protein folding 22 , 23 , 24 , aggregation studies 25 , protein-protein docking 26 , allosteric functional modulation 27 and protein flexibility prediction 28 .

However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing framework systematically integrates sequence embeddings generated by a Large Language Model (LLM) with graph-based structural information to predict single-residue properties, and no studies have quantitatively assessed the individual contributions of sequence and structural data in prediction tasks. In this work, we introduce INFUSSE, a Deep Learning framework that combines LLM sequence embeddings with graph representations of protein structures integrated through a diffusive Graph Convolutional Network (diff-GCN) 29 , to predict single-residue properties within proteins.

Although our framework is generally applicable to any single-residue property, we illustrate our framework through the prediction of local residue flexibility (B-factors) of antibody-antigen complexes. B-factors, also referred to as Debye-Waller or temperature factors, measure how much atoms deviate from their average position. In general, higher B-factors correspond to greater thermal motion at a given temperature 30 . B-factors provide insights about the dynamics of proteins 31 , as intrinsic flexibility correlates with conformational changes, thus connecting structure and function 32 , 33 .

Previous studies have explored several approaches to predicting B-factors in general proteins. Ref. 34 relied on Gaussian Network Models (GNMs) 35 and persistent homology concepts. Ref. 30 applied a range of Machine Learning techniques, including Random Forests, Gradient-Boosted Trees and Convolutional Neural Networks, to various features derived from PDB data (atomic coordinates, R-value, resolution, total number of heavy atoms, amino acid types, secondary structure) and achieved a maximum Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) of 0.66 0.66 with resolution being the most important feature. Recently, Ref. 36 applied a bidirectional LSTM with input data comprising atomic spatial coordinates, primary sequence, secondary structure, and chain-break information, and obtained a PCC of 0.8 0.8 on general proteins. However, we found that using this LSTM architecture for B-factor prediction in proteins with highly flexible, highly variable regions, as is the case in antibodies, leads to much lower performance (PCC of 0.48 0.48 ). Our results demonstrate that the combination of sequence-derived embeddings and weighted geometric graphs within INFUSSE leads to improved performance, and also reveals the different scenarios in which graph-based representations enhance predictive power.

## Training of the model

Our task is the prediction of a residue-level property (B-factors), which, from a graph learning perspective, corresponds to a node regression task. Note that, following Ref. 36 , we predict standardised B-factors (zero mean, unit variance), as they better reflect the dynamics of different protein regions. Furthermore, we do not use the resolution of the structure as an input in our analysis.

We produce splits of the 1510 1510 PDBs into training and test sets in a 95/5 ratio; hence for each split, the training set 𝒫 \mathcal{P} and the test set 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} contain 1435 1435 and 75 75 PDB entries, respectively. To avoid redundancy between the training and test sets, we ensure that the sequence identity of each complex is below a 90% threshold 54 . To achieve this, we randomly select an antibody-antigen complex and it is only added to the test set if it satisfies this threshold for all the remaining complexes in the training set; otherwise, it is rejected and reassigned to the training set. This process is repeated until the desired size of the test set is reached, see Ref. 55 . We produced 5 such splits to test the robustness of our results.

## INFUSSE outperforms baseline graph models and generic protein SOTA models for B-factor prediction

## INFUSSE disentangles sequence and structure contributions to predictions

INFUSSE’s two-step training allows for disentangling the sequence and structure contributions for B-factor predictions. A key research question consists in determining in which scenarios the addition of structural information via graphs can significantly improve predictions. We address this question by analysing the graph-induced improved performance at the residue level between INFUSSE (LLM + diff-GCN) and LLM (sequence alone), which we denote Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} (Methods), varies as a function of different residue properties.

From a general point of view, our study revealed that incorporating graph information consistently enhances B-factor predictions ( Figure 2 A , Figure S1 ). We highlight that Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} is predominantly positive, hence can be interpreted as the gain of the graph . Comparing the y-axis ranges in Figure 2 A and Figure S1 , reveals a consistently lower Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} for the light chain than for the heavy chain. This aligns with the lower sequence variability of light chains compared to heavy chains 60 .

The highest deviations between the two MSE curves correspond to insertion positions that are underrepresented in the dataset, i.e. , positions where only a limited number of antibodies contain an amino acid, or regions characterised by high diversity in residue type. We statistically assessed the latter point by analysing the magnitude of Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} for different entropy ranges. Residue entropy at a given position indicates the statistical variability of amino acids at that position (Methods). Our results show a statistically significant difference in the means of Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} for entropy values in the ranges 0 − 1 0-1 and 1 − 2 1-2 , as well as 1 − 2 1-2 and > 2 >2 (p-values < 10 − 6 <10^{-6} ). This suggests that the inclusion of graph information is more beneficial for regions with higher residue diversity.


## Geometric graphs improve predictions via corrections that correlate with regions and secondary structure

We next examined the impact on Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} of the secondary structure and the classification of antibody variable region residues as belonging to either a Framework (FR) or Complementarity-determining region (CDR). As pointed out by Ref. 58 , CDRs are often associated to unstructured, highly variable regions. We obtain consistently higher Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} values for amino acids in CDRs compared to those in FRs, as shown in Figure 3 A . An analysis of secondary structure for antigen amino acids in the test set revealed higher Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} values for unstructured motifs, such as loops, than for β \beta -strands and α \alpha -helices (see Figure 3 B ). When we further subdivided the results of Figure 3 A based on antibody secondary structure, we observed that FR residues followed a similar trend to antigens. Nonetheless, this is not the case for CDRs. Not only are α \alpha -helices typically shorter in CDRs than in antigens (see Figure 3 D ), but when present, they tend be structurally different across antibodies and fully exposed to the antigen, as they play a critical role in binding 61 . Our results show that the means of Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} are comparable between loops and α \alpha -helices in the CDRs, with both being significantly higher than that of β \beta -strands.


## Graph information enhances B-factor predictions for paratope and epitope members

Sequence-based approaches often struggle to accurately identify paratope and epitope residues due to their inability to capture how chains fold and interact within an antibody-antigen complex 62 , and therefore how they interact. We consider the human monoclonal antibody 237235 in complex with Plasmodium vivax reticulocyte binding protein 2b (PDB entry: 6wm9 ), and show in Figure 4 A that the highest values of Δ graph ( q ) \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}}^{(q)} are achieved in paratope and epitope members. The remaining peaks are related to the presence of unstructured regions or contacts between different portions of the same chain (see Figure S3 for an in-depth analysis).

Extending this study to the entire test set, we find that the graph information systematically improves predictions of local flexibility especially for residues belonging to the epitope-paratope interface. Specifically, this is evidenced by a high Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} for paratope and epitope members than for those residues outside the antibody-antigen interface (see Figures 4 B,C ). This enhancement arises from introducing spatial constraints via the graph, which effectively reduces the degrees of freedom of the paratope and epitope amino acids.


## 4 Discussion

In this work, we develop INFUSSE, which integrates a Language Model and a diffusive Graph Convolutional Network for antibody-antigen B-factor prediction, achieving greater accuracy than existing methods on the same test sets (Table 1). Our approach utilises enriched amino acid embeddings from a pre-trained Large Language Model (LLM), in this case ProtBERT, to capture sequence and structural features without the need for manual feature selection. We show that these enriched embeddings better inform the diff-GCN compared to directly using simply the sequence composition, thereby resulting in a better performance.

We define sequence and graph blocks and adopt a two-step training approach. The sequence block is initially trained by fine-tuning an LLM for B-factor prediction employing sequence information alone. During this step, the LLM’s weights remain frozen while new learnable layers are introduced. Next, geometric graphs are incorporated through the graph block, which consists of the diff-GCN, and the weights of the entire architecture are updated to refine the initial predictions. The improvement in performance between INFUSSE (LLM + diff-GCN) and LLM (sequence alone) predictions (denoted as Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} ) allowed us to disentagle the contribution of sequence and graph-mediated structural information to the prediction of residue-specific properties. To gain biological insight out of the model’s predictions, we examine whether Δ graph \Delta_{\mathrm{graph}} is greater in cases involving amino acids that are challenging to predict, such as those in unstructured or diverse regions, or within the paratope or epitope.

We provide statistical evidence that the addition of graph information significantly enhances performance with respect to the sequence-only case for predictions related to local structural flexibility. This is particularly evident when dealing with amino acids in a CDR over a framework, especially when characterised as a loop or α \alpha -helix in a CDR, as opposed to other types of secondary structure. This improvement is also notable when these amino acids are situated in highly diverse positions, and when part of either the paratope or epitope. Models like AlphaFold2 rely heavily on multiple sequence alignment (MSA) to capture the evolutionary information 63 . However, they are known to struggle with highly diverse or unstructured regions, such as the CDR loops 64 . We show that these regions coincide with those in which LLMs such as ProtBERT present limited accuracy, even after fine-tuning for the specific downstream tasks. In such cases, accurate single-property predictions require graph information, suggesting that these amino acids likely correspond to areas less subject to strong functional constraints, hence with higher potential as targets for protein engineering.

Despite the prevalence of B-factor measurements in most PDB files, quality can vary, and some studies indicate potential issues with these measurements 41 . Currently, we exclude structures exhibiting B-factors exceeding a specific threshold (see Methods). This node regression framework is versatile and not limited to B-factor prediction, which is why we intend to explore other applications in the future, such as epitope prediction or cysteine (CYS) reactivity prediction 65 .

Our framework could help understand the role of local residue flexibility in target-binding especially for antibodies, where the determinants of high binding affinity and specificity remain a formidable open question and a sought-after target in antibody discovery. While there is not a perfect correlation between high affinity and rigidity 66 , matured antibodies typically exhibit greater rigidity in the CDR loops compared to their naïve counterparts. Understanding amino acid neighbourhoods associated with high and low B-factors could provide insights into the affinity maturation process and thus help us to propose mutations. Additionally, in future work INFUSSE could serve to validate in silico the claim in Ref. 66 that antibodies with intermediate affinity maturation may be more flexible than their germline counterparts if there is initially a strong binding to the conserved antigen sites.
