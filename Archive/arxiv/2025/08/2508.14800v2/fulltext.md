We simulate TIP4P/2005 water in the temperature range of 257 K to 318 K with time steps of δ = \delta= 0.25, 0.50, and 2.0 fs. Within the computed statistical uncertainties, the density-temperature behavior obtained using 0.25 fs and 0.50 fs are in excellent agreement with each other but differ from those obtained using δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs, a choice that leads to a breakdown of equipartition. The temperature of maximum density (TMD) is 277.15 K with δ ​ t = \delta t= 0.25 fs or 0.50 fs, but is shifted to 273.15 K for simulations with δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs. This shift is comparable in magnitude to the shift in TMD due to nuclear quantum effects, emphasizing the care required in the parameterization and classical statistical mechanical simulation of a fluid that displays nontrivial nuclear quantum effects under ambient conditions. Enhancing the water-water dispersion interaction, as has been recommended for modeling disordered solvated proteins, degrades the description of the liquid-vapor phase envelope.

The liquid-to-solid transition of most liquids is accompanied by an increase in density. Water is a notable exception. Liquid water has a density maximum at 4 ∘ C (277.15 K). For temperatures below 4 ∘ C, the density of the liquid decreases up to the freezing point, and the solid phase (ice 1h) has a lower density than the liquid. This allows ice to float on water and is critical for life on planet Earth.

Capturing the temperature of maximum density (TMD) is a sensitive test of the computer simulation of liquid water Guillot 2002 . In this letter we restrict our attention to classical statistical mechanical simulations, which is of first interest in the simulation of large biomolecular systems. It helps to remember, however, that the TMD shows a strong nuclear quantum effect: the TMD of D 2 O is about 11.6 ∘ 11.6^{\circ} C and that of T 2 O being about 13.4 ∘ 13.4^{\circ} C.

Among classical statistical mechanical simulations, it is well known that the otherwise excellent three-site model SPC/E shows a density maximum deep in the supercooled regime ( T < 240 T<240 K) Guillot 2002 . The four-site TIP4P model improves the TMD to around 250 K Guillot 2002 . Abascal and Vega re-parametrized the TIP4P model including information about the ice phases Abascal and Vega 2005 . They used N ​ p ​ T NpT Monte Carlo simulations on a small system comprising 360 water-molecules to show that the resulting TIP4P/2005 model captures several of the properties of the liquid very well. Indeed, the TMD is improved to around 270 K (see Fig. 1 below), although the unavoidable scatter because of small system suggests that the TMD could be higher. Note that in computer simulations, to the best of our knowledge, the TMD is obtained at either 1 bar or 1 atm rather than the saturation pressure. This approximation is acceptable because the Poynting pressure correction is negligible for liquid water for the conditions of interest. We will follow this practice in our work.

In an effort to better describe the ensemble of expanded conformations of intrinsically disordered proteins, Shaw and coworkers noted that water models typically used in simulations underestimate the dispersion interaction Piana et al. 2015 . To this end, they re-parameterized the TIP4P/2005 water model by strengthening the dispersion interactions with reference to quantum chemical calculations while adjusting the partial charges to match target properties. From molecular dynamics simulations at 1 bar and a time-step of δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs, they note that the TMD of TIP4P/2005 water is 275 K and the TMD of their re-parameterized TIP4P-D model is 270 K.

Recently, we have shown that the δ ​ t \delta t used in molecular dynamics simulations of liquid water has non-negligible impacts on the computed properties. In particular, we have shown that δ ​ t > 0.5 \delta t>0.5 fs leads to the breakdown of equipartition in the SPC/E and TIP3P models of water Asthagiri and Beck 2024 ; Asthagiri et al. 2025 . (See also the Refs. 6 ; 7 .) Ultimately, this breakdown can be traced to the requirement of using a small time-step for capturing the fast rotational relaxation of water Asthagiri and Beck 2024 , a point that Rahman and Stillinger Rahman and Stillinger 1971 ; Stillinger and Rahman 1974 had already noted in their early simulations of liquid water. We showed that the breakdown of equipartition changes the p − V p-V behavior, and in particular, for the liquid phase a time-step that breaks equipartition also leads to a larger volume for a given target pressure. Motivated by these observations, here we map the liquid-vapor coexistence curve for the SPC/E, TIP3P, TIP4P/2005, and TIP4P-D water models in simulations that obey equipartition. Importantly, we find that with a choice of δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs, a choice that breaks equipartition, the TMD of TIP4P/2005 is shifted by about 4 K.

We simulate a system comprising 32,768 water molecules, initially occupying a cube of volume 100 × 100 × 100 100\times 100\times 100 Å 3 (mass density of 980.264 kg/m 3 ). The temperature is maintained using the canonical stochastic velocity rescaling thermostat Bussi et al. 2007 and the pressure of 1 bar is maintained using a Monte Carlo barostat Chow and Ferguson 1995 ; Åqvist et al. 2004 . The coupling time for the thermostat is 1 ps and the volume moves are attempted every 40 fs (160 steps for δ ​ t = 0.25 \delta t=0.25 fs, 80 steps for δ ​ t = 0.5 \delta t=0.5 fs, and 20 steps for δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs, respectively). Throughout electrostatic interactions are obtained using particle mesh Ewald summation. The PME grid spacing is set to 1 Å and the tolerance for energy at the cutoff length is 10 − 7 10^{-7} , a factor of 10 tighter than the default in the NAMD code. The cutoff distance for real-space electrostatic and LJ dispersion interactions is 10 Å. For the TIP4 models we include analytical corrections for long-range LJ interactions.

For simulations with TIP4P/2005 (with δ ​ t = 0.25 \delta t=0.25 fs), we simulated the system for over 10 ns and set aside the first 2.5 ns for equilibration. (The time for equilibration is rather conservative, as the volume relaxes within 0.25 ns.) We follow the same length of simulation when using δ ​ t = 0.50 \delta t=0.50 fs. For simulations with δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs, a time-step that breaks equipartition Asthagiri and Beck 2024 ; Asthagiri et al. 2025 , we simulated the system for 34.2 ns, setting aside the first 10 ns for equilibration. For simulations with TIP4P-D (with δ ​ t = 0.25 \delta t=0.25 fs), we simulated the system for 4.5 ns, setting aside the first 2.5 ns for equilibration. For all simulations, data was logged every 500 steps. Statistical uncertainties were obtained using the Friedberg-Cameron approach Friedberg and Cameron 1970 ; Allen and Tildesley 1987 .

Figure 1 shows that the TIP4P/2005 model captures the TMD in excellent agreement with experiments. The simulated density at 277.15 K is only 0.01% higher than the experimental value. Note also the inevitable scatter (and high estimated uncertainty of ± 1 \pm 1 kg/m 3 ) in the original Monte Carlo simulations of the TIP4P/2005 model; this is understandable given the small system size and much constrained sampling length. The excellent agreement between the ρ \rho -vs- T T behavior for the two indicated time-steps for integrating the equations of motion emphasizes that if care is taken to ensure equipartition Asthagiri and Beck 2024 ; Asthagiri et al. 2025 , the results are insensitive to the time-step.

Having established the expected behavior in simulations that obey equipartition, we next consider what happens when the time-step is large enough that equipartition does not hold. Figure 2 shows that the breakdown of equipartition shifts the phase envelope, and for the choice of δ ​ t = 2.0 \delta t=2.0 fs leads to a TMD of 273.15 K. It is interesting to note that this value is close to the value of 275 K that Shaw and coworkers Piana et al. 2015 quote as the TMD of TIP4P/2005 water in their simulations using a time-step of 2 fs. While the agreement is encouraging, we must caution that in simulations that do not obey equipartition, the dependence on the choice of thermostat and barostat never goes away Asthagiri et al. 2025 , and this perhaps explains the remaining deviation between the two results.

In Figure 3 we reconsider the role of enhancing the water-water dispersion interaction in simulations with a small time-step for integrating the equations of motion. Modifying the TIP4P/2005 model by enhancing the dispersion interaction worsens the predicted ρ \rho -vs- T T behavior. The TMD calculated here is shifted to 269.15 K, which can be compared to the 270 K reported in Ref. 3 . The density at the TMD is also lower, being in error relative to experiments by about 0.3%, which can be compared to the 0.01% deviation noted earlier for TIP4P/2005. These results encourage reconsidering the hypothesis that it is the weaker water-water dispersion interaction that is the source of the problems in capturing the conformations of intrinsically disordered peptides within the Amber99SB-ILDN protein force-field. We showed earlier using theory and computer simulations Tomar et al. 2016 ; Asthagiri et al. 2017 ; Tomar et al. 2020 that protein-water hydrophilic (attractive) interactions are decisive in the solution thermodynamics and conformational behavior. Juxtaposing that result with the observations in Ref. 3 suggests that enhancing the water-water dispersion interaction serves to enhance the protein-water hydrophilic interaction to compensate for the deficiencies in the Amber99SB-ILDN protein force-field.

For completeness, we also show the saturation curves for the 3-site SPC/E and CHARMM-modified TIP3P models (Figure 4 ). We followed the procedure for the TIP4 models, except that we performed 5.25 5.25 ns of total simulation with δ ​ t = 0.25 \delta t=0.25 ns, and set aside the first 2.5 ns for equilibration. We also do not apply the long-range LJ correction. For ambient conditions, as has been documented before for SPC/E-vs-TIP3P Guillot 2002 , we find that the SPC/E water model is significantly superior to the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model in capturing the saturation behavior.

In conclusion, by ensuring equipartition is obeyed in simulations, we find that the TIP4P/2005 model captures the TMD in excellent agreement with experiments, and it also gives an excellent description of the saturation curve for the stable liquid in conditions of most interest in biomolecular simulations. We find that the breakdown of equipartition can spuriously shift the TMD to lower temperatures. Emphasizing the subtleties inherent in describing water, this spurious shift may appear to be small but it is already comparable in magnitude to the shift in TMD due to nuclear quantum effects. This observation is important in tuning force fields for classical statistical mechanical description of systems that show non-trivial nuclear quantum effects even at ambient conditions. Including a dispersion correction to the TIP4P/2005 model only serves to degrade the performance of the model for describing the saturation behavior of the liquid, suggesting that the flaws in the protein force-field are being compensated by changes in the water model.
