# The Advantages of AI for Computational Protein Studies and Looking Ahead at the Next Challenges: Single Structures Are Not Enough


## Abstract
  The ability to understand proteins and their behaviors has been drastically improved by major successes in structure prediction and the appearance of Large Protein Language Models. The speed with which Deep Learning and Artificial Intelligence are now affecting computational protein studies is remarkable, but there are now many opportunities for further rapid progress with applications of these methods. Rapid gains are likely to come from studies using the approaches identified in this perspective. Addressing and predicting ligand-binding sites in protein structures, as well as the prediction of reliable structures of proteins interacting with other proteins, will be pivotal for fully details of structural mechanisms and dynamics. The prediction of multi-state protein ensembles, conformational transitions, dynamics of large protein complexes, and integration with experimental data is likely to happen quickly.


## Example of Generating Alternative Conformations
  The small G protein Arf6 has two different crystal structures: pdb:1E0S and pdb:2J5X, the latter in complex with GTPγS. They have the same sequence and are associated with the same UniProt ID: P62330 (Homo sapiens). But, the two experimental structures have somewhat different conformations. Figure 1 shows the superposition of the two structures in part A and comparison of all ESMFold generated structure with the two crystal structures, which suggests cohesive structures for the unbound form, but a continuum of less cohesive structures approaching the bound form. This was carried out without providing any information to the alternative conformation, ESM-IF1/ESMFold successfully predicted the other conformation of 1E0S, which is 2J5X. This is one example of several recent approaches to this problem. This example also suggests ways to discover the changes to structures upon binding.


## Inaccuracies in the training data and the problem of water in protein structures
  The experiment-based structure models in the Protein Data Bank (PDB)14,15 are interpretations of experimental data and are not themselves free of interpretation bias and errors. Numerous programs exist to assess the validity of experimental models16–18 and the PDB itself provides a comprehensive validation report to the experimenter prior to final deposition.19 These validation reports and the data needed to visualize the density maps can enable independent structure evaluation that could be used to exclude models, which in turn could be removed from datasets used to train AI systems such as AlphaFold1,2. High-quality structural models are essential for drug-discovery applications20–23 and for understanding the biological mechanisms of proteins and RNAs. Advanced automated refinement services, such as PDB-REDO,24 can improve many 3D models but cannot infer missing small molecules or waters25 (Figure 2). What would happen if structure prediction methods were retrained, e.g. with OpenFold,26 using filtered versions of the structural database? How much have the inaccuracies in deposited models affected the structural predictions currently available in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database?27

  Individual water molecules play a significant role in protein biological functions, via their stabilization of structures and their impact on protein flexibility and dynamics.28,29 As a result, the absence or incorrect placement of waters around protein structures is a serious problem, as water is not merely a passive solvent, but plays essential roles in stability, dynamics, and mechanisms. Nevertheless, many X-ray structures in the PDB do not include a single water molecule. Wlodawer et al.25 recently highlighted that there are around 800 such structures determined to 2.5 Å resolution or better without any water molecule, whereas some modelled water oxygens have unusually low or high occupancies.

  With advancements in Deep Learning (DL), several methods have been developed to predict and optimize the placement of water molecules around proteins. GALAXYWater-CNN30 uses a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to generate a water score map for a given protein structure, and showed higher performance when compared with previous energy-based methods. HydraProt31 employs a 3D U-net and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) framework, and was shown to surpass than existing state-of-the-art approaches in terms of precision and recall. SuperWater32 has recently been developed to integrate a score-based diffusion model with equivariant Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and was found to predict water molecule placements around proteins with an accuracy of 0.3 ± 0.06 Å relative to experimentally validated positions. More recently, Sun et al.33 presented a complementary approach based on Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations to improve the placement of water in maps derived from cryogenic Electron Microscopy (cryo-EM). Despite these progresses, some important limitations remain, such as the need for a more physically grounded modeling of water within DL methods, as well as the need to extend these methods to larger protein complexes, where the placement of water molecules can be important for understanding the stabilization of protein-protein interfaces. This problem will be solved with more high resolution crystal structures solved with the higher energy beam lines coming online now.


## Predicting ligand-binding sites, protein-protein interactions, and protein complexes
  Predicting protein–ligand binding sites is a fundamental problem for drug discovery. To tackle this problem, there are two main families of prediction methods: sequence-based and structure-based binding site prediction. Recently, a hybrid approach has been proposed that combines pLMs based on sequences with GNNs based on the protein 3D structures.34 Another method recently proposed for predicting binding protein sites for a variety of ligands, such as DNA, RNA, peptides, proteins, ATP, HEM, and metal ions, combines pLMs and geometric DL.35 A similar protein-binding site prediction method, MPBind, integrates pLMs with equivariant GNN that extract geometric features of 3D protein structures.36 The prediction of small-molecule binding sites using AlphaFold2 has been recently proposed by Gazizov et al.,37 and applied to the human proteome to produce a database that includes thousands of unseen binding sites in disease-relevant proteins. Diffusion and geometric DL models, including hybrid approaches that combine sequence and structure-based features, greatly improve the identification of ligand binding and enhance AI-driven drug discovery.38 A systematic review of the latest application of LLMs for predicting protein and nucleic acid binding sites is given in Wang and Li.39

  Ullanat et al.40 have developed a language model based on the protein-protein interaction data in the String database,41 indicating that two proteins can interact, but without any structural details. While LLMs and Machine Learning (ML) provide the opportunity to learn about protein interaction preferences even in the absence of details, to go further structures of the interacting proteins are required. In recent CASP experiments, the predictions of protein-protein co-structures showed a relatively low success rate. While the standard application of AlphaFold-multimer for modeling protein-protein co-structures has had limited success, recent CASP assessments demonstrate improved performance with stochastic sampling methods and customized MSAs.42.43 Nonetheless, accurate structural modeling of complexes remains an important challenge, particularly for proteins with limited MSA data or structural templates, including host-pathogen, antibody/nanobody-antigen, and protein-nucleic acid complexes.43


## Integrating predicted structures with experimental data
  Several methods have been described recently to integrate AlphaFold modeling with experimental data,44–47 a strategy that can also allow to uncover alternative conformational states of proteins. Montelione and coworkers have recently presented AF-NMR, a computational–experimental framework scoring models generated by stochastic sampling with AlphaFold against experimental NOESY data.48 Applied to Gaussia luciferase, AF-NMR revealed two interconverting states in dynamic equilibrium, involving rearrangements of lids, binding pockets, and cryptic surface cavities. AF-NMR also identified two distinct conformational states of the human tumor suppressor Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 2-Associated Protein. These multistate models can account for more NOESY peaks than single-state models determined using conventional restraint-based modeling, supporting the presence of previously unrecognized conformational states in fast exchange. Data-guided multistate modeling seeded with stochastically sampled AF models has also been demonstrated using cryo-EM.49,50 These methods have the potential to detect conformational heterogeneity and cryptic pockets not resolved by conventional single-state experimental data.


## How to Use LPLM Embeddings
  The embeddings provide numerical representations of any given sequence. The differences between embedding values can be used to identify the best homologs by finding the most similar pair of embeddings.

  If the raw embeddings from LPLM are used directly for any purpose, they often yield many false positives. The best way to use them is to train them by fine tuning or by selecting the embeddings to perform best. In every case, a way to validate the findings is critically important.

  The process is very much like machine learning in general. The first step is to identify the available true data for the properties you want to predict. This needs to be divided into a training set and a validation set. This can be repeated by different random selections for the two sets, which are then carried out separately in what is termed n-fold cross-validation, where n is the number of different ways the data set has been sampled into the training and validation sets. Fine tuning means that each part of the embedding will be given a weight, which for a model with billions of parameters is an extremely large computation. A simpler way is to apply the training to different parts of the embeddings and choose the part of the embeddings that then performs best in the validation step. This is the approach that was used to find new protein homologs in the following. It succeeded in finding many new homologs, with as low as 10% sequence identity.


## Discovering new protein homologs
  Identifying new protein homologs is extremely important as this can also be used to make function assignments by transferring the function of the known protein to the newly discovered homolog. New homologs can also be found by using AI by training, either by fine-tuning or selecting data from the LLMs.51,52 Protein structures predicted by AlphaFold, in whole or in part, have been used to validate the predicted relationships between protein homologs.52 It is also worth noting that when two closely related homologs exhibit small conformational differences, e.g. a small movement of an external loop, that can be used to inferred protein motions and functionally relevant dynamics.


## The challenge of modeling disordered proteins
  Despite the AI revolution in structure prediction brought by AlphaFold, a deeper understanding of proteins that are fully or partially disordered remains unsatisfactory. This is an important subject for investigation since the disordered parts of proteins can be sites of intermolecular interactions. Notably, many predicted protein structures contain exterior disordered regions (see Figure 3). Disordered segments of proteins are often important for binding and can function in entropy-modulated disorder-to-order transitions. Predicting conformational ensemble structures and their roles in assembling multiprotein complexes will ultimately lead to a deeper, more integrated view of how cells function. AlphaFold is not suitable for intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) that exist as dynamic ensembles of conformations. Usually, the disordered predicted parts of proteins have predictions with low AlphaFold confidence scores (pLDDT). These pLDDT scores have been shown to reliably distinguish ordered from disordered regions of proteins.1,52–56 However, simply defining a segment as disordered is not likely to be sufficient. A primary goal for modeling IDPs is to generate a representative ensemble of structures, instead of a single structure. Novel methods are now being developed that use inter-residue distances from experimental or predicted structures as constraints in MD simulations to construct structural ensembles of disordered proteins. DL methods such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs),57 Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).58 or diffusion models59 have been developed to generate representative ensembles of disordered proteins. However, initial efforts at CASP to assess ensemble modeling methods have demonstrated that this remains an unsolved problem.60


## Modeling protein ensembles and dynamics
  AlphaFold and DL approaches have revolutionized protein structure prediction, yielding reliable 3D structures for many proteins. However, useful representations of proteins usually require “dynamic” ensembles with multiple states required to capture the conformational changes required for function.62 Evolving AI-based sampling methods show promise for modeling distinct conformational states that proteins adopt. These approaches use a conventional AlphaFold2/3 platform with curated input such as (i) state-annotated conformational templates,62 (ii) shallow MSAs chosen either randomly (AlphaFold-alt)63,64 or by clustering homologous protein sequences (AF-cluster),65 (iii) very shallow MSAs and even single protein sequences66–68 that allow knowledge inherent to the AI to dominate the modeling process, or (iv) using MSAs masked at multiple positions, as implemented in both SPEECH_AF69 and AF-sample2,70 to bias the prediction toward alternative conformational states. AlphaFold calculations using network dropouts (AF-sample)48,71,72 can also generate conformational diversity, which includes experimentally observed alternative conformational states. More recently, it has been demonstrated that more accurate descriptions of conformational landscapes result from using representatives of each cluster as starting points for short MD simulations and recycling the resulting as templates for iterative modeling or retraining of the transformer network, e.g. in AF-RAVE73 and BioEmu74 or using diffusion-based flow matching methods.75 These studies, though generally limited to smaller monomeric proteins, demonstrate the potential to model multiple protein conformational states, and even conformational landscapes and transition trajectories. Despite these advances, challenges remain in reliably modeling alternative conformational states that are observed experimentally. Bias from specific conformational states present in the training data can prevent sampling of alternative conformational states.67,76,77 As an example, Scaramozzino et al.78 recently tested BioEmu on ribose-binding protein (RBP) and the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ ATPase1 (SERCA). Both proteins have extensive characterization from the PDB (apo, holo, and intermediate states) allowing to build rich structural ensembles.79 In both cases, BioEmu was able to capture some of the known conformations from X-ray, yet the resulting ensembles displayed a strong bias toward the closed-holo states, which are over-represented in the PDB. Moreover, the model also produced partially unfolded conformations which are unsupported by available data from X-ray and MD.78These observations suggest that cases of successful modeling might result from AI memorization, rather than its inherent “learning” of protein physical structure principles. This memorization can bias AI sampling toward the conformational state(s) used during training and prevent the modeling of realistic, alternative conformational states.

  Historically, one of the simplest ways to look at protein dynamics has been to use the Debye-Waller B factor from X-ray experiments. By looking at average values B-factors for each of the 20 amino acid types across large databases of high-quality structures, one can learn relationships between protein sequences and dynamics,80 as well as amino acid properties in sequence conservation.81 The resulting strings of average B-factors can be analyzed and compared using probabilistic, information-theoretic, and signal-processing methods. This approach enables the simultaneous analysis of large sequence databases, resulting in computational requirements that are orders of magnitude lower than those of standard methods, to infer experimentally observed protein dynamics.


## Modeling transition pathways and conformational changes
  A few attempts to use DL to simulate conformational changes and transition pathways between protein end states have also been recently carried out.82–84 While these methods can produce diverse sets of 3D conformations, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. Can we trust DL conformations when these differ substantially from available experimental structures? How to rank the predicted conformations and put them into sequences? At present, clear answers to these questions are lacking. Moreover, current DL attempts tend to bridge the gap in conformational space by performing extensive MD sampling from end-state conformers and reconstructing the sequence of transition steps ex post facto.82–84

  Many large cryo-EM assemblages likewise capture multiple conformations.85,86 DL approaches can in principle, be helpful to overcome the sampling problem of MD, but most available DL methods are still limited to monomeric proteins,70,74 or small complexes,82,83 and when larger multimeric complexes can be handled, interpreting their conformational diversity is not straightforward (see Figure 4).87

  On the other hand, coarse-grained (CG) models can offer a physics-based and more interpretable alternative. Elastic Network Models (ENMs)88–90 have been used for a long time to explore the conformational dynamics of proteins at a fraction of the cost of atomistic MD. Recent ENM path-sampling methods based on CG simulations, such as MinActionPath292 and eBDIMS2,78 achieve scalability to larger protein sizes, enabling the generation of transition pathways for large proteins and protein complexes (Figure 3) up to the megadalton scale. These CG methods are extremely valuable for providing intermediate conformations, which can subsequently be reconstructed with atomistic detail, via e.g., the DL method cg2all,92 and used to enhance conformational sampling93 or to understand the impact of pathogenic mutations.94 These physics-based methods provide time-ordered trajectories and tend to offer a more realistic description of conformational changes than current DL approaches. However, their sampling capabilities are restricted to the conformational space delimited by the end states or limited by the specific model implementation.

  One example of this need is predicting the structures corresponding to the steps in the cycles of biomolecular machines, as well as the order of compositional assembly of large complexes, since these machines are the main source of mechanical work in cells. Despite its successes, AlphaFold3 is still unable to predict functional structures (see Figure 5), even for oligomeric proteins that are part of the same clade as proteins whose structures were determined experimentally, e.g., by cryo-EM. There are likely two main reasons for this shortcoming. First, the lack of incorporation of non-homogeneous ligand effects in AlphaFold structure prediction, as states along the cycle correspond to different ligand-bound conformations (such as ATP, ADP-Pi, ADP, substrate). A second reason is the lack in DL algorithms of the essential allosteric effects in proteins, which are usually large structural changes in proteins upon ligand binding or protein-protein interactions. An example of a missing prediction is shown in Figure 4 for a member of the AAA+ (ATPases associated with various cellular activities) superfamily, the human katanin hexamer in a post-hydrolysis (active) state, which is an assembly of non-homogeneous monomers. In this case, the AlphaFold3 prediction for human katanin, either all-apo or with 6 ADPs bound, does not correspond to either the spiral state (because it is planar), nor to the ring state (because all the monomers have identical configurations) of the different conformations katanin experimentally observed in C. elegans.


## Integration of Deep Learning (DL) and data-assisted Coarse-Grained (CG) methods
  We suggest that a synergistic integration of data-driven DL approaches for ensemble generation with physics-based and data-assisted CG simulations could overcome many of the current limitations of both sampling strategies. Achieving this goal will first require the development of more accurate and computationally efficient CG models capable of reliably expanding the exploration of conformational spaces beyond what atomistic simulations can currently reach. Once such models are available, DL strategies could be trained on millions of CG-generated conformations to learn the physical principles underlying large-scale structural transitions and predict alternative, functionally relevant states. In this way, CG simulations would provide the physical realism and temporal context missing from current DL methods, while DL models would offer a data-driven route to accelerate the discovery of new conformations across diverse and larger protein systems.


## Predicted RNA Structures
  AI, especially DL and LLMs, has significantly advanced RNA secondary (2D) and tertiary (3D) structure prediction. Despite significant progress, predicting RNA structure with AI remains more challenging than predicting protein structure due to factors such as the relatively small number of experimentally determined RNA 3D structures compared to proteins and the fact that RNA molecules are highly dynamic and can adopt multiple functional conformations, making a single, static prediction difficult.

  Recently, several new methods for predicting the 3D structures of RNAs and their interactions with proteins and nucleic acids, based on deep learning, have been proposed, including AlphaFold3,95 RhoFold+,96 Nufold,97 RosettaRNA,98 and RoseTTAFoldNA.99 Since the number of crystallographically solved RNA structures is still relatively small, DL-based methods have limitations. This is clearly evident from the most recent comparison of RNA structure prediction accuracy in CASP16 experiments. In CASP16, the AlphaFold3 server’s predictions of 3D RNA structures were outperformed by physics-based models.100 Though capable of predicting joint structures and interactions with RNA using a diffusion-based model for atom coordinates, AlphaFold3 sometimes violates chirality, predicts only static structures, fails to accurately capture multi-state dynamic behavior, and can introduce false predictions in disordered regions, often with low confidence. RoseTTAFoldNA, an extension specifically designed for nucleic acid complexes, offers higher accuracy in confident predictions for various protein–nucleic acid complexes but may exhibit lower overall accuracy than AlphaFold2 for proteins and has memory limitations that initially restrict sequence input.101

  LLMs are redefining genomic analysis by learning directly from DNA and RNA sequences to uncover regulatory elements, predict variant effects, and model molecular interactions. A genomic language model (gML) is a neural network (often transformer-based) trained on large-scale biological sequence data to learn the statistical patterns, syntax, and semantics of genomes, enabling many difficult tasks such as variant effect prediction, gene annotation, regulatory element discovery, and protein function inference.102 The patterns found in DNA and RNA sequences resemble syntax, with k-mers serving as “words” and regulatory motifs as “grammar”. These sequences are structurally similar to natural languages, composed of a four-letter alphabet representing nucleotides, which facilitates the adaptation of natural language processing techniques to bioinformatics. Pushing these methods further will be beneficial to reach another structure prediction revolution, also for RNA and nucleic acids.
