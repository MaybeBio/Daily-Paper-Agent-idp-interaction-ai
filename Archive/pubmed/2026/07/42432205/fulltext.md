# The allostery principle supports the symbolic character of signaling circuits: gene regulation provides a form of intelligence to living systems


## Abstract
  Biology faces the challenge of articulating general principles capable of organizing and conceptually integrating its immense diversity. Since the operon and allosteric models of the 1960s, the regulatory landscape has expanded dramatically. Building on classical allosteric regulation, I propose the allostery principle, which stipulates that the combinatorial diversity in gene regulation arises from the use of two independent sites that can link any two molecular species within a signaling process, independent of specific mechanistic implementations. I provide empirical evidence that the allostery principle has been implemented repeatedly in six distinct molecular platforms during evolution, generating symbolic genetic explosions that expanded the combinatorial diversity of gene regulation. Four involve bacterial genetic platforms, including protein regulators, promoters, and small RNAs. Two involve eukaryotic platforms: intrinsically disordered proteins coupled with alternative splicing, and the extensive complex multicellular eukaryotic regulation mediated by non-coding RNAs. Remarkably, the central Merge operation within the minimalist generative model of the human language faculty—another symbolic explosion in evolution—conceptually satisfies the allostery principle. I further propose that regulatory signaling fluxes constitute a theory physically instantiated within organisms’ architecture, functioning as a compressed representation with a strong explanatory power of its capabilities. The concept of missing regulatability provides the required falsifiability for this theoretical construct. Gene regulation implements physiological capabilities whose global significance derives from alignment with universally encoded biological ends—survival, reproduction, development, and differentiation. A system that responds to the environment making the adequate changes to achieve its final ends is showing the ability to instantiate intelligent actions. Therefore, gene regulation provides a form of intelligence to biological systems. This definition matches with intelligence in physical symbol systems formalized early in artificial intelligence. Adequate interpretations of these results and their implications are discussed. “It is the union of passionate interest in the detailed facts with equal devotion to abstract generalization which forms the novelty in our present society” (Whitehead 1925).


## Introduction
  The conceptual foundations of the regulation of gene expression were laid by Jacques Monod, François Jacob and colleagues in the early 1960s. Their contributions included the operon model, defined as a group of co-transcribed genes controlled by a promoter and an operator site (Jacob et al. 1960; Jacob and Monod 1961), and the notion of allosteric transitions in proteins (Monod et al. 1963), illustrated in Fig. 1. These classic concepts, defined in bacteria decades ago are—with multiple adaptations—the basis of our understanding of gene regulation across biological systems. Older terminology is, however, frequently used, sometimes imprecisely, to describe recent advances in the field. Updated general principles of gene regulation are therefore welcome.   Systematically updating and formally redefining classic terminology to support revised ontologies and improve conceptual precision is a strategy that may distill general principles in biology. An example is provided by (Mejia-Almonte et al. 2020), where classical notions of bacterial regulation were systematically reconsidered. Allosteric regulation, discovered and formalized by Monod, Jacob and collaborators (Monod et al. 1963, 1965), serves here as the foundational concept to be abstracted into a general principle of gene regulation. The guiding hypothesis is that a formalism capable of describing a relatively simple organism such as Escherichia coli may prove useful for modeling complex organisms, paraphrasing Monod’s well-known assertion.

  This work develops a novel framework for gene regulation grounded in three methodological commitments: (i) a systematic pursuit of increased abstraction, (ii) consistency within broader conceptual contexts, and (iii) precise use of terminology. The successive models of allosterism illustrate a trajectory of progressively relaxed mechanistic constraints; continuing this tendency, I propose the allostery principle, which is evaluated against current knowledge of regulation. This analysis also reveals that certain definitions, properties, and conceptual associations in biology, natural language, and AI express the same logic. As illustrated by (Phillips 2024) such conceptual “sameness” is scientifically valuable. This convergence is not surprising since intelligence and language are biological capacities. AI derives from human intelligence; and natural language depends on genetic capacities that emerged through the evolutionary history of our species. Some of these abstractions may clarify established concepts, whereas others may simply render explicit what has long remained implicit. The unifying concept developed in this work is the allostery principle, defined in Section II.3.

### Allostery enables biology to construct regulatory networks beyond chemical restrictions
  Allosteric regulation occurs when a molecule or ligand (allolactose in Fig. 1) exerts its effect by binding to a distinct allosteric site separate from the active site of the same molecule (allosteric site comes the Greek allos “other” and stereos “site”). The active site, also called orthosteric or output site is, in the case of TFs, its DNA-binding site, whereas in enzymes the active site performs the catalytic function. Allosteric regulatory proteins act as molecular connectors between environmental signals — external or internal — and changes in gene expression. Allosteric binding of a ligand induces conformational changes in the TF, modifying its DNA-binding affinity and thereby enhancing or diminishing its binding. DNA-binding allosteric TFs play the role of “two headed molecules” in the words of Jacob (Jacob 1970). This mechanism, illustrated in Fig. 2B, serves as the connector in a signaling flux that enables cells to dynamically modify gene expression in response to environmental cues —an essential capability for a single genotype to generate multiple phenotypes.Fig. 2Allosterism and genetic regulation in the tryptophan pathway. B Diagram III emphasizes the lack of direct interaction between regulator and substrate in allosteric regulation. The indirect interaction between effector and substrate occurs thanks to the structure of the allosteric proteins (dark-blue circle) with two stereospecifically distinct sites.   As illustrated in Fig. 2A, competition by steric hindrance and direct interaction require the regulator and the substrate to have a certain chemical similarity in structure and reactivity, whereas indirect allosteric interactions are free of such limitations. As stated by Monod and collaborators (Monod et al. 1963), although mass action remains a component of biochemical changes, regulatory networks “would have been impossible if only direct interactions … had been used …(since they) would have been severely limited by chemical structure, the reactivity, or lack of reactivity, of metabolites.”

  The key feature of allostery is that the allosteric and the active site are distinct, allowing for complete chemical independence between the effector ligand bound at the allosteric site and the substrate recognized by the active site, see Fig. 2B. It was quickly recognized that allosteric mechanisms enable end-product inhibition, in which the final metabolite of a pathway (L-tryptophan in Fig. 2C) inhibits its own synthesis, saving the energy required for its production when present in the medium.

  Allostery, in combination with a minimal genetic platform — operons and their regulatory genes — provides the basis for the emergence of genetic regulatory sensing and response circuits in biology. For instance, in E.coli, when tryptophan is sensed, the collection of genes involved in its synthesis—encoded in the trpLEDCBA polycistronic transcription unit whose products are the enzymes shown in Fig. 2C—is repressed, generating a response that consists of consuming external tryptophan instead of synthesizing it. The synthesis of tryptophan can be conceived as a minimal physiological process or capability.

  As emphasized by Monod and colleagues, the biological significance of allostery lies in that it provides biology with the ability to implement interactions free from chemical restriction. This is achieved thanks to the two independent sites of the allosteric protein, a mechanistic virtue that enables evolution to generate practically any conceivable physiological process.


### A brief history of the expansion of the allostery concept
  Allosteric transitions were initially characterized by the combination of features such as Hill-type binding dynamics and a corresponding change in regulator binding behavior (Monod et al. 1963). They were first formalized in the Monod-Wyman-Changeux (MWC) concerted model, which assumes that protein symmetry is preserved while it transits between two conformations: the relaxed (R) and the tight (T) states (Monod et al. 1965). A year later the Koshland-Némethy-Filmer (KNL) sequential model, in which transitions occur in individual subunits one at a time, was proposed (Koshland et al. 1966).

  Since then, numerous studies have revealed a diversity of mechanisms by which information is transmitted between the allosteric and the active site, departing from the requirements of the MWC model. Allostery has been observed in monomeric proteins (e.g. the chemotactic CheY protein of E.coli), with no symmetry requirement (Cui and Karplus 2008), and can arise even in the absence of large conformational changes (Popovych et al. 2006; Goodey and Benkovic 2008; Jiménez-Osés et al. 2014). Furthermore, information transfer from the allosteric to the active site may be distributed across multiple residues, as shown by “disrupt and restore” experiments in the TetR TF, focusing on the thermodynamic balance as sufficient to preserve allostery (Leander et al. 2020). TetR binds to its operator sites in the apo (ligand-free) conformation but, contrary to the MWC model, its effector-bound crystal structure is more similar to the DNA-bound form (Liu et al. 2024). These findings are consistent with the ensemble allosteric model (EAM) which emphasizes the dynamic population of conformational states (Hilser et al. 2012; Motlagh et al. 2014).

  The thermodynamic perspective abstracts from mechanistic details (see Fig. 3), framing allostery in terms of the protein’s energetic landscape—a simplified model that accommodates the increased diversity of specific known mechanisms. The thermodynamic model is the updated abstraction with room for all current known diversity of mechanisms. ### The allostery principle as the source of the combinatorial power of gene regulation
  The power of allostery lies in its ability to generate, in principle, a potentially unbounded combinatorial repertoire, grounded in the distinct nature of the two sites involved in a single unit of signaling flux. As Monod noted, allostery’s “ability to explain is so great that it excludes nothing… there is no physiological phenomenon so complex and mysterious that it cannot be accounted for” (Monod 1966). All mechanistic variants illustrated in Fig. 3 retain this core property of allostery. Over time, empirical work has progressively pruned the original diagnostic criteria, eliminating the requirement of multimeric regulatory proteins and the associated symmetry constraints, while preserving the essential feature that gives allostery its etymological meaning: two sterically distinct sites.

  Just as diverse mechanisms can support allostery, the molecular regulators involved in gene regulation are highly diverse. For instance, it has been shown in E. coli that the metabolite ppGpp can regulate transcription initiation (Vercruysse et al. 2011; Sanchez-Vazquez et al. 2019), and numerous RNA architectures—small and large, coding and non-coding, entire molecules or segments of mRNAs (e.g., riboswitches)—can implement the allostery principle, as shown in Section II. The task that follows is to assess whether the allostery principle can be elevated from a hypothesis to a general abstraction supported by empirical evidence across diverse regulatory architectures. The allostery principle is formulated as follows:

  The combinatorial power of gene regulation rests on the modularity of two independent sites, capable of connecting any two molecules within a single genetic sensing regulatory signaling circuit. This modularity supports, in principle, a potentially unbounded number of novel regulatory arrangements.

  The hypothesis is that this principle has been implemented multiple times during the evolutionary expansion of gene regulation across distinct molecular infrastructures involving both proteins and RNAs, as analyzed in Section III.

  Two major topologies implement the allostery principle. In classical cases, the allosteric ligand and conformational signal transmission via the active site are organized within a linear signaling flux, as illustrated in Figs. 2B, 3C and 4A. Multi-input systems define an alternative topology in which two or more elements bind distinct allosteric sites, and their effects are integrated into a single output (Fig. 4B). A linear signaling process generates combinatorial diversity through the chemical independence between the input ligand and the regulated target. A multi-input architecture adds an additional combinatorial dimension through the independent interactions of multiple ligands, while retaining the chemical independence between inputs and the final regulated target.Fig. 4Topologies of allostery within a signaling pathway. ## Genetic explosions in the growth of regulatory networks in evolution
  Genomic evidence indicates that, in the course of evolution, the gene regulatory networks (GRNs) of species have grown as the number of genes and the complexity of living organisms have increased (Charoensawan et al. 2010); see also (Liu et al. 2013). The hypothesis of this work is that the growth of the network size and signaling power in evolution has gone through major expansions associated with implementations of the allostery principle by different molecular infrastructures, each generating a novel source of combinatorial power. I call these genetic regulatory explosions. In section IV, I show that gene regulation supports a form of biological non-neural intelligence, thus the term of symbolic explosions synonymous for regulatory explosions.

  It may be convenient to distinguish between effective symbolic explosions and potential explosions. The first being those with evidence that evolution took advantage and contributed to the increased power of gene regulation. By contrast, dormant or potential explosions are those for which there is evidence of the mechanism, but whose implementation has been marginal as far as current knowledge indicates.


### Two in-degree connectivity at promoters
  An early step of microbial gene regulation is a minimal architecture in which promoters are regulated by a single TF. In a plausible scenario TFs can control multiple downstream genes (multiple out-degree), either through duplication of their binding sites or via operons, but have a one in-degree connectivity, since promoters are regulated by only one TF. This scenario enables hierarchical control, as TFs may regulate other TFs. However, if a TF recognizes only one allosteric ligand, a gene could respond to only one variable. This is an important limitation for early microorganisms given the considerable number of variables of any environment (pH, temperature, carbon, nitrogen sources, and more,see (Tierrafria et al. 2019). There was a clear evolutionary advantage to enable genes to respond to multiple signals. One easy solution is to regulate promoters by more than one TF (two in-degree connectivity). Since in principle, there is large mechanistic freedom for two TFs to bind two distinct sites near a promoter, this relatively simple architecture supports another combinatorial source implementing the allostery principle in a multi-input topology. When genes and operons became subject to regulation by the simultaneous presence of two different signals, more elaborate environmental changes could be genetically encoded.

  An important additional capability with two in-degree connectivity is the emergence of global regulation. In a classic case global regulation implements a hierarchy of alternative carbon sources by co-regulating with local regulators of specific carbon alternatives (Gottesman 1984). Incidentally, coordinated expression of gene clusters fulfilling common biological roles is also documented in eukaryotes (Niehrs and Pollet 1999; Mezey et al. 2008; Kikuchi and Tanaka 2022). Furthermore, eukaryotic genomes tend to have larger space to accommodate binding sites for multiple TFs, offering the possibility for a larger number of signaling inputs integrated at each regulated decision of gene expression. Bacterial regulatory networks exhibit an in-degree distribution in which many nodes are regulated by two or more TFs  , not to mention the rich architecture of eukaryotic promoters. This multi-input signaling mediated by TFs enables diverse binary gates (Buchler et al. 2003) integrated at the promoter level. Thus, the emergence of multi-input promoter architecture was an effective regulatory explosion implementing the allostery principle.


### Bacterial signal transduction systems: two-component transduction and other mechanisms.
  TFs respond to changes in the environment once the signal is introduced in the cell; bacteria and other organisms have signal transduction systems (STS) with a component located in the membrane that detects stimuli in the external environment and transmits it internally to change gene expression.

  Two-component systems involve a histidine kinase (HK) receptor protein complex that senses the signal, and a response regulator (RR) (Robinson et al. 2000). The HK senses specific ligand/stimulus usually within the N-terminal domain. The signaling occurs by a phosphotransfer of the HK that autophosphorylates, and subsequently the phosphoryl group from the HK is transferred to the cognate regulatory RR. HKs and RRs are usually multimeric protein modular complexes with conserved core domains and variable domains that provide the specificity in the HK for sensing different stimuli, and in the RR for the output response. There is a range of different two-component systems and some may enable a multi-input and multi-output architecture. Most RRs function as TFs, where the RR phosphorylation changes its conformation altering its DNA binding affinity to its specific binding sites. This affinity change can be achieved by different mechanisms, for instance promoting dimerization required for DNA binding, enhancing binding, or enabling productive interactions with the transcriptional machinery (Robinson et al. 2000).

  Both the HK and the RR complexes have a modular architecture with different domains, including internal signaling transmitter domains see Fig. 1 in (Alvarez and Georgellis 2022). Their modular character is a key ingredient to search for bioengineering synthetic biosensors taking advantage of the external sensing and transmembrane signaling transduction of two-component systems (Cao et al. 2024a, b).

  The identification of combinatorial rules that apply to independent molecules or motifs and their use in bioengineering functional implementations provides valuable evidence supporting the combinatorial potential, and their functionality supports the existence of a signaling flux, thus supporting the allostery principle. This type of evidence will be repeatedly searched in different molecular platforms.

  Interestingly, allostery transduction occurs at multiple steps in the STS systems. First, there is allosteric binding of the ligand with the HR, this is internally transmitted to the “orthosteric” site of HR autophosphorylated site, which in turn interacts with the receiver site of the RR by a proposed phosphorylation-dependent allostery, and finally the internal signaling allosteric transmission within RR to its output effector domain (Imelio et al. 2021); see also the DesK-DesR allostery coupling (Almada et al. 2025). Allostery provides reversibility to this whole signaling process (Lima et al. 2023). Evidence suggests that this signaling platform emerged in ancient prokaryotes (Alvarez and Georgellis 2023), contributing for bacteria to live in diverse ecosystems (Alvarez and Georgellis 2022). Briefly, STSs are another protein-based platform implementation of the allostery principle.


### Bacterial cis and trans-regulatory RNAs
  A riboswitch is a cis segment of an mRNA that when bound by a ligand changes its structure, thereby regulating the expression of the gene(s) encoded in the mRNA. It contains two distinct, non-overlapping molecular elements, a ligand-binding domain—often referred to as the sensor, actuator, or aptamer—spatially and functionally separate from the RNA expression platform that changes in conformation forming a terminator, or blocking a ribosome binding site. Their modular architecture, featuring multiple aptamers or platforms, can produce cooperative regulatory responses (Breaker 2011). The combinatorial logic of sRNAs is supported by synthetic engineering studies in which sensor and actuator components can be independently combined (Liang et al. 2011).

  In several bacteria it has been found that frequently sRNAs contain a conserved secondary structure to interact with the Hfq chaperone protein which participates in the binding of the sRNA with its target. Hfq is an hexameric ring with multiple surfaces used in the binding of different sRNAs (i.e. DsrA) in distinct ways (Storz 2025), and (Gottesman 2025). Indeed, different synthetic-biology strategies have been implemented using two or three modular domains in the construction of synthetic sRNAs: its 20–30 nt base pairing sequence (with the target mRNA ribosome binding site (RBS) sequence region), an Hfq binding domain and a rho-independent terminator U-tail sequence (Xie et al. 2021). Synthetic sRNA strategies have been shown to work in diverse bacteria (Xie et al. 2021),

  Bacterial trans-acting sRNAs usually target the ribosome-binding site and block the entry of 30S ribosome preventing translation by direct base-pairing to the mRNA target. In the case of regulatory mechanisms that involve only the synthesis of the sRNAs followed by its direct base-pairing binding to its mRNA target, there is apparently no two distinct components directly participating in the sensing-to-response circuit. A similar situation is that of TFs that have no apparent conformational change -e.g. global regulators responding to changes in growth phase, but see (Guo et al. 2024). In those cases, the signal component is likely found affecting upstream regulators governing the synthesis of these regulators that are active once synthetized, with no apparent conformational change. Otherwise, these would be regulatory components insensitive to any signal, an incomplete signaling pathway.

  Two recent reviews by experts in the field, Gisela Storz (Storz 2025) and Susan Gottesman (Gottesman 2025) illustrate the diverse participation of sRNAs in regulatory circuits, and the increasing evidence of their relevance in bacterial gene regulation. sRNAs have been found to regulate TFs located high in regulatory hierarchies such as the FlhDC TF of motility genes; RpoS the sigma factor regulating general stress responses and stationary phase is subject to regulation by several sRNAs (OxyS, RprA, DsrA, ArcZ), each conveying different inputs. This illustrates how sRNAs contribute to increase the number of environmental signals in bacterial decisions (Gottesman 2025; Storz 2025). It is estimated that in Corynebacterium glutamicum 10.7% of regulatory interactions are mediated by sRNAs (Escorcia-Rodriguez et al. 2021); whereas in E.coli, based on current knowledge it ranges between 14 and 25% (Salgado et al. 2024; Karp et al. 2025). Given both their prevalence and regulatory relevance, riboswitches and trans sRNAs are to be considered two additional mechanistic variants of the implementation of the allostery principle in bacterial evolution.


### Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and alternative splicing
  IDPs are proteins that lack a stable three-dimensional structure and instead exist as dynamic ensembles of interconverting conformations; intrinsically disordered domains have also been identified locally within otherwise structured proteins. This structural flexibility suggested that IDPs are particularly well-suited for allostery and cellular signaling (Uversky et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2006).

  Several bacterial TFs have been experimentally shown to contain IDP motifs (Bergkessel et al. 2019; Corbella et al. 2025), for instance, the LacI repressor´s DNA-binding domain involves a transition from an intrinsically disordered N-terminal region to a structured helix-turn-helix (HTH) motif (Uversky et al. 2005). Evidence suggests that IDPs together with alternative splicing, contributed to an expansion of the regulatory power in evolution by generating multiple isoforms from a single gene increasing the functional repertoire of regulatory proteins and “bolster organism complexity” (Romero et al. 2006). This synergistic combination may have driven “explosive speciation events” and a steep increase in regulatory complexity during the emergence of multicellular life (Romero et al. 2006). This supports an effective symbolic explosion that implemented yet another source of combinatorial diversity in evolution.


### Eukaryotic non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)
  A major discovery that emerged from full genome sequencing of complex multicellular organisms was the surprising similarity in the number of protein-coding genes. It was unexpected that C. elegans, Drosophila, and humans each possess approximately 20,000 genes. Subsequently, it was discovered that essentially the whole human genome is transcribed (Mattick 2001), eventually leading to a paradigm shift in the understanding of regulatory networks. As shown by John Mattick and others, while the size of protein-based regulatory networks plateau as biological complexity increases, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) parallels the increase of organismal complexity measured by the number of distinct cell types within organisms, see Fig. 3 in (Liu et al. 2013); see also arguments for ncRNAs in the emergence of complex multicellularity in (Lozada-Chávez et al. 2011). Non-coding RNAs have a propensity to fold into secondary and tertiary structures and can rapidly shift among multiple conformations, and “undergo allosteric transitions, thereby acting as a responsive switch” See Fig. 3 in (Mercer and Mattick 2013).

  Non-coding RNAs in eukaryotes occupy a broad functional landscape, encompassing diverse mechanisms and interaction modalities (Chao et al. 2022; Cao et al. 2024a, b). The allostery principle, however, is restricted to signaling pathways within gene regulatory processes. A defining criterion for biologically significant regulation is specificity. Thus, for instance, ncRNAs functioning as components of the basal transcriptional machinery are excluded. By contrast, substantial evidence supports functional specificity in numerous ncRNAs, as reflected in restricted target repertoires, selective modulation of transcription initiation (e.g., RNA polymerase II) (Wang et al. 2011), condition-dependent expression patterns, and confinement to particular cell types or developmental stages (Wang et al. 2011; Chen and Kim 2024).

  Similar to bacterial sRNAs, some non-coding eukaryotic RNAs may act solely through sequence complementarity; however, if they form part of a regulatory signaling pathway, a signal detection component is expected to operate upstream of their expression.

  Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) with sizes that range from few hundred nucleotides to over 100,000 nt can have multiple domains for interactions with proteins, and their dynamic conformations facilitate a large space of regulatory possibilities (Chen and Kim 2024). In spite of the reduced fraction of characterized cases, a landscape of diverse mechanisms is already emerging (Ferrer and Dimitrova 2024), with lncRNAs likely playing different roles in the signaling flow governing gene expression. These include a role as sensors regulating transcriptional programs, like Mitlnc, a mitochondrial lncRNA identified as an allosteric activator in the mouse´s heart (Weiss et al. 2024); lncRNAs have been shown to regulate transcription binding directly to RNA pol lI; to affect histone methylation; to regulate downstream genes by close association to promoters; and to participate in nuclear organelle assembly such as the nuclear paraspeckles regulatory hubs composed of ncRNAs and protein complexes (Wang et al. 2011).

  In fact, RNA binding proteins constitute the largest transcriptional regulatory protein family in the genome (Wang et al. 2011). These proteins tend to contain multiple RNA binding domains enabling them to recognize multiple targets; and inversely, some lncRNAs can function as flexible modular scaffolds of protein complexes, by discrete functional modules bringing into proximity multiple proteins, as shown with TERC an RNA component of telomerase, likely implementing a multi-input allostery topology. They can guide chromatin-regulatory complexes to specific genomic regions (Guttman and Rinn 2012); since RNA can also base-pair with DNA, in some cases, they guide complexes to specific DNA loci. It is thus likely that many ncRNAs have two or more modules that bind distinct molecules in a multi-conformation dependent manner. It has been suggested that many such RNA-binding regulators might exhibit distinct “allosterically dictated” conformations depending on the specific RNA sequences bound, which may regulate interactions with other proteins for distinct functions (Wang et al. 2011). This illustrates how a new allostery platform can also expand the regulatory mechanism by combining with previously existing ones.

  Functional lncRNAs usually possess secondary structure modules or sequence motifs, to specifically interact with different proteins (Chen and Kim 2024). These mechanisms bring together distinct elements into a regulatory process, providing the basis for a plausible “modular RNA code” for creating engineered RNAs, as suggested by (Guttman and Rinn 2012). This hypothesis found support in design principles of lncRNAs based on an architecture with the following modules: a nuclear retention sequence as an address for nuclear location; DNA anchoring motifs; secondary structure motifs; followed by RNA–protein interaction motifs (See Fig. 1 in (Sharma et al. 2025). The high specificity characteristic of biology limits subsets of combinatorial possibilities of lncRNAs depending on the genomic context, the copy number and the specific cell type. Although still in its infancy, these engineering principles of lncRNA-mediated regulation support the natural combinatorial nature of lncRNAs and therefore the allostery principle. In summary, the diversity and ability for RNAs and proteins to have multiple sites and interactions within signaling processes, support in multicellular organisms collections of mechanisms capable of implementing the allostery principle with their effective regulatory symbolic explosions.


### Symbolic genetic explosions in evolution
  In summary, I have distilled the core of allosteric mechanisms into the allostery principle and presented empirical evidence that this principle has been implemented repeatedly during evolution across distinct molecular mechanisms and regulatory platforms, thereby expanding the combinatorial capacity of gene regulation.

  Even within the limited number of genetic platforms examined, a striking observation is the diversity of specific mechanisms operating under the same underlying logic. Diversity appears at multiple levels: in detailed mechanistic implementations; in classes of alternative molecular organizations that instantiate the same core molecular platform; and at the higher level of distinct symbolic explosions. Synthetic engineering strategies within each molecular platform further support their associated combinatorial potential. ## The origin of our language faculty. A genetic symbolic explosion
  The precise molecular mechanisms supporting the language faculty of Homo sapiens are unknown. The origin of human language has been called a “symbolic explosion”— (Henshilwood et al. 2002), an evolutionary event that enabled novel capabilities unconstrained by prior functional limitations. This event was the basis for a vast array of novel capabilities breaking limitations of different sorts that collectively constitute the most powerful source of our social, technological, and, unfortunately, also destructive powers, to the present (Tattersall 2010; Harari 2013; Bolhuis et al. 2014; Miyagawa et al. 2025).

  The symbolic activity in the archeology of human language is fundamental for dating its origin. Since spoken language leaves no fossils, researchers rely on symbolic artifacts and complex behaviors to infer the emergence of the language faculty. Symbolic artifacts lack a strict utilitarian function (Henshilwood et al. 2001, 2002), they are introduced “for symbolic rather than subsistence purposes” (Brumm et al. 2017), such as pierced shell beads, geometrically engraved plates (Tattersall 2010).   The genetic basis of our language faculty has likely originated from regulatory mutations (Gould 1977; Tattersall 2010), (Somel et al. 2011). Human language as a phenotype is among the best, if not the best, theoretically studied complex phenotype (Chomsky 1995; Marcolli et al. 2025). Its study defines a discipline, linguistics, as old as biology itself. Generative grammar is the school of linguistics that more clearly treats the human language faculty as a biological capability grounded in the so-called language organ. Decades of research have culminated in a minimalist generative theory centered on a single operation, Merge, at the core of the computational linguistic component, which combines two syntactic elements, a and b, into the set {a, b} (Chomsky 1995; Bolhuis et al. 2014). This model also finds support from neuroscience (Tanaka et al. 2019).

  The recursive application of Merge generates hierarchical linguistic structures: it can form categories by merging two words (as in a noun phrase), form sentences by merging categories, and yield complex sentences by combining two sentences. This basic operation is required “to arrive at the essentially infinite number of syntactic language structures” (Bolhuis et al. 2014). The striking observation is that the proposed core computational mechanism of human syntax, the Merge operation, is a clear abstract implementation, in a multi-input topology (see Table 2), of the allostery principle. This abstract generative sameness does not imply a shared biophysical implementation.


## The symbolic nature of genetic sensing regulatory signaling circuits
  We have seen the implementation of the allostery principle in a considerable diversity of regulatory mechanisms. In order to support the allostery principle we had focused in searching for evidence of two independent sites connecting any two molecules within a genetic sensing regulatory signaling circuit.

  The regulatory machinery supports a collection of individual steps that conform signaling regulatory circuits which support physiological processes and capabilities, such as the induction and repression of genes involved in physiological functions. Let us call this the local biological significance of gene regulation. This involves a sensing capability, as illustrated in Fig. 1 with the sensing of allolactose, and a second component illustrated in Fig. 2.C as an end-product inhibition, that more generally we can call “the response” that closes the reversible regulation enabled by allosteric transitions. A precise definition of these circuits and their curation in Escherichia coli were defined as Genetic Sensory–Response Units (GENSOR units) (Ledezma-Tejeida et al. 2017).

  At a higher level of biological significance, gene regulation underlies the capacity of organisms to modify gene expression in response to changes in external or internal conditions, thereby supporting survival, reproduction, development, and differentiation. In other words, gene regulation enables biological systems to attain their final ends. A system that responds to the environment making the adequate changes to achieve its final ends is showing the ability to instantiate intelligent actions. Therefore, gene regulation provides a form of intelligence to biological systems.

  Intelligence has multiple definitions, including adequately dealing or adapting to the environment (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence). Although intelligence is a trait usually asssociated to humans, cognitive properties have been proposed in bacterial regulatory networks (Lyon 2015), cell signaling (Mathews et al. 2023), and in multiple levels of biological organization that emerged long before human intelligence (Baluska and Levin 2016).

  Interestingly, this form of intelligence enabled by gene regulation corresponds to what Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon, in foundational work in AI, identified as the defining property of a Physical Symbol System (PSS), described as possessing “the necessary and sufficient means for …intelligent action,” where intelligence is understood as “behavior appropriate to the ends of the system and adaptive to the demands of the environment” (Newell and Simon 1976). As suggested in the introduction, the coincidence of a form of intelligence enabled by gene regulation with that defined early within AI is not surprising, given that intelligence is a biological trait, and AI is a product of human intelligence. It should be emphasized that this abstract coincidence does not imply similarities in their implementation. The knowledge supporting intelligent decisions of gene expression is the result of millions of years of evolutionary history before the emergence of our species, and even before the emergence of neural tissue, and its application occurs within the lifespan of organisms thanks to the sensory-regulatory genetic machinery as encoded in their genome.

  Regulatory interactions in isolation cannot confer local significance, likewise, symbols confer intelligence only as elements within PSSs; isolated symbols are insufficient because intelligent actions requires multiple elements for its realization (Newell and Simon 1976). This is additional logical identity might derive from the definition of intelligence assigned to an action or behavior, something impossible to a single-component isolated system.

  The term “symbolic” is used here to denote three properties: (i) a symbolic system can execute intelligent action, in syntony with the use of the term by Newell and Simon; (ii) intelligent action arises from interactions among multiple components within a system, which in gene regulation correspond to complete signaling circuits; and (iii) a symbolic system possesses combinatorial power capable, in principle, of generating a potentially unbounded number of arrangements. This third interpretation aligns with the use of “symbolic” to describe a uniquely human cognitive capacity grounded in our ability to produce an open-ended range of linguistic expressions (see Section IV and the final Discussion).

  The symbolic nature of allosteric interactions within signaling circuits may be regarded as a natural property in biological regulation, equivalent to parameters like the equilibrium constant for chemical reactions.


## Regulatory networks encode the theory evolution has built within organisms
  Gene regulation and their signaling component are central to understanding the functioning of living systems (Gough et al. 2005). Regulatory elements constitute only a fraction of the genetically encoded molecular components of any organism (Charoensawan et al. 2010), (Liu et al. 2013).

  The regulatory networks may represent biology’s closest approximation to a physical implementation of a reduced set of interactions and signaling circuits that, if we could dissect entirely and properly represent them, they would suffice to describe, if not the whole, most of the potential behavior and anatomy of organisms. The proposal is that organisms have two intertwined physical components, the symbolic and the non-symbolic body. The symbolic component physically instantiates the theory living systems have of themselves in the form of regulatory signaling networks that allow for intelligent changes of gene expression to support their genetic final ends —a theory shaped through evolutionary history and expressed in defined patterns of genetic signal–response transformations.

  Certainly, the assumption, common to a large number of experimental research in genetics, is that regulatory networks contain a strong explanatory power of biological systems.

  Regulation acts as a compressed, reduced representation of the organism capabilities. This poses the challenge of evaluating what may be termed “missing regulatability,” by analogy to “missing heritability” in genetics: the fraction of biological traits whose genetic basis remains uncharacterized (Witte et al. 2014). Assessing missing regulatability involves substantial difficulties, including, for instance, distinguishing genuine regulatory control from gene expression changes driven by mass-action effects, and estimating indirect and higher-order interactions. The concept of missing regulatability provides the required falsifiability for this theoretical construct.

  The pervasive presence of allostery across biological systems has been extensively illustrated (Phillips 2010). This regulatory symbolic component of a genome corresponds to what may be termed the full genomic Genetic Sensory–Regulatory-Response Circuitry: the aggregate of all signaling-and-response regulatory circuitry infrastructure encoded in a genome.The proposed physical component of symbolic regulatory circuitry, together with the language faculty grounded in the language organ, points to a unifying theoretical biology perspective shared across biological systems: capabilities are realized by physical structures.


## Discussion
  The central proposal of this work is the allostery principle: regulatory architectures that rely on two independent sites can, in principle, connect any pair of molecular species within a signaling flux, thereby generating a potentially unbounded combinatorial regulatory capacity. The combinatorial power of this open-ended principle in practice coexists with structural and functional restrictions linked to the high specificity of biological diversity, as for instance, those mentioned in section III.5, in the bioengineering principles of plausible synthetic lncRNAs.

  Evidence presented here suggests that this principle has been repeatedly implemented across distinct macromolecules and regulatory platforms, instantiating symbolic genetic explosions in the evolution of gene regulation. These include two-input regulated promoters, signal transduction systems, microbial RNAs (riboswitches and trans-acting sRNAs), IDPs coupled with alternative splicing, and the expansion of non-coding RNAs in complex multicellular eukaryotes. This constitutes a coarse-grained account of the growth of gene-regulatory complexity, highlighting novel mechanistic sources of combinatorial expansion. The platforms discussed likely represent only an initial and incomplete list. There remains substantial room for determining whether additional regulatory domains instantiate the allostery principle, and whether additional principles operate in parallel, particularly within eukaryotic systems. Importantly, repeated implementation of the allostery principle does not imply shared evolutionary mechanisms in their emergence. The modular character of macromolecules, together with the flexibility of their conformational ensembles in solution, likely contributed to the independent emergence of these architectures (Uversky et al. 2005).

  The notion of symbolic explosions offers an alternative lens through which to examine major evolutionary transitions (Szathmáry and Smith 1995), emphasizing the expansive potential of gene regulation circuitry. How such network expansions map onto functional and phenotypic novelties remains an open question. Classic TF allostery operates across diverse physiological contexts, including hormone-mediated regulation (McEwan 2012) and cognitive processes (Changeux 2013) among many others (Phillips 2010). For conceptual clarity and elegance, the principle is formulated in terms of two independent sites—the allosteric and active sites—which suffice to generate the required combinatorial logic, even though some regulators accommodate more than two ligands in multi-input topologies (Lazareno et al. 2002; de Kruijf et al. 2011).

  The allostery principle highlights the combinatorial power of gene regulation similar to how Merge captures the generative capacity of human language. Both reflect what Wilhelm von Humboldt described as the ability to “make infinite use of finite means” quoted in (Chomsky 1956). Just as human language exhibits a discrete infinity that uniquely distinguishes it from other biological communication systems (Bolhuis et al. 2014), Monod and Jacob noted that “any number of systems may be interconnected into regulatory circuits endowed with virtually any desired property” (Monod and Jacob 1961). While human language is considered the primary vehicle for thought, gene regulation pathways constitute, arguably, the primary biological infrastructure for intelligent genetic actions within the limitations of the genomic encoded alternatives much before the emergence of neurons. The acquisition of such regulatory intelligence is the product of evolutionary history, whereas its execution occurs at physiological timescales.

  As mentioned, the proposal that biological systems exhibit intelligent behavior is not new. Multiple studies have identified cognitive-like properties across levels of biological organization, from bacterial regulatory networks (Lyon 2015), to cells, multicellular organisms, and collective behaviors (See (Baluska and Levin 2016), (Trapp et al. 2025) and citations there). Much of this work focuses on capacities such as memory, learning, computation, and information processing, including within transcriptional networks (Biswas et al. 2022), (Mathews et al. 2023).

  An interesting implication of the present framework is the opportunity to examine the architecture of signaling regulatory networks themselves as a basis for assessing intelligence, independent of anthropocentric or AI-dominated conceptions. A first step could be to quantify the distribution of the number of inputs involved in a repertoire of decisions modifying gene expression.

  The allostery principle can certainly guide AI cohesive perspectives of the biology of gene regulation and signaling. It could inspire a multiple alignment of signaling regulatory circuits as a conceptual interoperable tool across multiple biodata resources. But several conceptual and practical challenges must be addressed. As mentioned regulatory networks (GRNs) represented solely as gene-to-gene graphs (Barabasi and Oltvai 2004) are inherently incomplete, as they omit sensing and response components required for regulation to perform intelligent decisions and achieve biological local and global significance as precisely defined in this work. Constructing complete signaling circuits demands high-quality experimental data, and high-throughput approaches to characterize the full repertoire of allosteric interactions—the allosterome—remain limited in scope (Orsak et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2020).

  Nonetheless, curated databases of signal transduction pathways across diverse organisms (Gough et al. 2005; Femerling et al. 2022; Salgado et al. 2024; Kanehisa et al. 2025; Consortium 2026), including human systems (Chowdhury and Sarkar 2015; Csabai et al. 2022) and references there) provide substantial resources for testing, refining, and potentially extending, as well as identifying the limits, as well as practical implications of this unifying framework.
