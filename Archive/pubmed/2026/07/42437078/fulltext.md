# Beyond the structure-function paradigm: A comprehensive review of intrinsically disordered proteins


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and regions (IDRs) challenge the classical structure–function paradigm by fulfilling essential biological roles in the absence of a stable three-dimensional fold. Rather than occupying fixed conformations, IDPs exist as dynamic ensembles that enable high-specificity, low-affinity interactions, multivalent regulatory functions, and context-dependent binding across diverse cellular environments. This conformational plasticity underlies their central roles in signaling, transcriptional regulation, chromatin organization, and the assembly of membrane-less organelles through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS). The present review offers several conceptual contributions. First, we develop a cross-kingdom synthesis of disorder-based chromatin regulation, demonstrating that bacterial nucleoid-associated proteins, plant transcription factors, and mammalian chromatin regulators share a conserved charge-regulatory logic, mediated by PTM-dependent mechanisms that dynamically couple environmental signals with genome organization. Second, we integrate mechanistically related but frequently siloed disease pathways, including mitophagy dysfunction, oxidative stress signaling, neuroinflammation, and aberrant phase separation, into a unified framework linking IDP conformational dysregulation to neurodegeneration and cancer. Third, we highlight underexplored regulatory dimensions of IDP biology, including proline isomerization and ubiquitylation-driven condensate formation, that influence conformational ensembles and signaling outputs in ways not captured by conventional structural approaches. Finally, we critically evaluate recent advances in AI-assisted disorder prediction and hybrid experimental-computational ensemble characterization, emphasizing both their transformative potential and current limitations. Dysregulation of IDPs underlies a broad spectrum of human pathologies, and we discuss the emerging opportunities and persistent challenges in targeting these conformationally dynamic proteins therapeutically, including through PROTAC-based degraders, condensate modulators, and ensemble-based drug screening strategies.


## Introduction
  The role of genes in cellular functions is directly linked to RNA transcripts and protein products. Proteins are structural molecular machines allowing for diverse cell interactions and functions. For decades, the concept that “structure is destiny” has shaped our perception of proteins as highly folded molecular machines with specialized functions. In fact, the role of proteins was described using the accepted structure-function model, which holds that a protein should adopt a stable, compact three-dimensional structure to function optimally under physiological conditions [1,2]. However, an emerging school of thought, the disorder-function paradigm, now challenges this long-standing view by highlighting the role of Intrinsically Disordered Proteins (IDPs), which function without rigid tertiary structure [3]. In fact, IDPs exhibit excellent structural plasticity, not static conformations but dynamic ensembles. Intrinsically Disordered Regions (IDRs) in these proteins correspond to functional domains involved in various biological processes [4]. IDPs and IDRs significantly increase the proteome's functional repertoire. Intrinsic disorder in proteins is regarded as an evolutionary adaptation for enhanced regulatory specificity. They are outstanding performers in conditions requiring adaptability, such as transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and dynamic scaffolding in cellular contexts [5]. IDPs act as molecular hubs for cellular networks, mediating transient, context-dependent interactions. Their capacity for rapid, multivalent interactions makes them powerful at organizing complex signaling events beyond the capabilities of structured proteins [6]. Moreover, structural disorder enables IDPs to act as scaffolds for large molecular assemblies by binding diverse partners, aided by short linear motifs (SLiMs) and molecular recognition features (MoRFs): the larger the complexes, the greater the fraction of disordered content. Notably, even after binding, IDPs maintain an open, dynamic conformation, and a single IDR can bind several partners simultaneously. This mirrors the architectural and functional richness of protein disorder-based assemblies.

  Although IDPs are functionally versatile, their lack of structural rigidity renders them prone to disease-causing misfolding or aggregation. For instance, proteins linked to neurodegenerative illnesses (e.g., α-synuclein, tau, TDP-43), cancers (e.g., p53, c-Myc), cardiovascular disease, and viral pathogenesis often contain large areas of disorder [7]. In disease contexts, the same conformational plasticity that allows for normal function can have deleterious consequences, such as toxic aggregation, aberrant phase separation, or dysregulated protein interactions [8]. These processes lead to the characteristic manifestations of disease, such as protein aggregates and oncogenic signaling. With increasing awareness of the limitations of the structure-based paradigm, the scientific community is embracing a deeper understanding of protein biology. Protein disorder is no longer a defect but a functional property that necessitates specialized tools for its study [9]. In this review, we track the conceptual development of IDPs, their varied physiological role, and their implication in disease. We emphasize novel methodologies revolutionizing our capacity to study these proteins. Understanding the functional dynamics of IDPs opens therapeutic avenues to otherwise untreatable diseases that are associated with IDP malfunction.


## Classification and structural features of IDPs

### IDP types: fully disordered vs. partially disordered proteins
  IDRs and IDPs are highly prevalent in eukaryotic genomes, yet their functional characterization is limited due to the lack of structural rigidity and rapid evolutionary divergence. Conventional protein annotation methods, which rely heavily on structured domains and sequence families, overlook a substantial fraction of proteins or protein parts that do not attain a structured conformation. For example, nearly half of human protein-coding genes contain long disordered segments, and genes lacking functional annotation are disproportionately enriched in IDRs [10]. Because sequence alignment and homology-based inference are less effective for IDRs, their functions are often underrepresented. These limitations prompted the need for a dedicated classification framework for IDRs and IDPs to improve functional prediction and systematic annotation of this large and biologically significant protein class.

  IDRs can be classified into fully disordered proteins, which remain unstructured across their entire length (such as α-synuclein), and partially disordered proteins that contain specific disordered segments, typically in N- or C-terminal regions or interdomain linkers [11]. Notably, many IDPs exhibit conditional disorder, in which they fold upon binding to their binding partners, a phenomenon termed “folding upon binding” [12]. This adaptability enables IDPs to engage multiple targets with diverse structural outcomes, often via conformational selection or induced-fit mechanisms [13]. This taxonomic diversity from fully disordered proteins such as α-synuclein to conditionally disordered conformational rheostats like nuclear co-activator binding domain (NCBD) has an important mechanistic implication that is often underappreciated: the functional consequences of disorder are not binary but instead exist on a continuum determined by folding kinetics, partner availability, cellular crowding, and the PTM state of key residues. Consequently, proteins classified as partially disordered under one physiological condition may behave as extensively disordered systems under another. This context-dependence suggests that intrinsic disorder should not be interpreted as a static structural category, but rather as a dynamically regulated functional state shaped by the cellular environment [14,15].


### Common structural traits: lack of stable 3D structure & amino acid bias
  IDPs or IDRs exhibit a fundamental simplicity of sequence compared to ordered proteins, reflected in their amino acid composition, sequence patterns, conformational plasticity, and structural organization. IDPs are enriched in disorder-promoting residues such as Proline, Serine, Glutamine, Lysine, and Glycine, and are notably depleted in hydrophobic, bulky residues such as Leucine, Isoleucine, and Phenylalanine [[16], [17], [18]]. This composition disrupts the formation of stable hydrophobic cores, a hallmark of structured domains, and supports their flexible, extended, or collapsed structural states. Unlike folded proteins that adopt a single stable conformation, IDPs fluctuate among diverse low-energy conformations [19]. The disordered regions often exhibit transient secondary structure elements, such as α-helices or polyproline II helices, which can become stabilized upon partner binding or post-translational modifications [20]. This structural fluidity allows them to function in a wide range of biological contexts, from molecular recognition to scaffolding and phase separation [19].


### Tools for prediction
  Due to their dynamic nature and the limitations of classical structural biology methods, such as X-ray crystallography, in capturing their conformational states, IDPs are primarily studied through computational predictions and specialized biophysical techniques. The field of intrinsic disorder prediction has advanced over four decades, producing over 100 computational predictors and extensive databases [21]. Predictive methods have evolved from early machine learning approaches to meta-predictors and, more recently, deep learning models, which currently offer the most promising performance. A wide range of accessible tools, including web servers, standalone programs, and large databases, facilitate accurate prediction and functional annotation of disordered regions. Prediction tools fall into several categories. Heuristic-based tools, such as the Uversky plot, rely on empirical charge-hydropathy correlations [22]. Machine learning-based predictors, such as PONDR [23,24], DISOPRED [25], and IUPred [26], analyze features like amino acid composition, sequence complexity, and evolutionary conservation to assign disorder probabilities to each residue. Newer meta-predictors, such as MobiDB-lite and flDPnn, combine outputs from multiple methods, thereby improving accuracy and reliability, especially for high-throughput applications [27]. Wang and colleagues provided the most comprehensive recent overview of intrinsic disorder prediction resources [28], systematically evaluating protein-language model–based predictors, deep learning meta-servers, and curated disorder databases. Their work serves as an important benchmark for assessing current prediction tools and their performance across diverse classes of intrinsically disordered proteins. Despite substantial advances in computational prediction, important conceptual and technical limitations persist in accurately characterizing IDPs. No single predictor performs uniformly well across all disorder subclasses, particularly for transient binding regions such as MoRFs and SLiMs, where prediction accuracy remains significantly lower than for long disordered segments. Importantly, this limitation reflects not only insufficient algorithmic performance but also a deeper conceptual challenge in distinguishing structural disorder from functional disorder. Furthermore, most machine-learning predictors are trained on experimentally characterized datasets dominated by well-studied eukaryotic proteins, potentially introducing systematic biases against unusual disorder mechanisms, viral proteins, and non-model organisms. Although recent deep learning approaches have improved predictive performance, they can still generate highly confident but biologically misleading predictions for compositionally atypical sequences. Consequently, computational approaches should currently be viewed primarily as hypothesis-generating tools that require rigorous experimental validation through NMR spectroscopy, SAXS, single-molecule approaches, and in-cell structural studies. Compositional biases in IDPs differ across disorder classes, defining both their biological roles and prediction accuracy [29]. This gap between computational prediction and biological reality highlights a central challenge in IDP research. It underpins the mechanistic and disease-focused perspective developed throughout this review. It emphasizes that, while computational tools are invaluable, they cannot fully capture the context-dependent functional consequences of intrinsic disorder in living systems. An important unresolved challenge is the prediction of condition-dependent disorder, where conformational behavior is influenced by post-translational modifications, molecular crowding, binding partners, and cellular context. Capturing these dynamic influences remains a major objective for next-generation disorder prediction frameworks.


## The sequence code: molecular grammar of disordered regions
  If IDPs lack a fixed three-dimensional structure, what, then, specifies their behavior? A growing body of work indicates that the function of a disordered region is encoded within its sequence, albeit not as a foldable architecture, but rather as an ensemble of compositional and patterning features that collectively constitute a “molecular grammar” [[30], [31], [32]]. Two proteins bearing markedly different primary sequences may nonetheless behave similarly provided they share the same underlying grammar; conversely, a single mutation that preserves overall composition while altering patterning can perturb behavior substantially. This perspective elegantly reconciles the apparent paradox whereby disordered sequences are poorly conserved at the residue level yet retain conserved functions across evolution [33].

  The most thoroughly developed component of this grammar is the stickers-and-spacers framework, itself borrowed from the physics of associative polymers [32]. Within phase-separating low-complexity and prion-like domains, a defined subset of residues function as “stickers” that establish the cohesive, multivalent contacts driving association, whereas the intervening “spacers” operate as flexible linkers that modulate solubility and chain dimensions [30,31]. In prion-like RNA-binding proteins such as FUS and hnRNPA1, aromatic residues (namely tyrosine and phenylalanine) constitute the dominant stickers, with arginine serving an auxiliary role, and their interactions are governed predominantly by π–π and cation–π contacts [30,34]. Decisively, both the valence (i.e., the number of stickers) and their patterning (i.e., their spacing along the chain) dictate phase behavior: a relatively uniform distribution of stickers favors the formation of dynamic liquid droplets, whereas their clustering predisposes the system to solid aggregates [31]. The molecular grammar thus encodes not merely whether a given region undergoes phase separation, but indeed which material state it ultimately adopts, thereby establishing a direct mechanistic link to the liquid-to-solid pathology.

  A second, complementary component is charge. For the substantial fraction of IDRs that behave as polyampholytes or polyelectrolytes, conformational behavior is dictated by the fraction of charged residues, the net charge per residue, and, crucially, the precise linear arrangement of oppositely charged residues along the chain. Sequences in which positive and negative charges are well intermixed tend to adopt expanded, soluble conformations, whereas those in which like charges are segregated into discrete blocks adopt more compact conformations and exhibit a heightened propensity for self-association [35,36]. This patterning is captured quantitatively by descriptors such as the charge-patterning parameter κ [35] and the sequence charge decoration (SCD) [37]; in conjunction with overall composition, these descriptors relate disordered sequences to their ensemble dimensions and phase behavior [35,36], while accessible toolkits such as CIDER/localCIDER render such analyses routine [38]. Importantly, charge patterning is biologically tunable in real time, insofar as the charge-altering post-translational modifications (i.e., phosphorylation, acetylation, and others) effectively rewrite the charge grammar of a given sequence.

  The same grammar that governs conformation and phase behavior likewise governs molecular recognition and subcellular localization. Binding is frequently mediated by short linear motifs (SLiMs) [39] and molecular recognition features (MoRFs) [40] embedded within disordered regions, with the surrounding sequence context serving to fine-tune their affinity and specificity. Localization signals, such as nuclear localization and export sequences, are similarly embedded within IDRs, and the composition and sequence features of a given protein contribute to determining which condensate it preferentially partitions into, thereby routing proteins, in effect, to a defined cellular address [41,42]. In this respect, the molecular grammar may be regarded as a unifying code that connects sequence to conformation, to binding, to localization, and ultimately to condensate identity.

  This framework furnishes a mechanistic vocabulary for disease. Pathogenic variants within disordered regions frequently act not by disrupting a fold (there being, in fact, none to disrupt), but rather by rewriting the grammar itself: by adding or removing a sticker, or by converting well-mixed charges into a more blocky arrangement, thereby shifting both the conformational ensemble and the underlying phase diagram. The multisystem-proteinopathy and ALS mutations within the prion-like domains of hnRNPA1 and hnRNPA2B1 are paradigmatic in this regard, introducing alterations that heighten the propensity to form solid, fibrillar assemblies from what would otherwise constitute dynamic condensates [43,44]. The interpretation of variants of uncertain significance within disordered regions through the lens of molecular grammar therefore represents an emerging diagnostic and mechanistic strategy.


## Physiological roles of IDPs
  Given their highly dynamic and flexible nature, IDPs are now recognized for their crucial involvement in a wide array of cellular processes, often mediating complex interactions and regulatory mechanisms distinct from those of their globular counterparts [45,46]. Their unique structural characteristics, particularly their lack of a fixed conformation, enable them to adopt diverse functional roles critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to environmental cues [47,48].


### In signaling, transcription, and scaffolding
  IDPs are centrally involved in cellular signaling and information transduction, primarily through their interactions with other macromolecules [46]. IDPs bind diverse protein partners with high specificity, enabling complex physiological processes through conformational refolding upon binding, making them critical components in protein complex assembly and function [49]. Beyond simple binary interactions, IDPs function as “interaction specialists” and “global controllers” playing crucial roles in regulating the activity of their binding partners and orchestrating large-scale biological networks [45,50]. Their significance in cell signaling pathways is broadly acknowledged [51], and their pervasive involvement extends to other vital biological areas, such as the intricate mechanisms governing synaptic vesicle trafficking, exocytosis, and the overall organization of synaptic structures [[52], [53], [54]]. Several well-characterized examples illustrate the critical roles these flexible proteins play at the synapse [55].

  Alpha-synuclein represents a prominent example of an intrinsically disordered presynaptic protein that regulates fundamental aspects of synaptic vesicle dynamics [55]. This protein regulates vesicle clustering, trafficking, and neurotransmitter release, illustrating how intrinsic disorder supports diverse synaptic functions [56]. Its dose- and conformation-dependent activity highlights the importance of intrinsic disorder. Likewise, synapsins regulate synaptic vesicle dynamics and availability [57]. Their flexible structure facilitates the clustering and organization of vesicle pools, which are essential for maintaining sustained neurotransmitter release [57].

  Synaptotagmin-1 and related synaptotagmin family members exemplify how intrinsic disorder combined with functional calcium-binding domains enables sophisticated regulation of exocytosis [58]. Calcium-binding C2 domains mediate interactions with phospholipids and SNARE proteins, coupling calcium sensing to membrane fusion and neurotransmitter release [57].

  Complexins represent another class of intrinsically disordered regulatory proteins that modulate both the kinetics and extent of synaptic vesicle fusion [59]. These proteins fine-tune synaptic vesicle fusion by coupling synaptotagmin activity to the fusion machinery [59]. The lack of stable structure enables complexins to adopt multiple conformational states, allowing them to perform their dual regulatory roles during different phases of the vesicle cycle [59].

  Beyond these well-known examples, IDPs participate in organizing the architecture of synaptic terminals themselves [51]. Active zone scaffolding proteins exploit intrinsic disorder to coordinate vesicle recruitment, priming, fusion, and neurotransmitter release [57]. Several important limitations temper the interpretation of current studies on synaptic IDPs. Much of the available mechanistic evidence derives from in vitro reconstitution systems or protein overexpression models that may not accurately reproduce the highly crowded, PTM-rich environment of intact presynaptic terminals. In addition, functional assignments frequently rely on loss-of-function approaches that may reflect compensatory network responses rather than direct mechanistic roles. Certain widely cited models, including α-synuclein-mediated vesicle clustering, have also faced reproducibility challenges across experimental systems. These unresolved issues highlight the need for approaches capable of studying IDPs directly within intact cellular environments, including in-cell NMR, proximity-based interactomics, and cryo-electron tomography of native synaptic structures.

  Intrinsic disorder is central to membrane trafficking, exocytosis, gene expression, and chromatin remodeling, enabling dynamic interactions that allow IDPs to function as versatile regulatory hubs in diverse cellular processes [60]. Several well-characterized examples illustrate the critical importance of IDRs within transcription factors for controlling diverse aspects of gene regulation.

  p53: The Guardian of the Genome represents a paradigmatic example of an intrinsically disordered transcription factor [61]. The intrinsically disordered N-terminal transactivation domain (TAD) of p53 mediates interactions with coactivators and the transcriptional machinery. This structural flexibility enables p53 to engage multiple regulatory partners and activate diverse stress-responsive genes [62]. The conformational plasticity conferred by p53's IDRs enables context-dependent transcriptional regulation, a feature essential to its tumor-suppressor function and nuanced responses to different types of cellular damage.

  c-MYC (c-Myc) serves as another prominent example of an intrinsically disordered transcription factor with profound implications for cancer biology [63]. c-MYC, activated in over 70% of human cancers, is largely intrinsically disordered and contains an N-terminal transactivation domain that dynamically switches between inactive and active conformations [63]. This conformational switching highlights how intrinsic disorder enables dynamic transcriptional regulation, a principle similarly employed by bHLH transcription factors through their IDRs [64]. Members of the bHLH family contain intrinsically disordered C-terminal regions enriched in short linear motifs that mediate diverse and context-dependent protein interactions. [65]. These IDRs contribute to target-gene specificity and temporal regulation while promoting phase separation, which concentrates the transcriptional machinery at active genomic loci [66].

  OCT4 and Pluripotency Factors demonstrate how IDRs enable cell-type-specific functions of a single transcription factor [67]. OCT4 and GATA transcription factors exemplify how IDRs mediate context-dependent chromatin interactions and cooperative gene regulation by dynamically associating with chromatin-remodeling complexes [68]. Interactions between transcription factor IDRs and the SWI/SNF complex exemplify how intrinsic disorder promotes cooperative chromatin binding and transcriptional regulation [69]. The cBAF subunit ARID1A utilizes an extensive IDR to promote condensate formation and transcription factor interactions, thereby regulating chromatin accessibility and cellular differentiation [70].

  IDRs enhance transcription factor function by facilitating target recognition, dynamic interactions, and phase separation, thereby fine-tuning gene expression during development, differentiation, and cellular homeostasis [64,70].

  For instance, in plants, IDPs are integral to transcription factor networks, where their flexibility enables them to act as integrators of diverse signals, effectively linking distinct transcription factor subnetworks [71]. This flexibility enables transcription factors to integrate environmental, hormonal, and developmental signals through interactions with multiple partners. Histone tails provide another prominent example of IDRs that regulate chromatin organization and gene expression [72]. These N-terminal protruding regions of histone proteins are sites of extensive post-translational modification, including acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation, that control chromatin accessibility [73]. The conformational flexibility of histone tails is functionally important, as modifications such as H4 acetylation alter their charge properties, weaken histone–DNA interactions, and enhance DNA accessibility [74]. The intrinsically disordered C-terminal region of histone H1 drives phase separation-mediated heterochromatin condensation and regulates higher-order chromatin organization [75]. Beyond eukaryotic systems, bacterial nucleoid-associated proteins perform functions analogous to those of histones, organizing the bacterial chromosome while simultaneously regulating gene expression [76]. These NAPs, including H-NS, HU, IHF, and Fis, constitute a diverse group that collectively compact and organize bacterial DNA into a dynamic nucleoid structure [77]. IDRs in nucleoid-associated proteins, such as the linker region of H-NS, contribute to DNA binding and chromatin organization through electrostatic interactions. [78]. H-NS exhibits remarkable environmental responsiveness by forming dynamic phase-separated condensates with DNA that respond to temperature, osmolarity, and nutrient availability, enabling bacteria to couple genome organization to environmental stress responses [79]. Mycobacterial HupB contains a distinctive C-terminal domain with eukaryotic histone-like motifs; this eukaryotic-like tail is indispensable for HupB's association with the nucleoid and its role in organizing newly replicated DNA regions [80]. Like eukaryotic histone tails, these bacterial NAP domains undergo post-translational modifications, such as the acetylation of Streptomyces HupS, that dynamically regulate NAP-DNA interactions and their transcriptional regulatory activity [81]. Topoisomerases, a well-characterized class of essential enzymes, also exhibit structural flexibility due to several intrinsically disordered loops within their rigidly structured domains [82]. Such flexibility is essential for these enzymes to catalyze topological alterations in DNA during replication and transcription [82].

  A large-scale study of IDPs in Arabidopsis thaliana revealed that certain functional classes, predominantly those associated with environmental response, exhibit a notable enrichment in disorder [48]. Intrinsic disorder promotes environmental adaptability in plants, with AP2/ERF and DREB transcription factors utilizing IDRs to mediate diverse regulatory interactions [71]. The flexibility of these IDRs enables rapid adaptation within complex interaction networks, particularly during plant stress responses that require integration of diverse environmental signals. [83]. Similarly, the conformational plasticity of histone tails enables rapid remodeling of chromatin architecture in response to environmental stimuli, allowing genes encoding stress-response proteins to be quickly activated when conditions warrant [84]. In bacteria, NAP-mediated nucleoid reorganization serves analogous functions: nucleoid-associated proteins act as coupled sensor-effectors that directly regulate gene expression through chromatin remodeling in response to physicochemical environmental signals [85]. Collectively, these examples highlight intrinsic disorder as a conserved strategy for dynamic chromatin regulation and adaptive gene expression across diverse biological systems. An important conceptual implication emerging from these observations is that intrinsic disorder may represent an evolutionary strategy optimized for regulatory adaptability rather than maximal structural precision. Unlike globular proteins, whose functions frequently depend on maintaining highly stable folded conformations, IDPs appear particularly suited to fluctuating signaling environments that require rapid rewiring of interaction networks. However, this adaptive flexibility is accompanied by increased vulnerability to proteotoxic stress, aberrant phase transitions, and pathological aggregation. The widespread evolutionary conservation of intrinsic disorder, therefore, likely reflects a fundamental trade-off between functional plasticity and biophysical stability.

  IDPs are also prominent in scaffolding functions, facilitating the assembly of macromolecular complexes and membrane-less organelles [86,87]. Proteins characterized by multiple localization signals and a high number of interaction partners, often referred to as hub proteins, frequently possess a multi-domain architecture with substantial intrinsic disorder [88]. Hub proteins containing extensive IDRs act as central scaffolds that organize diverse interaction networks across multiple cellular compartments. Their disordered regions promote multivalent, low-affinity interactions, enabling the assembly of dynamic protein complexes involved in processes such as transcription and post-translational regulation [89].

  These hub proteins often shuttle between the cytoplasm and nucleus through PTM-regulated IDRs. HP1 exemplifies this principle, using multiple IDRs to confer conformational flexibility, mediate intermolecular interactions, and integrate diverse cellular signals [90]. These IDRs are critical for HP1's function in linking nucleosomes with chromatin-associated proteins in a chromatin environment, and their conformational flexibility allows HP1 to undergo dynamic rearrangements necessary for its multiple roles in gene silencing, transcriptional elongation, DNA repair, and cell cycle regulation [90]. PTM-dependent histone mimicry within HP1 IDRs enables autoinhibitory regulation, allowing modifications to control its activity and subcellular localization [90]. Similar scaffolding functions are observed in PELP1, a rixosome-associated protein whose proline-rich IDR mediates interactions with multiple partners, illustrating how a single disordered region can coordinate the assembly of diverse regulatory complexes. [91]. Transcription factors such as CEBPA utilize IDR-driven phase separation to form nuclear condensates that regulate chromatin organization and cellular differentiation [92]. Together, these examples illustrate how intrinsic disorder drives dynamic scaffolding and condensate formation, enabling the organization of complex regulatory, cellular, and viral processes [86]. Simplified polypeptide systems, which narrow the scope of chemical space for exploration, have been used to understand the impact of IDP/IDR sequences on phase separation, demonstrating that IDP function can be understood in terms of general chemical features, such as charged amino acid clusters [93].


### Flexibility allowing one protein to interact with many binding-partners
  A hallmark characteristic of IDPs is their extreme dynamism and inherent flexibility, which are fundamental aspects enabling their diverse functional roles. This structural malleability is widely considered a key evolutionary driver of their existence, as it confers the unique ability to transiently bind to a wide range of partners while maintaining high specificity in each interaction [46]. Unlike globular proteins, IDPs interact with multiple partners through flexible binding modes, enabling dynamic regulation of complex cellular networks [45].

  While IDP-mediated interactions are often characterized by high specificity, they typically exhibit low affinity, a critical feature for the rapid assembly and disassembly of complexes, facilitating dynamic cellular responses [48]. The adaptable interaction surfaces of IDPs facilitate rapid, dynamic interactions essential for signaling and cell-cycle control. [47]. The ability to bind to multiple partners, sometimes simultaneously, enables IDPs to act as molecular hubs, integrating information from various pathways [48]. For example, the tumor suppressor PTEN, identified as an IDP, possesses an IDR at its carboxyl-terminus (C-tail IDR) that is crucial for its functions [94]. Both PTEN and its interacting protein network (interactome) are notably enriched for other IDPs, which collectively form pliable network hubs. These intricate networks operate through multiple IDP-IDP interactions, all facilitated and nucleated by the flexible C-tail IDR of PTEN, showcasing how disorder underpins complex multi-partner associations [94].


### Post-translational modifications regulate IDP functions
  Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are pivotal regulatory mechanisms that exert profound control over the functions and structural properties of IDPs [46]. These chemical modifications significantly alter the energy landscapes of IDPs, leading to a wide range of effects that modulate their behavior and interactions (Table 1). The impact of PTMs can be localized, stabilizing or destabilizing transient secondary structures within the IDP, or global, inducing complete disorder-to-order transitions [95]. Such transitions can even drive fundamental state changes, shifting an IDP from a completely disordered state to a folded state, or from a dispersed monomeric state to a phase-separated state, which is crucial for the formation of membrane-less organelles [95]. Consequently, many biological processes depend on precise PTM-mediated regulation of IDPs, underscoring their essential role in cellular physiology [95].   Among the numerous types of PTMs, phosphorylation is among the most abundant, significantly influencing the conformational changes and functional states of IDPs [96]. Studies examining transcription factors reveal that phosphorylation events directly remodel protein conformations; the phosphorylation of Myc at specific serine and threonine residues destabilizes transient α-helical structures formed during protein-protein interactions, thereby disrupting its binding to Max and DNA [96]. In contrast, phosphorylation of conserved IDRs in bacterial kinases is necessary for signal transduction, where the modification promotes trans-autophosphorylation by enabling cooperative phosphorylation cascades [97]. In histone systems, phosphorylation of IDRs within histone variants, such as macroH2A1, produces distinct phosphorylation patterns between isoforms that contribute to their differential biological responses [98]. Beyond phosphorylation, the overall charge content and the specific patterning of charged residues within an IDP sequence are profoundly affected by charge regulation, which encompasses multiple PTMs, including acetylation, succinylation, PARylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and even non-enzymatic glycation, as well as pH-dependent charge changes [99]. These alterations in charge state, which can also arise from conformational fluctuations modifying the pKa values of ionizable residues, have a significant impact on the conformational ensembles adopted by IDPs, influencing their interactions and stability [73]. Histone tails exemplify this principle perfectly, as acetylation and PARylation modulate their electrostatic potentials in a tail-specific manner, with charge-altering modifications such as acetylation decreasing the positive charge concentrated in basic patch regions and suppressing tail-DNA interactions, thereby enhancing histone tail dynamics and increasing DNA accessibility to transcriptional machinery [99]. The H4 tail demonstrates that acetylation significantly alters the chemical environment of basic patch residues and mediates tail compaction by altering intramolecular electrostatic interactions [74]. At the same time, histone glycation, a non-enzymatic covalent modification that accumulates under normal physiological conditions and is stimulated by metabolic stress, has global ramifications on histone enzymatic PTMs, nucleosome assembly and stability, and chromatin architecture, with evidence suggesting a physiologic regulation mechanism through the enzyme DJ-1 functioning as a potent histone deglycase [100]. HupS exemplifies how charge-altering PTMs regulate nucleoid-associated protein function by dynamically modulating DNA-binding interactions [81]. The acetylation of HupS lysine residues reduces positive charge, thereby diminishing DNA binding and disrupting chromosome organization, demonstrating that NAPs employ the same charge-regulatory strategies as eukaryotic histones [101]. At the same time, the bacterial NAP H-NS further illustrates this charge-regulatory principle by exhibiting remarkable environmental responsiveness, in which osmolarity-dependent changes in ionic strength effectively alter the protein's charge state by modulating electrostatic screening, thereby controlling its oligomerization state and DNA-binding propensity [102]. Collectively, these examples highlight charge regulation as a conserved mechanism through which PTMs modulate IDP conformations and interactions, linking environmental cues to chromatin organization and gene regulation. Among post-translational modifications, N-glycosylation is a notable exception near aggregation-prone regions, where it helps suppress protein aggregation and protects against misfolding [103]. The modulation of IDP binding-strength by PTMs is so significant that specialized resources, such as the DIBS repository, have been developed to describe potential PTMs that influence these interactions. This repository provides crucial information on how PTMs can fine-tune the affinity and specificity of IDP interactions with ordered proteins [104]. PTEN exemplifies how intrinsically disordered regions enriched in linear motifs and recognition elements mediate protein–protein interactions, with PTMs dynamically regulating these networks and their associated cellular functions [94].

  Another important PTM is ubiquitylation that broadly regulates cellular physiology, including cell signaling, cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and transcriptional control [105]. TRIM family proteins, such as TRIM37, function as E3 ubiquitin ligases and orchestrate renal cell carcinoma progression through histone H2A ubiquitination-dependent mechanisms [105]. The critical insight linking ubiquitination to IDPs is that E3 ubiquitin ligases predominantly recognize their substrates through short linear motifs called degrons, which are characteristically located within the IDRs of target proteins, thereby establishing a fundamental mechanism by which IDRs facilitate substrate specificity and ubiquitination [106]. BRCA1/BARD1 illustrates how IDR-mediated multivalent interactions promote chromatin recruitment and efficient histone H2A ubiquitination, linking intrinsic disorder to DNA repair and transcriptional regulation [107]. Intrinsic disorder enables E3 ubiquitin ligases such as San1 to recognize structurally diverse substrates, a strategy conserved across multiple ligases that employ disordered regions and short interaction motifs to balance substrate recognition with controlled ubiquitination [108]. The CHIP E3 ubiquitin ligase further demonstrates this paradigm by binding directly to intrinsically disordered domains in its substrates, such as the tau protein, where the flexibility of the IDP allows CHIP to direct ubiquitination at multiple sites without requiring chaperone assistance [109]. Ubiquitylation can drive IDR-mediated phase separation, creating condensates that spatially coordinate and enhance ubiquitination reactions [110]. Collectively, these mechanisms demonstrate how IDRs regulate ubiquitin ligase activity and specificity through structural plasticity, multivalent interactions, and dynamic post-translational control, thereby contributing to genomic stability and disease prevention [111].

  Collectively, these examples demonstrate that the functional importance of intrinsic disorder extends beyond simple structural flexibility. Across signaling pathways, chromatin regulation, stress adaptation, membrane trafficking, and condensate assembly, IDPs repeatedly emerge as dynamic regulatory platforms that reorganize interaction networks in response to changing cellular conditions. A recurring mechanistic principle linking these diverse systems is the coupling between conformational heterogeneity, multivalent interactions, and PTM-dependent regulation. Importantly, this same functional plasticity also explains the pathological susceptibility of IDPs: the conformational adaptability that enables physiological responsiveness can, under dysregulated conditions, promote aggregation, aberrant phase separation, and maladaptive signaling amplification.


## Biomolecular condensates and Liquid–Liquid phase separation
  Beyond their well-established roles as conformationally adaptable binding partners and molecular scaffolds, IDPs and IDRs have emerged as central drivers of biomolecular condensates, namely membrane-less compartments that selectively concentrate specific proteins and nucleic acids in the absence of a delimiting lipid bilayer [112,113]. Such condensates orchestrate a substantial portion of cellular biochemistry, encompassing organelles as diverse as the nucleolus, stress granules, P-bodies, Cajal bodies, and nuclear speckles, and they arise through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), whereby the cytoplasm or nucleoplasm demixes into a condensed, protein-rich phase that coexists with a more dilute one [112,114]. Notably, even constitutive heterochromatin appears to be organized, at least in part, through phase separation of HP1 proteins [115,116]. The recognition that intrinsic disorder underlies this organizing principle has reframed IDPs not merely as conformationally plastic molecules, but as the very physical substrate of a major layer of subcellular compartmentalization.

  The thermodynamic driving force underlying LLPS is a network of weak, multivalent interactions. Whereas folded proteins typically achieve multivalency through arrays of discrete structured domains [117], IDRs furnish multivalency directly through their primary sequence, insofar as low-complexity and prion-like domains, together with regions enriched in aromatic or charged residues, present numerous weak interaction sites distributed along a single flexible chain. Once the concentration of such a protein surpasses a saturation threshold, itself tunable by valency, binding partners, and prevailing solution conditions, the system demixes into droplets [44,118]. Archetypal examples include the RNA-binding proteins FUS, hnRNPA1, and TDP-43, whose disordered low-complexity domains are necessary and frequently sufficient to drive condensation, as well as the germline protein DDX4 [44,118,119].

  Within condensates, it is instructive to distinguish scaffold molecules, which supply the multivalent interactions that nucleate and sustain the dense phase, from client molecules, which lack sufficient valency to phase separate in isolation yet partition readily into pre-existing condensates; it is ultimately the balance between scaffolds and clients that governs condensate composition and stoichiometry [41,42].

  Functionally, condensates afford a layer of spatiotemporal control that complements the conformational plasticity emphasized earlier in this review. By selectively concentrating certain reactants while excluding others, they can accelerate or, conversely, insulate biochemical reactions, sequester components during periods of cellular stress, buffer the effective concentrations of free molecules, and assemble dedicated signaling platforms; the clustering of the LAT adaptor downstream of the T-cell receptor, reconstituted in vitro from purified components, constitutes a particularly well-characterized example in which condensation directly potentiates signaling output [117,120]. Within the nucleus, IDR-driven condensation concentrates the transcriptional machinery at active loci and super-enhancers, thereby furnishing a physical mechanism for the cooperative, switch-like behavior that typifies gene control [121]. Importantly, these roles are subject to dynamic regulation: concentration and valency set the threshold for condensation, RNA and other nucleic acids can either promote or dissolve condensates, and post-translational modifications operate as rapid molecular switches. Phosphorylation of low-complexity domains, for instance, can raise the saturation concentration and thereby dissolve condensates [122], whereas ubiquitylation can conversely promote condensate formation, consistent with the ubiquitylation-driven condensation discussed in the preceding sections.

  Critically, condensates do not embody a single material state, but rather occupy a continuum extending from dynamic liquids through viscoelastic gels to rigid solids. Although many physiological condensates behave as liquids, the very same multivalent interactions that drive LLPS can, over time (a process termed “aging”) or under disease-associated conditions, precipitate a liquid-to-gel-to-solid transition that ultimately nucleates amyloid fibrils. This progressive maturation links condensate biology directly to the aggregation pathology developed later in this review: disease-associated mutations within the prion-like domains of FUS and hnRNPA1 accelerate the conversion of liquid droplets into solid assemblies in ALS and frontotemporal dementia, and tau undergoes an analogous liquid-to-solid transition [44,119]. Condensates therefore represent, simultaneously, both a physiological organizing principle and a latent point of failure.


## IDPs vs globular proteins
  The shift in protein science has brought into focus the distinct characteristics and mechanisms of IDPs when compared to their well-structured globular protein counterparts [45]. While both classes of proteins are fundamental to life, their approaches to folding, binding, and maintaining thermodynamic stability are profoundly different, necessitating distinct experimental and theoretical frameworks for their study [123,124]. Understanding these differences is crucial for a comprehensive appreciation of protein function and pathology.


### Differences in folding, binding, and thermodynamic stability
  A primary distinction between IDPs and globular proteins lies in their fundamental structural definition. Globular proteins typically fold into compact, well-defined three-dimensional structures, which are essential for their specific functions, adhering to the conventional structure-function paradigm [49,123]. In contrast, IDPs do not, by themselves, fold into such compact globular structures under physiological conditions, existing instead as highly dynamic conformational ensembles [46,86]. This inherent lack of a stable tertiary structure in IDPs challenges the long-held belief that a unique structure is intimately associated with protein function, emphasizing the importance of biologically active proteins that remain disordered [45]. Despite their unstructured nature, IDPs play vital roles in cellular processes, prompting researchers to re-evaluate conventional understandings of protein biophysics [123].

  While the fundamental forces governing conformational changes and binding are universal across all proteins, the mechanisms through which these processes occur differ significantly between globular proteins and IDPs [124]. The folding and binding mechanism of globular proteins differs from the coupled folding and binding mechanisms observed in IDPs. For IDPs, coupled folding and binding predominantly occur via an induced-fit mechanism. This is mainly due to the characteristically slow folding kinetics of IDPs in their free, unbound state, which often results in them adopting a more ordered structure only upon encountering their binding partner. Conversely, ligand binding by globular proteins typically proceeds through a conformational selection mechanism. This mechanism is characterized by rapid conformational changes within the pre-existing ensemble of the globular protein, allowing it to select a binding-competent conformation that then interacts with its ligand [124].

  Protein folding itself can be conceptually understood as the binding of intramolecular segments, a process often accompanied by the formation of secondary structures [124]. In this context, multi-subdomain proteins tend to fold primarily through an induced-fit (hydrophobic collapse) mechanism, in which the connection of interacting segments enhances compaction. Smaller proteins with fewer hydrophobic residues often exhibit lower intramolecular binding rates, leading to a nucleation-condensation folding mechanism. Despite these mechanistic differences, the folding and binding processes of both globular proteins and IDPs ultimately adhere to the same general biophysical principles [124]. This unified perspective suggests that coarse-grained statistical mechanical models of protein folding hold promise for providing a comprehensive theoretical description that encompasses all these diverse mechanisms. The thermodynamic stability of IDPs is intrinsically linked to their conformational heterogeneity. Under physiological conditions, IDPs exist as highly dynamic ensembles of rapidly interconverting conformations, rather than a single stable 3D structure [45,86]. The overall charge content and the specific patterning of charged residues within an IDP's amino acid sequence are crucial determinants that profoundly influence the specific conformational ensembles adopted by these proteins [125]. These sequence-specific characteristics dictate their interaction profiles and functional plasticity.


### Unique dynamic behavior and “fuzzy” interactions
  The defining characteristic of IDPs is their extreme dynamism and flexibility, setting them apart from the more rigid nature of globular proteins [46]. IDPs exist not as single, fixed structures, but as highly dynamic ensembles of rapidly interconverting conformations, often described as a multitude of short-lifetime structures. This inherent structural fluidity confers immense binding promiscuity on IDPs, enabling them to engage in a broad spectrum of binding modes. These interactions frequently result in the formation of short-lived complexes, which are crucial for transient signaling events and rapid regulatory responses within the cell [45].

  A particularly distinctive mode of interaction for IDPs is termed “fuzzy binding”. This refers to complexes formed between an IDP and another macromolecule where the IDP maintains significant conformational flexibility even in the bound state, interacting through multiple transient sites or an extended, adaptable interface (Fig. 1). Fuzzy binding is distinct from other interaction types, such as avidity and allovalency, which also involve multiple binding sites but typically imply a more ordered or stable bound state. This dynamic, multi-site interaction allows for fine-tuning of affinity and specificity, enabling IDPs to act as versatile molecular rheostats in cellular networks [46].Fig. 1Schematic Comparison of Globular Protein and Intrinsically Disordered Protein Interactions. ### Biophysical tools to study IDPs
  Studying the unique dynamic behavior of IDPs requires specialized experimental techniques that can capture their inherent conformational heterogeneity and transient structural elements. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy stands as a cornerstone methodology for IDP characterization, offering unparalleled insight into the functional dynamics and interactions of these flexible proteins [123,126,127]. NMR yields ensemble-averaged structural and dynamic parameters at the individual-residue level, allowing researchers to unveil the conformational energy landscape of IDPs and characterize the nature and timescales of both local and long-range dynamics, even within the complex cellular environment [126,128]. This residue-specific perspective is compelling because it captures the heterogeneous conformational populations that IDPs sample, revealing how different regions of the protein engage in transient secondary structures and long-range contacts that may be critical for function [129]. NMR-based titration experiments are instrumental in elucidating the thermodynamics and kinetics of IDP interactions with various other biomolecules, including proteins, DNA, and RNA, providing mechanistic insights into binding mechanisms that would be invisible to static structural approaches [128]. Given the high flexibility of IDPs, specialized NMR methods such as methyl-transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy (methyl-TROSY), relaxation rate measurements, and chemical shift perturbation analysis are employed to assess their dynamics across a wide range of timescales, from high-speed picoseconds-nanoseconds processes reflecting local bond rotations and backbone vibrations to slower seconds-milliseconds events capturing domain-level rearrangements and conformational switching [123,128]. These multiscale NMR measurements collectively provide a comprehensive temporal window into IDP behavior, revealing both the rapid local fluctuations that underpin functional flexibility and the slower cooperative motions that may drive binding or regulatory transitions.

  Complementing NMR spectroscopy, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have become increasingly sophisticated tools for capturing the conformational dynamics and temporal evolution of IDPs at atomic resolution [127]. Rather than simply describing the static properties of molecules, these simulations reveal how IDPs continuously explore their conformational landscape, sampling multiple distinct ensemble states on various timescales and demonstrating the fundamental dynamical nature that makes IDPs functionally distinct from folded proteins [130]. However, MD simulations have revealed substantial differences between various force fields in accurately representing the local structural properties of IDPs, highlighting an ongoing computational challenge in capturing their dynamic ensembles with high fidelity [131,132]. A key insight from atomistic simulations is that polyampholytic IDPs, which are common and characterized by a mixture of oppositely charged residues, typically sample two distinct stable states: compact globule-like conformations and extended self-avoiding walks [133,134]. The interplay between electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged residues and the favorable free energies of hydration for charged residues drives the preference for these competing states, creating a delicate balance [135]. These systems can exhibit sequence-specific temperatures of biostability, where globules and self-avoiding walks coexist, and can undergo switch-like transitions in response to environmental perturbations, implying a sophisticated regulatory capacity encoded directly within amino acid sequences [136]. Furthermore, the cellular environment, which is characterized by molecular crowding, a densely populated milieu containing numerous different macromolecules, nucleic acids, and membranous structures, significantly influences the dynamic behavior and interactions of IDPs in ways that differ markedly from dilute solution conditions [135,137]. This crowded cellular context can alter the conformational preferences and interaction kinetics of IDPs by excluding particular conformational space, promoting specific compacted states, and enhancing promiscuous binding, thereby adding another layer of complexity to their functional regulation [138].

  The synergistic integration of NMR spectroscopy and MD simulations has emerged as a powerful approach for obtaining comprehensive, accurate ensembles of IDP conformational states [127]. Rather than relying on either method in isolation, where NMR provides sparse but reliable experimental data while MD provides atomic detail but suffers from sampling limitations and force field uncertainties, combining both approaches through data reweighting techniques, maximum entropy procedures, and ensemble refinement methods yields conformational ensembles that are more accurate, complete, and force-field independent than either method alone [130]. This integrated methodology has proven particularly valuable for examining challenging IDP systems that contain transient proline isomerization, where slow cis/trans interconversion processes occur on timescales that neither NMR nor conventional MD can readily access independently [139]. By employing enhanced sampling techniques, such as metadynamics, together with experimental NMR data and small-angle X-ray scattering measurements, researchers have successfully characterized proline subensembles and shown that different proline isomeric states can dramatically impact binding specificity and functional outcomes, with some exhibiting thousand-fold differences in binding affinity [136,140]. Similarly, the combination of NMR spectroscopy with MD simulations has illuminated how IDRs transiently engage with membranes through fuzzy, dynamic interactions, in which bound conformations remain highly disordered while maintaining key electrostatic and aromatic contacts with lipid headgroups [141]. The hybrid MELD (Modeling Employing Limited Data) approach represents another powerful integration strategy, wherein NMR chemical shift restraints guide MD simulations to predict not only the bound structures of peptide-protein complexes involving disorder-to-order transitions, but also relative binding affinities, a capability that conventional structural prediction methods often lack [142]. These integrated biophysical approaches have fundamentally transformed our understanding of IDP dynamics by revealing that these proteins do not exist as random, featureless coils, but rather as dynamic ensembles populated with transient secondary structures, long-range contacts, and conformational networks that are highly sequence-specific, environmentally responsive, and functionally relevant [143].

  Despite remarkable methodological advances, significant challenges remain in accurately characterizing IDP conformational landscapes under physiological conditions. Many structural techniques rely on ensemble averaging, potentially obscuring low-population states that may nevertheless be functionally significant. Similarly, most in vitro systems fail to fully recapitulate the crowded intracellular environment, dynamic PTM patterns, and transient interaction networks that strongly influence IDP behavior in living cells. Molecular dynamics simulations remain highly dependent on force-field selection, sampling strategies, and electrostatic modeling, particularly for highly charged or compositionally biased sequences. These limitations underscore the need for integrative experimental frameworks combining residue-level structural information with real-time cellular context, including in-cell spectroscopy, live-cell imaging, cryo-electron tomography, and proteome-scale interaction mapping.

  The comparison between IDPs and globular proteins ultimately suggests that the protein universe is not divided into strictly ordered and disordered classes (Table 2). Instead, it occupies a dynamic continuum whose position is itself biologically regulated. The same protein may shift along this continuum depending on PTM status, binding partners, subcellular localization, molecular crowding, and condensate state. This conditional nature of disorder has significant implications for both biology and therapeutics. Biologically, it implies that assigning proteins into rigid structural categories without considering cellular context may be functionally misleading. ## Disease implications
  IDPs and IDRs are increasingly being recognized for their pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of a broad spectrum of human diseases, notably neurodegenerative disorders and various cancers [[144], [145], [146]]. The dynamic and flexible nature of IDPs, while essential for their normal cellular functions, also renders them susceptible to aberrant conformational changes, leading to misfolding and aggregation that underpin many debilitating conditions [147,148]. The dysregulation of IDPs can result in protein misfolding, misidentification, and mis-signaling, contributing to a diverse array of diseases, viz, neurodegeneration, cancer, cardiovascular disease, renal diseases, and diabetes [146,149,150]. This pervasive involvement underscores the critical need to understand the molecular mechanisms by which IDPs contribute to disease and to explore novel therapeutic strategies targeting these challenging protein species.


### Role in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer
  The involvement of IDPs in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer represents a significant area of biomedical research, as these proteins are frequently implicated in the initiation and progression of both categories of conditions. In neurodegenerative diseases, characterized by the slow, irreversible, and progressive loss of cells within specific regions of the brain and/or spinal cord, many of the misfolded proteins are intrinsically disordered [151,152]. These diseases, often referred to as proteinopathies, are directly linked to the formation and accumulation of protein aggregates in the brain [153,154]. Key examples of IDPs implicated in neurodegenerative disorders include beta-amyloid, alpha-synuclein, and tau in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases, as well as prion protein (PrP), FUS, and TDP-43 in conditions like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal degeneration (FTD) [146,147,[155], [156], [157]].

  The accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a fundamental trigger of ER stress and the unfolded protein response (UPR), a cytoprotective signaling cascade that restores proteostasis by reducing protein synthesis, increasing chaperone expression, and activating protein degradation pathways [158]. However, IDPs play a paradoxical and multifaceted role in this system. While many IDPs function as regulatory hubs and scaffolding proteins essential for normal UPR signaling, their inherent structural flexibility makes them particularly vulnerable to misfolding under stress conditions [145]. AMBRA1, an intrinsically disordered scaffold protein with a unique property of regulating both proliferation and autophagy core machineries, serves as a critical example of how IDP dysfunction directly contributes to proteostasis failure in age-related neurodegenerative diseases [158]. AMBRA1 has recently emerged as a key regulator of mitophagy, the selective autophagic removal of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria, in post-mitotic cells of adult tissues, and defective AMBRA1 activity has been functionally linked to mitophagy alterations observed in age-related degenerative disorders, including muscular dystrophy, sarcopenia, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and age-related macular degeneration. The dysfunction of AMBRA1 and related IDPs under prolonged stress conditions demonstrates how the UPR pathway, when chronically activated or dysregulated, can be propelled toward cellular demise and inflammation rather than adaptive recovery. This transition depends critically on the conformational state and functional availability of multiple disordered regulatory proteins [158,159]. When the adaptive capacity of the UPR is exhausted, a state termed proteostasis collapse, neurons experience a cascade of events, including the accumulation of protein aggregates, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and ultimately neuronal death, making this IDP-dependent transition a central mechanism in neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis.

  Oxidative stress represents another established hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases, contributing to genomic instability and neuronal loss in conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease by disrupting the delicate balance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant systems [160,161]. The Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway stands at the epicenter of cellular antioxidant defense, and both key components, namely Keap1 and Nrf2, contain extensive IDRs or are themselves IDPs, whose structures and functions are exquisitely sensitive to oxidative modification [161]. Under basal conditions, Keap1, itself susceptible to oxidation due to its high cysteine content, binds to Nrf2 and facilitates its ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation through E3 ligase activity, thereby suppressing antioxidant gene expression [162]. However, upon oxidative stress, both Nrf2 and Keap1 are susceptible to protein misfolding and inclusion formation, with the IDRs within Nrf2 and the structural vulnerabilities of Keap1 contributing to their oxidation and subsequent dysfunction [161]. This oxidation-induced misfolding of these critical IDPs impairs cellular antioxidant responses precisely when neurons need them most, creating a pathological amplification loop wherein oxidative damage to IDPs prevents activation of the very defense systems required to counteract oxidative stress [162]. In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, impairment of the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway due to IDP misfolding is particularly detrimental in post-mitotic neurons, which have high metabolic rates and consequent elevated reactive oxygen species production, making them exquisitely dependent on intact antioxidant signaling for survival [163]. Furthermore, the Keap1/Nrf2 system regulates not only classical antioxidant genes but also genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, proteostasis, and selective autophagy, linking IDP-dependent oxidative stress responses to the broader maintenance of neuronal homeostasis across multiple cellular compartments [164].

  The dysfunction of optineurin (OPTN), a multifunctional adaptor protein intricately involved in selective autophagy and mitophagy, exemplifies how defects in IDP-mediated organellar quality control directly drive neurodegeneration [165,166]. Optineurin functions as a selective autophagic receptor through binding of polyubiquitinated cargoes via its ubiquitin-binding domain and coordinates cellular processes associated with the trafficking of membrane-delivered cargo through interactions with diverse proteins, including myosin VI, huntingtin, Rab8, and Tank-binding kinase 1. Critically, mutations in the optineurin gene are associated with familial and sporadic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and, more recently, have been found to correlate with Paget's disease of bone. ALS-associated optineurin mutations result in defects in neuronal vesicular localization, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and secretory pathway function [166]. The selective role of optineurin in mitophagy, the process by which damaged or depolarized mitochondria accumulate autophagy receptors and are selectively engulfed for lysosomal degradation, links optineurin dysfunction to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria that produce excessive reactive oxygen species and accelerate neuronal loss in Parkinson's disease and other mitochondrial-associated neurodegeneration [167]. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are cardinal features of neurodegenerative diseases, and that optineurin deficiency impairs the clearance of damaged organelles, optineurin dysfunction represents a critical nexus in which IDP-mediated organellar quality control failure directly propagates the oxidative stress cascade that drives neuronal death [158]. Moreover, OPTN-mediated selective autophagy operates through phase-separation mechanisms and fuzzy interactions with other IDPs, suggesting that the conformational dynamics and phase behavior of these disordered proteins are essential for their mitophagic function, and that perturbations in these biophysical properties contribute to disease pathogenesis [166].

  The nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, itself regulated by intrinsically disordered regulatory regions and ubiquitin-dependent protein dynamics, represents another central node where IDP dysfunction drives pathological inflammation in neurodegenerative disease [168,169]. NF-κB exists in the cytoplasm as an inactive complex bound to inhibitor of kappa B (IκB) proteins, which contain IDRs that facilitate their interaction with NF-κB dimers and regulate their degradation upon phosphorylation [170]. Upon activation by oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokines, or pathogen-associated molecular patterns, IκB undergoes rapid ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, releasing NF-κB dimers that translocate to the nucleus where they activate transcription of hundreds of genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, and enzymes involved in inflammatory amplification [169]. In neurons, while low-level NF-κB signaling promotes synaptic plasticity and cell survival through activation of protective genes, prolonged or excessive NF-κB activation, characteristic of neurodegenerative disease states, drives transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, that perpetuate neuroinflammation, recruit peripheral immune cells, activate glial cells, and collectively promote neuronal death [171]. Critically, the p65 subunit of NF-κB, which functions as the primary trans-activator of inflammatory genes, contains extensive IDRs that mediate its interactions with co-activators, including the p300 histone acetyltransferase, thereby modulating chromatin accessibility and transcriptional output through post-translational modifications of both the p65 IDP and its binding partners [169]. In the context of Alzheimer's disease, the p300/NF-κB pathway has been shown to promote neuronal pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory cell death, through epigenetic activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, revealing how IDP-mediated transcriptional regulation directly links to programmed inflammatory death pathways [169]. Thus, dysregulation of IDP-dependent NF-κB signaling creates a vicious cycle in which oxidative stress or protein misfolding triggers NF-κB activation, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators that increase ROS production, further damage mitochondria and proteins (including the IDPs that regulate NF-κB itself), and perpetuate chronic inflammation that accelerates neuronal degeneration [172].

  Cysteine cathepsins (B, H, C, and X/Z), a family of papain-like cysteine proteases traditionally associated with lysosomal degradation, have emerged as critical players in neurodegenerative disease when found in extra-lysosomal compartments where they participate in processing of misfolded proteins, regulation of cell death pathways, and modulation of neuroinflammation [173,174]. These proteases possess remarkable specificity for their substrate proteins through recognition of both structured and IDRs, and their dysregulation, whether through aberrant localization, altered expression, or pathological activation, contributes substantially to the accumulation of aggregation-prone proteins in neurodegenerative disease. Notably, IDPs that have undergone oxidative modification, proteolytic cleavage, or pathological aggregation often become resistant to canonical proteasomal and autophagic degradation pathways, making them targets for cathepsin-mediated degradation both inside and outside lysosomal compartments. In Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, dysregulated cysteine cathepsins have been implicated in the generation of neurotoxic protein fragments from amyloid-beta and alpha-synuclein, as well as in the propagation of tau pathology through proteolytic processing of tau protein aggregates. Furthermore, cysteine cathepsins released by dying or degenerating neurons can function as danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate pattern recognition receptors on microglia and other innate immune cells, thereby amplifying neuroinflammatory responses through NF-κB and other signaling pathways [174]. The regulatory interplay between cathepsins and the proteolytic systems that control misfolded IDP clearance reveals how dysregulation of cathepsin activity, whether primary or secondary to dysfunction of upstream quality control systems, becomes a key driver of IDP aggregation and neurodegeneration [175].

  Similar to neurodegeneration, ER stress and the subsequent activation of UPR pathways are implicated in cancer, with increased expression of all three UPR signaling pathways observed in these pathologies, suggesting a common cellular stress response mechanism across diverse diseases [159]. In the context of cancer, IDPs and their associated cellular stress responses acquire additional layers of complexity, as cancer cells develop mechanisms to evade both oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and the protein quality control systems that would usually eliminate cells with catastrophic proteostasis failure [176]. Many oncogenes and tumor suppressors contain extensive IDRs that mediate their function in transcriptional regulation, signaling, and cell cycle control, and dysregulation of these IDPs drives malignant transformation [62,[144], [145], [146], [147]]. For instance, the proto-oncogene c-MYC, a master transcription factor driving proliferation and metabolism in cancer cells, contains a large intrinsically disordered domain that mediates its interaction with multiple cofactors and whose conformational state is sensitive to post-translational modifications that alter its activity and stability [62]. Similarly, the tumor suppressor p53, while containing some structured domains, has significant IDRs, particularly in its transactivation domain, that enable it to function as a "fuzzy" transcription factor capable of adapting its structure upon binding to different co-factors, allowing it to activate distinct gene programs in response to various cellular stresses [62]. Specific IDPs, such as NUPR1, have been identified as important players in pancreatic cancer. At the same time, p53 is associated with over 50% of human cancers, highlighting its critical function as a tumor suppressor when properly regulated [177,178].

  In cancer cells, aberrant NF-κB signaling, often driven by constitutive activation of upstream kinases through mutation or by chronic inflammation in the tumor microenvironment, promotes survival signals that protect cancer cells from apoptosis while simultaneously driving expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that promote tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and immunosuppression [170]. Crucially, the IDP-dependent Keap1/Nrf2 pathway, while protective in normal cells against oxidative stress-induced toxicity, becomes aberrantly activated in many cancers, allowing cancer cells to tolerate and even exploit elevated oxidative stress for their survival and growth [179]. Phase separation mediated by IDRs of transcription factors and chromatin-associated proteins has also emerged as a critical mechanism driving oncogenic transcriptional programs in cancer, wherein aberrant assembly of biomolecular condensates concentrates proto-oncogenes and oncogenic transcription factors at specific genomic loci to drive malignant gene expression [15]. The dysregulation of mitophagy in cancer cells, mediated in part through altered expression or function of optineurin and related IDPs, creates a double-edged sword wherein cancer cells must maintain sufficient mitochondrial function to support their high metabolic demands while simultaneously using selective mitophagy as a mechanism to eliminate mitochondria that would otherwise trigger apoptosis through excessive ROS production [158]. Thus, in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer alike, IDPs serve as central regulatory nodes where perturbations in their structure, localization, post-translational modification, and phase behavior drive pathological cellular stress responses, whether toward uncontrolled inflammation and neuronal death in neurodegeneration, or toward survival and proliferation in malignancy, highlighting the extraordinary versatility and vulnerability of these dynamic proteins in determining cellular fate.

  A major unresolved challenge in interpreting disease-associated IDP dysfunction concerns causality. In many neurodegenerative and oncogenic systems, IDP misfolding, aggregation, or abnormal phase separation strongly correlate with pathology. Yet, it remains unclear whether these alterations represent primary pathogenic drivers or secondary downstream consequences of broader metabolic, mitochondrial, or inflammatory dysfunction. For example, while disruption of the Keap1/Nrf2 axis may result from aberrant IDP conformational changes, oxidative stress generated by primary mitochondrial dysfunction may, in turn, induce subsequent IDP damage, thereby reversing the apparent causal hierarchy. Resolving these relationships will require temporally controlled experimental systems capable of separating initiating conformational perturbations from downstream cellular responses, including inducible phase-separation systems, optogenetic condensate models, and real-time in-cell structural approaches. Although substantial evidence links IDP dysregulation to neurodegeneration and cancer, the causal relationships between altered conformational ensembles, aberrant phase transitions, and disease progression remain incompletely understood. Distinguishing disease-driving mechanisms from secondary pathological consequences remains an important challenge for future studies.


### Aggregation and misfolding
  The aberrant aggregation and misfolding of IDPs are central pathological hallmarks in numerous neurodegenerative diseases and contribute significantly to various other protein dysfunction maladies [149,151,152,180]. Unlike well-ordered proteins that adopt stable three-dimensional structures, IDPs exist as dynamic conformational ensembles, and this inherent flexibility paradoxically makes them prone to misfolding and self-assembly into toxic aggregates or amyloid fibrils [147,148,181,182]. The accumulation of these misfolded or unfolded proteins triggers cellular stress responses, such as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which, if prolonged, can lead to cell death and inflammation [159].

  A critical mechanism underlying pathological aggregation is liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a phenomenon where IDPs or proteins with IDRs can form membrane-less organelles (MLOs) or biomolecular condensates [155,156,181,183,184]. These liquid-like droplets, formed by weak multivalent interactions, create a microenvironment with a very high local protein concentration [184]. While LLPS can serve normal cellular functions, aberrant transitions can occur, leading to a viscoelastic or liquid-to-solid state transition that often nucleates amyloid fibril formation [155,156,183,184]. This process of protein misfolding, oligomerization, and amyloid aggregation is initiated by LLPS of various neurodegeneration-related proteins, suggesting a generic LLPS-mediated multistep nucleation mechanism [184]. For instance, FUS and TDP-43, RNA-binding proteins with substantial IDRs, form ribonucleoprotein and stress granules via LLPS; however, these liquid-like droplets can “age” and transform into less dynamic assemblies, such as hydrogels, inclusions, and amyloid fibrils, which are pathological hallmarks of ALS and FTD [156]. Similarly, tau, a neuronal IDP, can undergo LLPS for its normal functions, but its liquid-to-solid transition and abnormal aggregation are directly associated with neurodegenerative diseases [183].

  Specific IDPs are well-known for their aggregation in neurodegenerative conditions. Alpha-synuclein, an abundant presynaptic brain protein, is a prime example; its misfolding, aggregation, and fibrillation into amyloid fibrils are critical factors in synucleinopathies like Parkinson's disease (PD) and Lewy body dementia [[185], [186], [187], [188]]. Due to mutations or elusive factors, it misfolds into toxic protofibrils, and its aggregation can be influenced by various parameters, including mutations, the biological environment, and interactions with other compounds [185,187,188]. The toxic accumulation of misfolded proteins like alpha-synuclein, tau, and PrP in inclusions, fibrils, or aggregates is a hallmark of many neurodegenerative diseases [147,189,190]. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), extracellular accumulations of amyloid beta (Aβ) in the form of senile plaques and intracellular aggregations of tau in the form of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are characteristic features [178,191,192]. The human prion protein (huPrP), another IDP, undergoes substantial unfolding from its native cellular form (PrPC) to a more stable intermediate (PrPC*), which can then oligomerize into the toxic scrapie form (PrPSc), with weak stabilization of secondary structural elements by tertiary contacts and solvation forces making them susceptible to perturbations [190].

  Cellular chaperone systems are crucial for modulating proteostasis, facilitating proper nascent folding, refolding of misfolded proteins, and their degradation/sequestration [180,189,191]. However, in disease states, chaperones can exhibit both protective and aberrant effects, sometimes even promoting or stabilizing toxic protein aggregates depending on the specific interaction [180]. Heat shock protein 70 kDa (Hsp70) and 90 (Hsp90) are ATP-dependent foldases that actively transition misfolded proteins back to their native conformation, while also possessing ATP-independent holdase functions that prevent aggregation [189]. Small heat shock proteins (sHsps) are another class of chaperones that associate early with misfolded proteins, holding them in a reversible state to facilitate refolding or degradation by other chaperones, and these interactions are ATP-independent [180]. The role of metal ions in altering the aggregation pathways of IDPs in neurodegenerative diseases also provides another layer of complexity to the misfolding process [151]. The continuous aggregation of misfolded proteins in the absence of their proper clearance can lead to amyloid diseases, highlighting the critical imbalance in protein homeostasis [191]. For instance, HSPB1, an sHsp containing intrinsically disordered N- and C-terminal regions, holds misfolded IDPs like tau in sequestered complexes; release of HSPB1's disordered N-terminal region dramatically enhances its chaperone activity toward tau aggregation [193]. The Hsp40 chaperone Ydj1 directly incorporates into tau condensates formed via liquid-liquid phase separation and prevents their transition to pathological amyloid fibrils by blocking access to amyloidogenic motifs [194].

  Additionally, zinc ions accelerate alpha-synuclein aggregation by inducing conformational compaction of the monomeric IDP ensemble through electrostatic bridging of charged residues [195]. At the same time, the chaperone-assisted selective autophagy pathway (CASA), which utilizes HSPB8-Hsp70-BAG3 complexes, specifically targets aggregation-prone IDPs for autophagic clearance [196]. Collectively, these molecular mechanisms, encompassing IDP sequence-encoded aggregation propensity, metal-ion-driven conformational transitions, and chaperone-mediated regulation of IDP phase behavior and clearance, establish a comprehensive framework for understanding how dysregulation of any component of this proteostasis network permits pathological IDP aggregation and amyloid disease progression [197].


### Challenges and potential for drug targeting
  The unique structural characteristics of IDPs present both significant challenges and novel opportunities for drug discovery. Conventionally, drug design has relied heavily on targeting rigid, well-defined three-dimensional protein structures, a paradigm that is inherently difficult to apply to IDPs due to their intrinsic conformational variability and lack of a stable 3D structure [144,147,177,[198], [199], [200]]. This dynamic nature precludes an atomic-level understanding of small molecule binding using traditional structure-based methods, making the development of specific and compelling drugs challenging [198,200]. Furthermore, the study of membrane-less organelles and condensates, which are crucial to understanding IDP-mediated liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and its role in amyloidosis, is still in its infancy, limiting the immediate development of drugs directly targeting these processes for diseases like ALS and FTD [156]. In addition, there is an inherent uncertainty regarding whether preventing aggregate formation by molecular chaperones might inadvertently stabilize deleterious oligomers, potentially increasing overall neurotoxicity rather than mitigating it [189].

  Despite these formidable challenges, the widespread involvement of IDPs in numerous human diseases, including cancer, neurodegeneration, and metabolic disorders, positions them as attractive and essential drug targets [17,146,177,199,201]. Research interest is rapidly growing in therapeutic strategies that specifically target ER stress-related interventions, given the critical role of ER stress and UPR pathways in various IDP-mediated pathologies [159]. One promising avenue involves modulating IDPs to develop new drug design strategies, with some studies already identifying low-molecular-weight inhibitors that interact with these flexible proteins [144,147]. Computational approaches are proving invaluable for overcoming structural challenges, with hierarchical computational strategies for IDP drug virtual screening (IDPDVS) successfully identifying compounds that bind to IDPs such as p53 TAD1 and restore wild-type function in cancer cells [202].

  The proteasome system, which degrades IDPs, is another key target. Small molecule activators of the 20S proteasome, such as fluspirilene analogs, have shown promise in restoring impaired proteasome activity and preventing IDP oligomerization, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases [203]. Specifically, compounds like TCH-165 have been reported to modulate proteasome assembly, thereby enhancing the degradation of aggregation-prone IDPs such as alpha-synuclein and tau without affecting structured proteins [204]. Beyond direct protein targets, modulating key signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB pathway, offers a broad therapeutic approach for both neurodegenerative diseases and cancer, as its inhibition can prevent the development of various pathological conditions [168]. Similarly, a deeper understanding of the functions of cysteine cathepsins, which are involved in both neurodegeneration and cancer, could lead to the development of novel, targeted therapeutic strategies [173].

  Molecular chaperones, including small heat shock proteins (sHsps), which regulate IDP function, are also being explored for therapeutic modulation. Strategies include altering the expression or post-translational modification of endogenous sHsps, developing small molecules that target sHsp domains, and delivering engineered molecular chaperones to manage pathological protein aggregates [180]. Furthermore, ligand-based design approaches have also yielded significant successes. For instance, ZZW-115, a potent NUPR1 inhibitor derived from Trifluoperazine, demonstrated dose-dependent tumor regression in pancreatic cancer models by inducing necroptosis, notably without the neurological side effects of its parent compound [177]. This success highlights the possibility of developing particular drugs for IDPs through rational design.

  Advances in computational methods, particularly long-time-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, are now enabling an atomic-level understanding of small-molecule binding to IDPs, as demonstrated by studies of fasudil binding to alpha-synuclein [198]. These simulations, which can reveal dynamic shuttling mechanisms and predict key structural features, suggest a strong potential for MD-based strategies to facilitate rational drug design for IDPs [198]. The integration of experimental and computational frameworks, enhanced by advanced sampling techniques, efficient computing power, and improved force fields, is crucial for generating reliable conformational ensembles of IDPs and understanding their dynamic interactions, thereby paving the way for quantitative and predictive modeling in therapeutics [199,205]. The overarching concept of D2 (disorder in diseases) further emphasizes the critical role of IDPs as attractive targets for drugs that modulate protein-protein interactions, moving beyond the traditional focus on folded proteins [146,201]. Even specific IDRs, such as the amino-terminal domain (NTD) of the androgen receptor (AR), are validated therapeutic targets for diseases like prostate cancer, underscoring the potential to target these disordered regions directly [200].


## Evolutionary and proteomic insights
  The widespread prevalence and diverse functional repertoire of IDPs offer profound insights into the evolutionary trajectories of life, particularly as biological complexity increases.


### Enrichment in eukaryotes and complex organisms
  The distribution of IDPs across the tree of life reveals a striking pattern: IDPs are highly abundant and notably enriched in the proteomes of eukaryotes and, more specifically, in complex organisms [17,146,147,149]. While IDPs are present in all known proteomes, they are abundant in eukaryotic genomes, comprising approximately 30% of the human genome [50,147,206]. This high prevalence, ranging from yeast to humans, suggests intense evolutionary pressure that favored the retention and expansion of disordered regions [17,146].

  The presence of long stretches of amino acids lacking a well-defined three-dimensional structure is a common feature of eukaryotic proteins [123]. This enrichment in eukaryotes, compared to prokaryotes, is often correlated with the increased complexity of cellular processes and regulatory networks characteristic of higher organisms [146]. In eukaryotes, IDPs are integral to the dynamic control of diverse cellular functions, including signaling, regulation, and transcriptional control, which are more elaborate and finely tuned than in simpler life forms [86,144,181]. The ability of IDPs to engage in one-to-many and many-to-one signaling events, facilitated by their structural flexibility, provides a unique advantage for orchestrating complex cellular decisions and interacting with multiple partners in a highly regulated manner [146]. This suggests that intrinsic disorder is not merely a structural anomaly but an adaptive feature that has been positively selected for during the evolution of biological complexity, enabling the intricate regulatory mechanisms found in multicellular organisms and their specialized cells.


### Adaptive significance of disorder
  The adaptive significance of intrinsic disorder lies in its unique ability to confer functional versatility and efficiency that ordered proteins often cannot achieve. IDPs functionally complement ordered proteins, primarily by regulating crucial cellular processes [146,149,151,152,201]. Their lack of a stable 3D structure is not a defect but rather a fundamental feature that facilitates their diverse functions [178]. This structural flexibility enables IDPs to partake in dynamic regulatory networks that require transient associations and rapid dissociation [146]. The ability to adopt multiple interconverting conformational states allows IDPs to act as molecular hubs, interacting with various proteins, DNA, and RNA and modulating their functions [17,123].

  A key adaptive advantage of IDPs is their capacity to undergo LLPS, which drives the formation of MLOs [86,156,181,183]. These biological condensates play critical roles in the spatio-temporal organization of the cell, orchestrating functions ranging from transcriptional regulation to stress response coordination. Collectively, these features highlight intrinsic disorder as an evolutionarily advantageous trait, enabling proteins to operate at the interface of structural plasticity and functional complexity [207,208]. Moreover, these properties collectively position IDPs as essential regulatory molecules that enable organisms to respond dynamically to environmental changes while maintaining cellular homeostasis, fundamentally expanding the regulatory toolkit available to eukaryotic cells beyond what ordered proteins alone could achieve.


## Artificial intelligence and machine learning for prediction and design of IDPs/IDRs
  The prediction of intrinsic disorder from sequence has matured over the course of four decades, progressing from heuristic and classical machine-learning predictors to contemporary deep-learning and meta-server approaches. A more recent shift, however, is qualitative rather than merely incremental: machine learning is now being marshalled not solely to label individual residues as ordered or disordered, but to predict the functional and biophysical properties of disordered regions, and indeed to design entirely new ones.


### Predicting disorder and its functional context
  Deep-learning predictors and, increasingly, methods predicated upon protein-language-model embeddings have substantially improved both accuracy and computational speed. Metapredict affords fast, proteome-scale consensus predictions of disorder and structure [209]; flDPnn couples accurate disorder prediction with the concomitant prediction of common disorder functions [210]; and embedding-based predictors such as SETH infer continuous, nuanced disorder directly from language-model representations [211]. The per-residue confidence metric (pLDDT) of AlphaFold is likewise widely employed as a proxy for disorder, albeit with important caveats: low confidence may reflect either genuine disorder or, alternatively, a merely difficult-to-predict fold, and AlphaFold returns a single static structure rather than the conformational ensemble that fundamentally defines a disordered region [212].


### Predicting phase-separation propensity
  A dedicated class of predictors now estimates the propensity of a given sequence to undergo LLPS, thereby operationalizing, in a direct manner, the molecular grammar delineated in the preceding section. PScore quantifies the density of planar π–π contacts [34]; FuzDrop estimates a droplet-promoting probability while additionally flagging aggregation hot-spots and the propensity for conversion to the amyloid state [213,214]; and deep-learning methods such as DeePhase learn nonlinear sequence determinants directly from sequence embeddings [215]. Multimodal models such as PhaSePred, in turn, integrate sequence, structure, and additional features so as to distinguish bona fide condensate drivers from clients [216]. While such tools are undeniably valuable for the prioritization of candidates, they are trained upon relatively small, curated positive sets and not infrequently disagree; their outputs are accordingly best regarded as hypotheses rather than definitive predictions [217].


### Predicting the conformational ensemble
  Perhaps the most consequential advance for IDP biology is the transition from predicting disorder per se to predicting the properties of the underlying ensemble. ALBATROSS, trained upon synthetic sequences in conjunction with large-scale coarse-grained simulations, predicts global ensemble dimensions (namely the radius of gyration, the end-to-end distance, the polymer-scaling exponent, and asphericity) directly from sequence and at proteome scale [218]. Complementary proteome-scale coarse-grained simulations, exemplified by the CALVADOS model, have generated conformational ensembles for tens of thousands of human IDRs, thereby linking chain dimensions to cellular function and evolutionary conservation [219]. Collectively, these approaches begin to bridge the long-standing gap between sequence-based disorder prediction and the inherently ensemble nature of disorder, and they furnish quantitative targets against which experimental measurements (i.e., NMR, SAXS, and single-molecule FRET) may be rigorously compared.


### Predicting regulatory motifs
  Machine learning has additionally been brought to bear on the recognition of the functional motifs that punctuate disordered regions, namely short linear motifs, MoRFs, degrons, and post-translational-modification sites [39,40], with disorder-function predictors such as flDPnn explicitly predicting protein-binding and other functional regions embedded within IDRs [210]. Given that E3-ligase degrons and numerous signaling motifs reside within IDRs, accurate motif prediction bears direct relevance to the ubiquitylation and signaling mechanisms discussed earlier.


### From prediction to design
  The very models employed to predict properties are now being inverted so as to design sequences de novo. On the disordered side, tools such as GOOSE generate synthetic IDRs endowed with prescribed biophysical properties (for instance, a target radius of gyration, charge pattern, or composition) while simultaneously controlling for confounding features [220], thereby enabling hypothesis-driven sequence engineering as well as the generation of training data for predictors such as ALBATROSS [218]. On the binder side, the de novo design pipeline comprising RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN, and AlphaFold now reliably yields high-affinity binders to selected targets [221,222], while dedicated systems such as AlphaProteo report correspondingly high experimental success rates [223]; AlphaFold3, for its part, extends accurate structure prediction to encompass protein complexes and their interactions [224]. The design of binders directed against disordered targets is intrinsically more demanding, precisely because the target itself possesses no fixed structure; nonetheless, appreciable progress is being made, as exemplified by designed proteins capable of recognizing extended or helical peptide segments [225], and by diffusion-based methods adapted to design binders directly against intrinsically disordered targets [226]. Such capabilities ultimately feed into the therapeutic strategies discussed elsewhere in this review, including condensate modulators and the rational design of degron-directed degraders (i.e., PROTACs and molecular glues).


## Conclusion and perspectives

### Summary
  The past two decades have witnessed a paradigm shift in protein science, recognizing and appreciating IDPs and their central roles in fundamental biological processes [227]. IDPs fundamentally challenge the classical structure-function relationship that has dominated biochemistry since the mid-20th century, revealing that proteins need not adopt a unique three-dimensional structure to perform sophisticated biological functions [228]. Instead, these proteins exist as dynamic ensembles of interconverting conformations, displaying remarkable structural flexibility that enables them to serve as versatile molecular hubs in cellular regulation, signaling transduction, and gene expression control [229].

  Through our review, several critical insights have emerged regarding IDP biology. First, IDPs and IDRs are not exceptions but rather ubiquitous components of cellular proteomes, particularly enriched in eukaryotic organisms and complex multicellular systems [230]. Second, their disorder-based functionality provides distinct advantages over well-folded proteins, including the capacity for promiscuous protein-protein interactions, enhanced accessibility to post-translational modifications, and rapid adaptation to changing cellular environments [231]. Third, despite their prevalence in normal physiology, dysregulation of IDPs and aberrant LLPS are intimately linked to major human diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cancer, and cardiovascular pathologies [232].


### Computational and experimental challenges
  A significant emerging challenge in IDP research concerns the accurate characterization of their structural ensembles and dynamics. While molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide atomistic insights into IDP behavior, they remain computationally expensive and often struggle to adequately sample rare, functionally relevant conformational states [227]. Recent advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning have begun to address these limitations. Deep learning approaches, including protein language models and diffusion-based generative models, now enable efficient conformational sampling and proteome-wide predictions of intrinsic disorder with unprecedented speed and accuracy [229]. Methods such as IDP-EDL and AI-driven ensemble learning approaches have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional sequence-based predictors, offering new pathways for large-scale IDP characterization [233]. However, significant methodological challenges persist. The integration of disparate experimental techniques, including NMR spectroscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and single-molecule techniques, with computational simulations, remains technically demanding and resource-intensive [234]. Given the dependence of all these techniques on a homogeneously purified protein, IDPs pose an additional challenge, as they are prone to aggregation during bulk purification. Furthermore, the predictive accuracy of current machine learning models depends critically on the quality and representativeness of the training data, underscoring the continued importance of high-quality experimental data for validation [235]. Thus, it is necessary to further optimize, refine, and innovate biophysical and computational tools to understand the biophysical properties and functional landscape of this important class of proteins.


### Drug targeting of IDPs: opportunities and constraints
  One of the most exciting yet challenging frontiers in IDP research involves the development of therapeutics targeting these proteins. Historically considered “undruggable” due to their lack of stable binding pockets and dynamic nature, growing evidence now demonstrates that IDPs can be successfully targeted through rational drug design strategies [236]. Innovative approaches, including molecular glue degraders, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), and conformational ensemble-based screening, have enabled the identification of ligands that modulate IDP function [199]. Additionally, targeting aberrant phase separation by modulating biomolecular condensates represents an emerging therapeutic avenue for diseases in which dysregulated LLPS contributes to pathogenesis [237].

  Nevertheless, translating these insights into clinical therapeutics faces considerable hurdles. The dynamic nature of IDP conformational ensembles makes predicting ligand-binding modes and calculating binding affinities more challenging than for well-structured protein targets [236]. Furthermore, achieving appropriate selectivity among the plethora of IDP-mediated cellular processes while minimizing off-target effects remains an active area of investigation requiring enhanced computational and experimental methodologies.


### Current limitations and open questions in IDP-targeted therapy
  Despite growing interest in IDP-targeted therapeutics, several major limitations continue to constrain translational progress. The conformational ensemble problem remains particularly challenging because predicting which transient state a small molecule preferentially recognizes often requires extensive empirical optimization. In addition, achieving selectivity among compositionally similar IDPs remains difficult using conventional affinity-driven screening approaches. Many clinically important IDP targets, including c-Myc, tau, and α-synuclein, also lack stable ligand-accessible conformations under physiological conditions, complicating conventional structure-guided drug design strategies. Emerging efforts to therapeutically modulate phase separation introduce additional complexity, since condensate-disrupting compounds may unintentionally perturb physiologically important membrane-less organelles. Furthermore, substantial translational gaps persist between simplified biochemical assays and the crowded intracellular environments in which IDPs normally function. Addressing these limitations will require integrated strategies combining in-cell structural biology, quantitative ensemble analysis, condensate imaging, and proteome-wide selectivity profiling.


### Unresolved questions in IDP biology
  Despite major conceptual and technological advances, several central questions in IDP biology remain unresolved. One major challenge involves quantitatively defining conformational ensembles under physiological conditions and distinguishing functionally meaningful transient states from stochastic structural fluctuations. Another unresolved issue concerns the precise role of LLPS in disease progression, particularly whether pathological condensates function as initiating drivers of cellular dysfunction or emerge secondarily during stress adaptation. The increasing use of AI-driven structural prediction has also highlighted the conceptual mismatch between static structure prediction and the inherently ensemble-based nature of IDP behavior. Current computational approaches still struggle to accurately model PTM-dependent disorder transitions, fuzzy complexes, and context-specific conformational shifts. Finally, although IDPs are increasingly viewed as promising therapeutic targets, fundamental questions remain regarding which dynamic interaction surfaces are genuinely druggable within living systems. Resolving these issues will require close integration between computational prediction, experimental biophysics, live-cell imaging, and perturbation-based functional biology.


### Emerging frontiers and future directions
  Several promising directions warrant future investigation in IDP research. First, the continued development of hybrid computational approaches that integrate knowledge-based, physics-based, and machine-learning methods offers substantial potential for improving ensemble prediction accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency [228]. Second, leveraging advances in cryo-EM structure determination and complementary high-resolution techniques to directly visualize IDP conformational ensembles within functional complexes will provide invaluable experimental validation for computational models, thereby facilitating the development of potential therapeutics [238]. Third, exploring the functional roles of post-translational modifications in modulating IDP structure and interactions represents a largely underexplored dimension with significant implications for biological regulation and disease mechanisms [234]. Fourth, expanding our understanding of how IDPs and IDRs contribute to membrane-less organelle formation and phase separation will clarify fundamental principles of cellular organization and provide new therapeutic targets for diseases characterized by aberrant phase transitions [32].

  Finally, enhancing interdisciplinary collaboration between structural biologists, computational scientists, biochemists, and clinicians will be essential for translating IDP-focused discoveries into therapeutically actionable insights. As our knowledge of IDP biology deepens and our technical capabilities expand, IDPs will increasingly shift from being viewed as exceptional oddities to being recognized as central players in cellular physiology and disease, with significant implications for future drug discovery, biomarker development, and our fundamental understanding of protein function in living systems [239]. Reflecting critically on the current state of the field, several major tensions are likely to define progress in IDP biology over the coming decade. First, the rapidly increasing power of AI-driven structural prediction remains fundamentally challenged by the ensemble-based nature of intrinsic disorder, as most current architectures are optimized for predicting a single dominant conformation rather than dynamic conformational landscapes. Second, despite the growing therapeutic urgency surrounding IDP-associated diseases, mechanistic translation from basic biophysical understanding to rational drug design remains comparatively underdeveloped compared with that for folded protein systems. Third, the expanding recognition of physiological IDP functions raises an important conceptual caution against over-attributing pathological significance to every observed conformational change or condensate phenotype. Addressing these tensions will require not only improved experimental methodologies but also more rigorous conceptual frameworks capable of quantitatively defining functional disorder across diverse biological contexts. In addition, significant challenges remain in linking conformational ensembles observed in vitro to functional states operating within living cells. Future progress will require integrative approaches that combine advanced biophysical methods, in-cell structural studies, artificial intelligence, and ensemble-based modeling to capture the context-dependent behavior of IDPs.

  Ultimately, the study of IDPs is reshaping modern molecular biology by demonstrating that biological function can emerge not only from structural stability, but also from regulated conformational heterogeneity, dynamic interaction landscapes, and context-dependent structural plasticity. Rather than representing exceptions to the classical structure–function paradigm, intrinsically disordered proteins are increasingly recognized as a complementary regulatory layer of biological organization that enables adaptability, signaling plasticity, and dynamic control of complex cellular networks.
