# Progress toward linking single-molecule behavior and condensate material properties


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates exhibit distinct material properties that are intimately linked to their functions. A major open question is how the properties of biomolecules shape emergent condensate behaviors. Experimental approaches have provided valuable insights into bulk material properties, yet direct observation of individual molecules within condensates remains challenging. Complementing experiments, molecular simulations have become indispensable in helping bridge scales from molecules to mesoscale assemblies. In this review, we discuss methodological advances in experiments and simulation that elucidate the relationships between molecular properties and condensate behavior and highlight frontiers in probing molecular ensembles within condensates. By integrating single-molecule perspectives with collective material properties, we outline emerging principles that connect microscopic interactions to macroscale condensate properties.


## INTRODUCTION
  Living cells comprise numerous organelles that provide structural coherence and compartmentalize essential biological functions. Beyond membrane-bound organelles, growing attention has focused on membrane-less organelles known as biomolecular condensates. Examples include the nucleolus, stress granules [1], P-bodies [2], Cajal bodies [3], and heterochromatin [4]. These condensates have been predicted to form via phase transitions of biomolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids.

  Recently, significant attention has been dedicated to characterizing material properties of biomolecular condensates, as their functional roles inherently depend on their physicochemical characteristics [5]. Specifically, decoding how molecule-scale changes give rise to condensate behaviors is crucial for understanding phenomena such as viscoelastic response, condensate aging and arrest [6, 7], transcription and translation efficiency [8, 9], and mechanotransduction [10]. The heuristics gleaned will deepen our understanding of how condensates function, why they become aberrant, and how condensates can be leveraged for engineering purposes.

  However, elucidating how molecular-level interactions give rise to emergent condensate behaviors has been a challenge. This difficulty stems from the inherently multiscale nature of condensate biophysics. While experiments can reliably measure bulk properties, they struggle to probe sub-molecular interactions within condensates. In contrast, computational approaches provide molecular level detail but are limited by finite-size effects. Consequently, bridging molecular interactions to emerging macroscale properties in condensates demands approaches that can probe relevant phenomena across multiple interaction scales.

  Recent advances in experimental and computational approaches are helping close this knowledge gap, offering mechanistic insights into how microscopic interactions of proteins and nucleic acids give rise to condensates with diverse material properties and functions. In this perspective, we outline state-of-the-art experimental and computational approaches for measuring bulk thermodynamic and dynamical material properties of condensates. We then discuss key methodological advances that allow us to interrogate single-molecule behavior within condensates. Finally, we highlight strategies that connect macroscopic and single-molecule length scales – uncovering multiscale principles governing condensate form and function.


## MEASUREMENT OF CONDENSATE THERMODYNAMICAL PROPERTIES

### Experimental Approaches
  Measuring condensate thermodynamic properties clarifies their biological functions and helps illuminate how these properties arise. A major goal is to compute the phase diagrams for biomolecular systems as a function of various parameters (e.g. temperature, pH, crowder, salt, etc.) which has shown to regulate phase behavior in native and synthetic systems. This involves determining the dilute phase and dense phase density under various conditions.

  Experimentally, the saturation concentration, Csat, is a key thermodynamic observable, corresponding to the dilute phase concentration at the onset of phase coexistence (the left arm of the binodal). The simplest approaches to measure Csat involve titration experiments that detect this onset point, monitored either by turbidity/light scattering or fluorescence microscopy [11, 12]. Microfluidic platforms can substantially increase throughput: for example, PhaseScan uses combinatorial droplet microfluidics to rapidly generate and screen thousands of distinct compositions, enabling high-resolution phase diagrams to be mapped [13]. It is important to distinguish Csat from the related but conceptually distinct “cloud point,” which refers specifically to the temperature at which phase separation is first observed under a given set of conditions. Cloud points are most reliably identified by tracking the temperature dependence of absorbance curves [11] (Fig. 1a).

  In order to capture complete binodals, more sophisticated in vitro studies often focus on measuring biomolecule Csat under different conditions. Notably, sedimentation analysis(Fig. 1b) can be used to calculate dense and dilute phases to map out phase diagrams [14, 15]. Such experiments can be conducted in a sequence-dependent manner (e.g., mutating select protein residues), thereby providing sequence-dependent phase diagrams and critical parameters. In addition to helping elucidate the role of molecular sequence on condensate stability, these experiments provide invaluable data for benchmarking computational approaches designed to predict phase transitions of proteins [16–18].

  Another important thermodynamic observable to characterize is the free energy cost of sustaining interfaces. Characterizing surface tension gives insight about properties such as multiphasic organization and size control. One of the most common techniques for measuring surface tension is micropipette aspiration (MPA) (Fig. 1c), which is especially advantageous for capturing droplet measurements on a micrometer scale [19, 20]. However, MPA models are limited to condensates behaving as Newtonian fluids – in order to apply the Young–Laplace equation – and many condensates have been shown to exhibit non-Newtonian behaviors [21]. Complementary approaches include the sessile drop method [22], in which interfacial tension is inferred from the gravity-deformed equilibrium shape of a condensate droplet coexisting with its dilute phase. By probing a condensate’s static morphology rather than its fusion kinetics, this techniques allows for surface tension to be extracted without independent viscosity measurements [23]. Nevertheless, researchers also rely on droplet fusion experiments using laser-trap optical tweezers to drive coalescence and measure forces, from which the ratio of surface tension to viscosity can be accessed [24].

  While such experimental measurements provide invaluable macroscopic observables, they are limited in spatial and temporal resolution. Thus, molecular simulations have emerged as complementary, powerful tools for probing condensate thermodynamics at submolecular resolution.


### Simulation Approaches
  Molecular simulations can provide atomistic-resolution insights explaining emergent condensate behaviors. However, high-resolution models incur significant computational costs when modeling the multi-biomolecule systems needed to probe condensation. To overcome these barriers, in recent years, one-bead-per-amino-acid (or nucleic acid) coarse-grained models have emerged as powerful tools to accelerate condensate simulations [16, 18, 25–27], while maintaining sufficient physical fidelity to capture essential thermodynamic behaviors.

  Past computational studies have estimated Csat from dilute-phase concentration, which is useful for model calibration [18]. However, although dilute-phase concentrations are relatively straightforward to determine via in vitro reconstitution, simulations are prone to obscuring such measurements. To address this, new simulation methods have been developed to elucidate biomolecular Csat, such as the use of thermodynamic integration schemes as well as Machine Learning (ML) based predictors trained on coarse-grained simulation data [28, 29]. Recently, Chakravarti et al. devised a new method termed “CLOUD-FIT” (Fig. 1d), which exploits density fluctuations from finite-size effects in direct coexistence simulations to compute absorbance curves and obtain cloud point temperatures [17].

  To date, the most common simulation strategy for phase diagram construction relies on direct coexistence simulations (Fig. 1e) in the slab geometry [25]. In this method, dilute and dense phase densities are directly obtained from biphasic simulations in high-aspect ratio geometries (or slabs) at different conditions. From these simulations, critical parameters can be extracted using the law of rectilinear diameters and the law of coexistence densities [16, 18, 25]. Recent work has proposed alternative approaches, such as Gaussian Cluster Theory, for yielding coexistence curves from simulation data [30]. Overall, computed phase diagrams from simulations using residue-resolution level coarse-grained force fields have been able to achieve near quantitative agreement with experiments. Thus, these models can be used to systematically probe vast regions of phase space beyond what is possible with experiments alone.

  Direct coexistence simulations in slab geometry can also be leveraged to estimate the free energy cost of creating interfaces between coexisting phases (i.e., the surface tension). Here, the pressure tensor gradients at condensate interfaces are translated into quantifiable surface tensions via the Kirkwood-Buff formalism [31]. However, this formalism is limited due to its assumption of sharp interfaces, which falls apart approaching critical points and when condensates are enriched in polar residues. Recent work has addressed this limitation by directly using stress profiles (Fig. 1f) based on per-atom virial stresses and explicitly accounting for variable interfacial thicknesses [32].

  Together, extracting critical and cloud point temperatures, Csat, and surface tensions enable the quantitative assessments of how biomolecule sequence influence condensation propensity and phase stability. Alongside understanding thermodynamical behaviors of condensates, elucidating the dynamical material properties of condensates is critical for decoding their functions, as well as dysfunctions, in living cells.


## MEASUREMENT OF CONDENSATE DYNAMICAL MATERIAL PROPERTIES

### Experimental Approaches
  Perhaps the most widely used techniques to assess condensate dynamicity are fluorescence-based techniques like Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) [33]. Such techniques measure properties related to mobility of molecules (e.g rate or diffusion, fraction of free and trapped particles). However, dyes used in FRAP experiments arguably perturb molecular conformations, sometimes producing misleading assessments such as incomplete recovery times [34]. To overcome these shortcomings, experiments like MPA enable the quantification of viscosity using linear mechanical models [20]. However, MPA cannot characterize condensates undergoing gelation from aging or related to amyloid fibrils formation [35]. This is a critical limitation because many condensates are structurally inhomogeneous and exhibit viscoelastic responses. In fact, these tunable mechanical responses likely affect biological function and motivate diverse experimental probes [6, 36].

  To probe inhomogeneities, the most widely used method remains tracking optically trapped probe particles (typically 0.1–1 μm in diameter), yielding high-precision position and force data [37]. This can be done actively, using optical tweezers [38, 39], which restrains particles to either study their thermal fluctuations, or induce suprathermal forces to measure condensate response (Fig. 2a,b). Bead tracking can also be a passive process [7, 40], used to obtain mean squared displacements and power spectral density analysis, which can then translate into bulk viscosity and a time-dependent shear relaxation modulus, G(t).

  Once G(t) is obtained, a Schwartzian-type Fourier transform [41] of G(t) leads to the frequency-dependent loss or viscous (G”(w)), and storage or elastic (G'(w)) moduli of condensates. Based on whether G”(w) or G'(w) dominates at a given w, the condensate can be described as being dominantly viscous or elastic respectively.

  However, usage of these microrheology techniques requires the consideration of important caveats: usage of small probes may underestimate network elasticity, and large probes can overestimate viscosity. Furthermore, interactions between the probe and medium may cause heightened surface binding or even laser-induced heating, which can bias measurements [42]. These concerns have recently spurred probe-free approaches such as atomic force microscopy coupled with infrared nanospectroscopy (AFM-IR) (Fig. 2c) and other advances in scanning probe microscopy to investigate hydrodynamic drag and interfacial energy changes in condensates under deformation [43].

  In sum, probing condensate mechanics experimentally requires multimodal approaches to link mesoscopic observables to sequence-dependent microstructural dynamics.


### Simulation Approaches
  Complementing experiments, MD simulations help elucidate how molecular-level interactions give rise to distinct dynamical properties of condensates.

  Recently, bead-tracking (BT) simulations (Fig. 2d) have been implemented to probe spatiotemporal inhomogeneities within condensates analogous to experiments [42]. These simulations apply either controlled forces such as harmonic traps, or freely-diffusing probes to perform ‘computational microrheology’. However, improper parametrization of probe sizes and probe-medium interactions can substantially under- or overestimate effective condensate viscosity and viscoelastic response and so care must be taken when implementing these techniques.

  Another simulation analog to active rheology experiments involves non-equilibrium simulations that deform simulation boxes via boundary conditions. The most common boundary conditions are laminar shear rates that can be used to quantify condensate shear-thinning or shear-thickening behaviors, and sinusoidal shear stresses to determine condensate viscoelasticity (Fig. 2e) [44, 45].

  In contrast to the aforementioned simulation efforts, droplet coalescence is an alternative method that has remained relatively underexplored, eluding the mechanisms of coalescence [46]. Only recently have studies based on the simulations of Fused in Sarcoma protein [47], and other investigations of Histone H1–nucleic acid condensates [48] begun to demonstrate that droplet coalescence is a biomolecule-dependent, kinetically controlled process.

  Other key techniques to determine dynamical properties involve calculating the shear relaxation modulus, G(t), via the Green–Kubo formalism [41] to estimate condensate zero-shear viscosities (Fig. 2f) [44]. In this approach the dense-phase is simulated in an isotropic box with periodic boundary conditions and the stress autocorrelation function is computed. Similar to macroscopic rheology, G(t) plateaus indicate persistent material stress, whereas G(t) collapse to a delta function indicates a Newtonian liquid. Software like “REPTATE” [49] can be used to obtain frequency-dependent viscous (G”(w)) and elastic (G' H) moduli directly from G(t).

  From a practical standpoint, standard coarse-grained models designed to study IDP phase behavior often predict condensates as predominantly viscous, due to not fully capturing the mechanical stiffness of these molecules. Recent efforts have augmented coarse-grained force fields to better quantify relaxation timescales within condensates [45, 50].

  Also, it is important to note that coarse-grained molecular dynamics exhibits accelerated dynamics arising from the smoothening of the underlying energy landscape. As a result, dynamical quantities computed for condensates – such as viscosity or diffusion coefficients – will be faster than their true counterparts, and quantitative outputs will require rescaling against atomistic or experimental reference values [51]. That said, coarse-grained simulations remain powerful for qualitatively mapping how condensate dynamics is modulated by molecular sequence.


## CHARACTERIZATION OF SINGLE-MOLECULE PROPERTIES INSIDE CONDENSATES

### Experimental Approaches
  Beyond bulk phase and material property characterization, a key question concerns the behavior of individual molecules, namely proteins and nucleic acids, inside condensates. Such measurements illuminate how single-molecule dynamics cascade into the emergent mesoscale properties of the condensate as a whole. While resolving single-molecule behavior within condensates remains experimentally challenging, significant progress has been made in recent years, as discussed below.

  Single-molecule fluorescence methods have been invaluable in illuminating the kinetics of biomolecules within heterogeneous condensate environments. For example, single-molecule Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (smFRET) has been applied to determine intra- and intermolecular distances, as well as dynamics on nanometer and micro- to millisecond scales (Fig. 3a). Importantly, smFRET requires site-specific labeling and careful control of dye–protein interactions [52, 53]. More recently, photon time series from smFRET has been analyzed with hidden Markov models to extract single-molecule structures and quasi-kinetics [54].

  Complementing smFRET, Fluorescence Correlation Spectroscopy (FCS) measures fluctuations in fluorescence, which can be used to extract single-molecule diffusion coefficients with sub-millisecond resolution (Fig. 3b) [53, 55]. In parallel, super-resolution approaches, including highly inclined and laminated optical sheet microscopy, have also been used to probe single-molecule trajectories within condensates [56].

  Recent advances in solution Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy have enabled characterization of single-molecule structure within condensates (Fig. 3c). NMR is uniquely capable of delivering residue-level information on how proteins structures (e.g., IDPs) are perturbed in condensates [57]. For example, spin relaxation and chemical shifts obtained via NMR perturbation identify residues mediating interactions within condensates, while providing detailed insight into changes in molecular structure and dynamics between dilute and condensed phases [58]. However, NMR on condensates is hindered by peak broadening, high local viscosity, and low sequence complexity, which reduce spectral resolution and complicate resolving site-specific kinetics. Regardless, NMR data (e.g., as stored in the Biological Magnetic Resonance Bank) is useful for discerning site-specific mutational effects, therefore informing coarse-grained force field development and validating simulations [59, 60]. Lastly, relaxation-dispersion NMR and nucleotide-labeling are also useful approaches for measure RNA–RNA base-pair thermodynamics and kinetics inside condensates [61].

  Together, these approaches provide increasingly detailed views of single-molecule conformational dynamics within condensates.


### Simulation Approaches
  In contrast to experiments, single-molecule behavior within condensates is more readily accessible via simulation. Here, the key challenges lie in developing robust (coarse-grained) force fields that can accurately describe the underlying energy landscapes of the constituent molecules and faithfully capture differences in molecular behavior in dilute and dense phases. Once an appropriate force field is identified, single-molecule properties such as radius of gyration, end-to-end distance, persistence lengths, and shape anisotropy are readily extracted from molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 3d) [16, 45, 62]. Intra- and intermodular contact frequencies can also be resolved to identify interaction motifs sustaining condensation in single- or multicomponent condensates [63, 64]. Dynamical properties such as single-molecule diffusion coefficients are also routinely obtained via coarse-grained molecular dynamics by computing single-molecule mean squared displacements (Fig. 3e) or velocity autocorrelation functions.

  While most studies to date have focused on the behavior of disordered biomolecules within condensates, recent work has broadened this scope to examine how structured elements, including folded protein domains and RNA secondary structures, are perturbed upon condensation [27, 65, 66]. As biomolecular conformations are highly sensitive to their chemical environment, understanding how condensates reshape underlying conformational landscapes is central to linking molecular behavior with emergent condensate properties. For example, using the recently developed Mpipi-Helix model, it was demonstrated that condensates modulate the folding landscape of protein a-helices, with distinct condensate environments promoting different forms of kinetic frustration to folding (Fig. 3f) [66].

  As highlighted above, residue-resolution coarse-grained models are suitable for capturing large-scale conformational and dynamical behaviors of biomolecules over longer timescales and larger assemblies [16, 18, 25–27]. However, atomistic simulations are better suited to resolve pico- to nanosecond kinetics important for characterizing local chemical group rearrangements [53]. Atomistic studies of molecular behavior within condensates have been carried out for both protein-only systems [52, 53] and condensates containing nucleic acids such as RNA [67], though such simulations typically focus on model systems given the computational cost involved. For example, a recent study designed peptide based condensates to probe RNA destabilization in condensates [67]. Because atomistic MD produce conformational ensembles resolved at the level of NMR probes, chemical shifts can be back-calculated using machine-learning predictors like LEGOLAS [68], which can be compared directly with measured spectra.

  Together, these simulation approaches are helping provide a detailed molecular picture of how individual biomolecules behave and interact within condensates.


## LINKING SINGLE-MOLECULE BEHAVIOR TO MACROSCOPIC CONDENSATE PROPERTIES
  Current efforts to understand condensate material properties have developed robust scaling relationships between single-molecule features and measurable material properties [69]. These advances provide a powerful foundation for connecting sequence to macroscopic behavior, while also highlighting an important question: how collective interactions at molecular-scales give rise to these properties. Addressing this gap requires integrating the outlined advances – which now enable the concurrent probing of single-molecule and mesoscale properties – to uncover how information across length and time scales gives rise to mechanisms responsible for condensate macroscopic behavior.

  Towards bridging this gap, approaches have begun probing condensate microstructure and microenvironments within condensates, thereby facilitating studies of information propagation within the condensate interior. Indeed, recent experimental advances – such as fluorophore-based tracking (Fig. 4a) – have revealed that even single-component condensates exhibit pronounced architectural and dynamical heterogeneity [70–72]. Further, advances in high-resolution structural methods such as cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) (Fig. 4b) [73] have directly visualized biomolecular organization within condensates, revealing how specific biomolecular features correlate with condensate material properties and, ultimately, biological function [74, 75]. Additionally, recent work has employed small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) to determine condensate substructures – revealing short-range ordering within condensates [76, 77].

  Complementary simulation studies, leveraging graph-theoretical representations, have systematically characterized condensate microstructure [32, 45, 78, 79]. In this framework, proteins (e.g., IDPs) are modeled as nodes connected by transient interaction edges, allowing condensate dynamics and mesoscale organization to be conceptualized as evolving interaction networks (Fig. 4c) [32, 45, 78, 79]. These measurements, alongside other characterizations of microstructure such as entanglement spacing or mesh geometry (Fig. 4d), are being used to derive unified principles that directly connect molecular and condensate scales. For example, single-molecule shape memory and mesh reconfiguration timescales have been shown to rationalize sequence-dependent viscoelasticity through a condensate Deborah number [45], elucidating mechanotransductive roles of condensates.

  As with many of the simulation approaches discussed here, such quantities are typically computed under equilibrium conditions. Recent advances in simulation methodology now permit characterization of single-molecule dynamics and structure within condensates under steady-state non-equilibrium conditions [80]. Specifically, the TRACE method (Fig. 4e) captures molecular behavior inside active condensates that sustain directed fluxes, more closely mirroring the behavior of biologically active condensates such as the nucleolus.

  Taken together, bridging single-molecule behavior with condensate-scale properties provides a powerful framework for uncovering mechanisms that link molecular sequence to emergent material behavior. By resolving how molecular interactions organize into mesoscale interaction networks and microstructures, these approaches establish the missing connection between sequence-encoded interaction motifs and macroscopic condensate properties. Continued advances in simulations, modeling, and experimentation are refining our view of how sequence features encode material and functional diversity via mechanistic descriptions. This multiscale perspective reveals the physical principles governing condensates in cells and enables the rational design of synthetic condensates with programmable mechanics, composition, and responsiveness.
