# Pathological Mutation
E46K Acts as an Electrostatic
Switch to Increase Distant Interdomain Interactions and Promote α‑Synuclein
Phase Separation


## Abstract
  The Parkinson’s disease-related protein α-synuclein can form solid amyloid fibrils through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) and liquid-to-solid phase transition. The most deleterious familial mutation E46K has recently been shown to enhance α-synuclein LLPS and subsequent solidification; yet, the precise mechanisms remain largely unknown. Here, using molecular dynamics simulations at different spatiotemporal scales combined with biochemical experiments, we show that the E46K mutation acts as an electrostatic switch to remodel interactions between the oppositely charged N-terminal domain (NTD) and C-terminal domain (CTD) of α-synuclein. This remodeling shifts the interaction site of NTD with CTD from its N-terminus to the mutation region. Such reorganization increases the hydrophobic solvent exposure of key LLPS-promoting motifs, potentially facilitating intermolecular interactions that initiate phase separation. Phase coexistence simulations further support this hypothesis, showing that the E46K mutant exhibits enhanced LLPS and solidification propensities. This enhancement is primarily driven by intermolecular electrostatic interactions between the mutation region and the CTD, followed by the hydrophobic NAC–NAC interactions mediated by the LLPS-promoting motifs. Collectively, these results reveal that NTD–CTD electrostatic crosstalk acts as the key modulator of α-synuclein phase separation, while NAC–NAC interactions play an auxiliary role, both of which synergistically govern α-synuclein phase separation. This study offers a complete and detailed mechanistic framework for understanding α-synuclein phase separation and its enhancement induced by the E46K mutation.


## Introduction
  Biomolecular condensates formed through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) are crucial for a variety of cellular functions, including the spatial organization of biochemical reactions, regulation of gene transcription, signal transduction, and stress adaptation. −     Under stress or pathological conditions, liquid biomolecular condensates can congeal over time via liquid-to-solid phase transition (LSPT), forming solid cytotoxic amyloid fibrils that are linked to various neurodegenerative disorders and cancers. In recent years, a growing number of proteins have been identified to exhibit LLPS and LSPT capabilities, many of which are intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), including α-synuclein, Tau, FUS, hnRNPA1 and TDP-43.

  α-Synuclein (α-syn) is a prototypical IDP that is highly abundant in the nervous system. This protein has attracted increasing attention due to its close association with Parkinson’s disease (PD)the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder and a leading cause of death in the elderly worldwide. The pathological aggregation of α-syn can lead to the formation of amyloid fibrils, which are the main component of Lewy bodiesthe hallmark of PD and other synucleinopathies, including dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and multiple system atrophy (MSA). −   The α-syn protein comprises an amphipathic N-terminal domain (NTD, residues 1–60) harboring the majority of known familial mutations, a hydrophobic non-amyloid-β component (NAC, residues 61–95) essential for fibrillogenesis, and an acidic C-terminal domain (CTD, residues 96–140). Various point mutations, including A30P, E46K, H50Q, G51D, and A53T, have been identified in patients with familial forms of PD and DLB, and these variants have been shown to affect α-syn aggregation to different extents and accelerate pathological processes. Among these mutations, E46K exhibits the strongest enhancement of α-syn LLPS, A53T shows a moderate promoting effect, whereas A30P and H50Q have minimal influence on the LLPS propensity of α-syn. Importantly, it has been demonstrated that the charge-reversal mutation E46K not only enhances α-syn fibrillization but also promotes α-syn LLPS and the pathogenic solidification of the resulting condensates, thus rendering this variant more toxic than most other mutations and elevating the risk of neurodegeneration. Elucidating the pathological mechanism of the E46K variant is essential for advancing our understanding of the pathogenic basis of synucleinopathies and may guide the rational design of targeted therapeutics for these disorders.

  As amyloid fibrils are the pathological hallmark of PD, extensive experimental studies have focused on the atomic-level structures of α-syn fibrils and explored how they are altered by the familial mutations, such as the E46K mutation. −     Cryo-EM studies have shown that wild-type (WT) α-syn fibrils are polymorphic and share a conserved cross-β core dominated mainly by the NAC domain and part of the NTD. −   The E46K mutation leads to the formation of a more twisted protofibril and fibrils with a distinct interprotofibril β-sheet arrangement. ,  To unveil the molecular mechanisms underlying α-syn fibrillization, an increasing number of experimental and computational studies have also paid attention to low-molecular-weight α-syn species, including monomers, dimers, and oligomers. For instance, spectroscopic experiments including solution nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and atomic force microscopy have shown that α-syn exists largely in a disordered state in solution, −   whereas β-structured conformations increase dramatically under α-syn aggregation-promoting conditions. A substantial body of experimental work further demonstrates that α-syn monomers can self-associate to form oligomeric species that differ in structure, stability, and cytotoxicity. −    These oligomers generally possess a degree of β-sheet structure lying between the largely disordered monomer and the fully ordered amyloid fibrils. Different oligomeric forms can interconvert and ultimately drive the formation of pathological fibrils. Inspired by these experimental findings, computationalists have investigated the conformational ensembles of monomeric and oligomeric states of α-syn using all-atom and coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, providing atomistic/molecular insights into the conformational properties of low-molecular-weight α-syn species. −

  To uncover the driving forces that dictate protein phase separation, theoretical and computational methods have been developed in recent years and applied to explore the phase behavior of different IDPs. For instance, simulations based on mean field theory and random phase approximation have been utilized to characterize the effects of charge distribution on the phase separation of low-complexity domains of proteins. Phase-coexistence MD simulations, combined with one-bead-per-residue protein models, have been used to explore the phase behavior of IDRs/IDPs and the effects of microenvironmental factors (e.g., ionic strength and temperature). −    Simulations using a hybrid-resolution protein force field have been applied to the TDP-43 low-complexity domain, indicating a link between helical secondary structure propensity and LLPS capability. Multiple computational studies revealed a strong correlation between single-chain conformational properties and the phase behavior of IDPs. −    All-atom replica exchange molecular dynamics (REMD) simulations on the microtubule-binding domain of Tau protein (known as K18) monomer suggested that the LLPS of Tau K18 is encoded at the monomeric level, providing atomistic insight into the mechanisms governing K18 phase separation. Recently, we dissected the molecular determinants governing the phase separation of WT α-syn by using a multiscale MD simulation strategy. We identified seven β-sheet-rich motifs that form an interconnected network via persistent and transient H-bonding interactions crucial for α-syn phase separation. In spite of extensive studies, little is known about how the E46K mutation promotes α-syn LLPS and condensate solidification.

  In this study, we employ all-atom and coarse-grained simulations in combination with experiments to elucidate the precise molecular mechanism underlying the enhancement of α-syn phase separation due to the E46K mutation. All-atom simulations were performed on single α-syn chains with the aim of revealing the impact of the E46K mutation on its intrinsic conformational and physical properties. Our simulations show that the E46K mutation shifts the intramolecular interaction site of the NTD with CTD from its N-terminus to the mutation-site region (Mut-region). This alteration increases the hydrophobic solvent exposure of two key motifs: the aggregation-promoting motif in the NTD N-terminus and the LLPS-promoting motif in the NAC tail. Such changes in interactions and increased hydrophobic solvent exposure would facilitate the intermolecular association and ultimately phase separation of α-syn. This hypothesis is further supported by phase-coexistence simulations. We found that, upon phase separation, the E46K mutation strengthens interchain electrostatic interactions between the mutation-site region within the NTD and the CTD, along with hydrophobic interactions mediated by the LLPS-promoting motif in the NAC tail, thus promoting α-syn LLPS and condensate solidification. These results provide significant molecular-level insight into how the E46K mutation enhances α-syn phase separation and underscore the key roles of the Mut-region–CTD electrostatic crosstalk and the hydrophobic NAC–NAC interactions in driving this enhancement.


## Results and Discussion

### The E46K Mutation Increases the Conformational Compactness of
the α-syn Monomer
  We first evaluated the impact of the E46K mutation on the conformational properties of the α-syn monomer. All-atom REMD simulations were conducted for both WT α-syn and its E46K variant using 60 replicas spanning a temperature range of 300–400 K. The initial conformations of α-syn for the WT simulations were taken from our recent computational study, while those for the E46K simulations were generated by side chain replacement using PyMOL (Figure  b). The N-terminus of α-syn was acetylated, and the C-terminus was modeled in its deprotonated form (COO–) to mimic the physiological state of α-syn in cells. The 60 initial WT α-syn conformations used in the REMD simulations are provided in Figure S1. The sampling efficiency of our REMD simulations was confirmed by the nearly uniform contributions of all 60 replicas to each of the five representative temperature trajectories (Figure S2), along with the spanning of the full temperature range of each of the five representative replicas (Figure S3). Our convergence check shows that the simulations are reasonably converged after t = 100 ns (Figure S4). Therefore, the initial 100 ns data of each simulation were discarded as equilibration, and the remaining 400 ns data were used for further analysis.

  By conducting clustering analysis on WT and E46K α-syn conformations with a backbone root-mean-square deviation (rmsd) cutoff of 0.4 nm, we found that the E46K mutation noticeably increases the compaction of the α-syn monomer, and results in redistributed and elongated β-strands compared to WT (Figure  c,d), as seen from the central conformations of the top four most populated clusters and their respective populations (Figure  c,d). To quantify these differences, we calculated the potential of mean force (PMF) for both the WT and E46K α-syn monomer using radius of gyration (R g) and β-structure propensity as order parameters (Figure  e,f). Two deep energy basins are observed in the PMF of the WT system, centered at (R g, β probability) values of (1.51 nm, 0.07) and (1.52 nm, 0.16). These two basins also exist in the PMF of the E46K monomer and have a higher population than their counterparts in WT. Moreover, the distribution of E46K conformations on the PMF is markedly shifted to small R g value regions compared to WT, showing a clear increase of conformations with small R g values (<1.44 nm) and a reduction of conformations with large R g values (>1.58 nm), as indicated by the dashed squares (Figure  e,f). The increased conformational compactness of the E46K variant is also evidenced by the shift of solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) toward smaller values (Figure S5). Coarse-grained hydropathy scale (HPS) simulations on α-syn monomers also demonstrated the more compact conformations of the E46K variant (Figure S6). Our simulation results are consistent with previous NMR experiments showing that the E46K variant exists in solution in a more compact form than the WT protein. ,  We also evaluated the impact of the E46K mutation on the secondary structure preferences of monomeric α-syn. Our calculations show that coil conformations are predominant in both WT and E46K α-syn monomers (with a coil propensity of ∼80%, as shown in Figure S7), which aligns well with previous CD analysis showing a similar random coil secondary structure for both WT α-syn and its E46K variant in aqueous solution. Moreover, we find that the content of each dominant secondary structure (including coil, β-strand and helix) is largely similar between WT and E46K α-syn monomers (Figure S7), while the β-strands in the E46K variant are longer than those in the WT protein (Figure S8). This increase in β-strand length indicates enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions in the E46K mutant, which may contribute to the formation of percolated interaction networks responsible for α-syn phase separation.

  Previous studies have demonstrated that there is a close correlation between single-chain compaction and phase separation propensity for proteins with low sequence complexity. −   Therefore, the increased compaction of the E46K α-syn monomer observed in our simulations suggests that this variant may have a stronger LLPS propensity than the WT protein. In addition, given that cross-β sheets are a common structural feature of amyloid fibrils, the observed elongation of β-strands in the E46K α-syn suggests an increased fibrillization propensity compared to WT, providing a molecular-level explanation for the previous experimental finding that the E46K variant exhibits an enhanced fibrillization capacity.


### The E46K Mutation Alters the Local β-Structure Propensity
of Segments Crucial for α-syn Phase Separation and Their Associated
Physical Interactions
  To further explore whether the E46K mutation changes the local β-structure distribution of α-syn monomer, we calculated the β-structure probability of each residue and of regions/motifs crucial for α-syn LLPS and aggregation (Figures  a,d and S9). Notably, for WT α-syn, the NAC region exhibited a relatively high propensity for β-structure formation, consistent with several previous simulation studies on WT α-syn. −   We first focus on the PreNAC (residues 45–57, see Supporting Information for definition) and NACore (residues 68–78) segments, two regions that were previously shown to play critical roles in the aggregation of WT α-syn. ,  Our analysis reveals that the E46K mutation increases the β-propensity of the NACore region while decreasing the β-propensity of the PreNAC region (Figure  b). Intriguingly, this increase/decrease in β-propensity of NACore/PreNAC upon E46K mutation is also observed in the N-terminally acetylated E46K α-syn fibril structure (PDB ID: 6L4S) compared to the WT α-syn fibril (PDB ID: 6A6B) (Figure S10a,b). Furthermore, the structural comparison of the fibrils reveals that the fibril interface in WT is primarily composed of β-sheets formed by the PreNAC region, whereas in the E46K fibril it is instead formed by β-sheets of the NACore region (Figure S10c,d). These findings suggest that the effects of E46K on the local β-structure distribution of α-syn monomer are closely mirrored in the fibrillar state, implying that the structural characteristics of α-syn fibrils are encoded at the monomeric level.

  We then centered on the seven heptapeptide segments with high β-propensity identified for WT α-syn in our recent study, namely, 4FMKGLSK10 (β1), 26VAEAAGK32 (β2), 36GVLYVGS42 (β3), 61EQVTNVG67 (β4), 75TAVAQKT81 (β5), 88IAAATGF94 (β6), and 113NEEGAPQ119 (β7). These motifs provide both persistent interactions (β1–β5 motifs, namely the aggregation-promoting motifs) and transient interactions (β6/β7 motifs, namely the LLPS-promoting motifs) for the formation of a percolated network that drives the condensation of WT α-syn and the subsequent presolidification. As seen in Figures  c and S9, these seven motifs also exhibit relatively high β-propensity in the E46K α-syn monomer, but their β-structure probabilities differ from those in the WT protein. The E46K mutation decreases the β-structure probability of those motifs located in the NTD and CTD domains (β1, β2, β3, and β7) (Figure  d), while notably increasing the β-structure probability of motifs within the NAC domain (β5 and β6) (Figure  d). Strikingly, the enhanced β-propensity of the β5 and β6 motifs is also observed in the cryo-EM-resolved structures of WT and E46K α-syn fibrils (Figure S11). Representative snapshots of WT and E46K monomers in Figure  e,f further illustrate the spatial distribution of these β-rich motifs, highlighting that in the E46K variant, the NAC domain forms more β-sheet structures.

  Previous studies have reported that the phase separation of IDPs is driven by multivalent physical interactions, including electrostatic, polar, hydrogen-bonding (H-bonding), aromatic (π–π and cation–π), and hydrophobic interactions. ,  To explore whether the E46K mutation impacts these physical interactions involving the aforementioned seven β-sheet-rich motifs in the α-syn protein, we calculated the polar contact number, mainchain (MC) H-bond number, π–π/cation–π stacking number, and hydrophobic solvent-accessible surface area (HP SASA) for each motif in both WT and E46K monomers. It can be seen from Figure  g that the E46K mutation enhances polar contacts involving β1, β2, and β6 motifs, while reducing those contacts involving the other four motifs. In terms of MC H-bonding and π–π/cation–π interactions, only the β6 motif exhibits an increase, whereas the other six motifs show either diminished interactions or minimal changes (Figure  h,i). These results demonstrate that all types of physical interactions involving the LLPS-promoting motif β6 are strengthened in the E46K variant. This finding suggests that the β6 motif may play a pivotal role in enhancing the intermolecular interactions that facilitate α-syn LLPS. This is further supported by the higher and increased HP SASA of the β6 motif in the E46K mutant (Figure  j), as increased hydrophobic exposure is conducive to intermolecular interactions. In addition, the β1 motif in the N-terminal region also displays an increased hydrophobic exposure in the E46K mutant. This observation, together with recent studies showing that this N-terminal motif is critical for α-syn aggregation and subsequent toxicity, ,  indicates that the elevated hydrophobic exposure of the β1 motif may predispose the E46K mutant to aggregate and fibrillize. In short, the increased β-propensity of the β6 motif and the strengthened physical interactions involving β6, together with the elevated hydrophobic exposure of the β1 and β6 motifs observed in the E46K mutant, suggest that these two motifs could play crucial roles in the E46K-induced enhancement of α-syn LLPS and LSPT observed in recent experiments. This speculation was validated through coarse-grained phase-coexistence simulations of WT and E46K mutant α-syn.


### The E46K Mutation Remodels the Intramolecular Interaction Network
of α-syn
  We performed interaction analyses to elucidate how the E46K mutation enhances the local β-structure propensities of the β-sheet-rich motifs crucial for α-syn LLPS and aggregation and alters the related intramolecular interactions. In the WT protein, several pairs of β-sheet-rich motifs, including β1–β7, β3–β4, and β4–β6 (Figure S12), display the highest contact numbers, underscoring their central role in forming intramolecular interactions. Relatively higher contact numbers are also observed between these motifs and the intervening regions, as well as among the intervening regions themselves (Figure S12), indicating the non-negligible contributions of the intervening regions to the overall contact network. The intervening regions include residues 11–25 between β1 and β2 (denoted as the N-head), residues 45–57 between β3 and β4 (PreNAC), residues 96–112 between β6 and β7 (PostNAC), and residues 120–140 following β7 (the C-terminal tail) (Figure S13).

  In the E46K mutant of α-syn, the contact patterns for β-rich motifs and the intervening regions are altered compared to those in the WT protein (Figures  a and S14). To further reveal how the E-to-K substitution at residue 46 enhances intramolecular interactions involving the β6 motif (Figure  a), we calculated the difference between the residue–residue contact map of E46K and that of WT α-syn. It is found that the E46K mutation reduces local interactions near the mutation site (highlighted by Box I) and increases contacts between its neighboring segments and the CTD (see Box II). These enhanced interactions compete with the native long-range interactions between the CTD and the N-terminal region (see Box III) in WT α-syn, thereby releasing the N-terminal region to form strong contacts with the motifs within the NAC domain, particularly the β5 and β6 motifs (see Box IV).

  To illustrate the E46K-induced alteration in the intramolecular interaction patterns of the α-syn monomer, we constructed a graph representation of the interaction network based on the residue–residue contact map. The β-sheet-rich motifs and their intervening regions were grouped into six contiguous sequence segments according to their corresponding structural domains, and each group was defined as a node in the interaction graph. The thickness of the edges connecting two nodes reflects the total number of atomic contacts between the corresponding regions (see Methods in Supporting Information for details). The resulting interaction graph of the α-syn monomer contains three major nodes, denoted as the N-terminal region (N-ter), the mutation-site region (Mut-region), and the CTD (Figure  b,c). The primary components of these nodes are depicted in Figure  b. In addition, the β4, β5, and β6 motifs are separately included in distinct nodes. In the WT interaction graph, the edge connecting the CTD to the N-ter region within the NTD is significantly thicker than the other edges, followed by the edge between the CTD and the Mut-region, showing the important contribution of long-range NTD–CTD interdomain interactions. This result is consistent with previous experimental and simulation studies showing that long-range interdomain interactions between the NTD and CTD modulate the conformational properties of α-syn. ,  We assessed the effect of the E46K mutation on intramolecular contacts by a difference interaction graph representation (Figure  c). Notably, the E46K mutation reduces local contacts within the Mut-region itself and interactions between the N-ter and the CTD, while significantly enhancing interactions between the Mut-region and the CTD (Figure  c). Additionally, the N-ter node forms markedly thicker edges with the β5/β6 nodes, indicating increased interactions between the N-ter region and the β5/β6 motifs within the NAC domain. These interaction changes can be clearly seen from the representative snapshots in Figure  d.

  To determine which types of physical interactions dominate the E46K-induced changes in intramolecular interaction patterns, we computed the differences in the numbers of electrostatic, polar, and hydrophobic contacts, as well as mainchain hydrogen bonds for residue pairs between the E46K and WT proteins (Figures S15–S18). Our calculations suggest that the electrostatic interactions make the most significant contribution to the increased contacts between the Mut-region and CTD, followed by polar and hydrophobic interactions (Figure S19a–c). The elevated electrostatic attraction is attributed to the increase of the local net positive charge of the Mut-region (Figure S20a). The enhanced N-ter–β6 contacts mainly result from hydrophobic interactions, with a lower contribution from mainchain H-bonding interactions (Figure S19b–d), likely due to the highly hydrophobic nature of β6 and the presence of hydrophobic patches (such as β1 and β2 motifs) within the N-terminal region (Figure S20b). Conversely, hydrophobic interactions between β6 and the Mut-region are reduced, leading to an increase in the hydrophobic SASA of β6. Representative local structural snapshots further illustrate these altered interaction patterns between key segment pairs (Figure S19e,f). These findings highlight the critical roles of electrostatic forces in promoting Mut-region–CTD intramolecular contacts and hydrophobic interactions in mediating β6-involved interactions.


### CG MD Simulations Demonstrate the Enhancement of α-syn
Phase Separation and Condensate Solidification by the E46K Mutation
  We then conducted phase-coexistence simulations for both WT and E46K α-syn to assess the impact of the E46K mutation on α-syn phase separation. The slab geometry simulations were performed in combination with the coarse-grained HPS force field, which has been successfully used in dissecting the phase behavior of various IDPs and the underlying driving forces. −   Recent computational studies show that the HPS model can predict how charge-altering mutations affect phase separation propensity, especially for IDPs lacking stable secondary structures. −   For IDPs whose phase behavior strongly depends on specific secondary structures (such as TDP-43, whose phase separation is governed by helical structures), incorporating secondary-structure information into the HPS model may be required to accurately predict their phase separation propensity. The initial coarse-grained conformations of α-syn molecules used in the phase-coexistence simulations were obtained by mapping α-syn conformations sampled from our all-atom REMD simulations, thereby preserving similar R g values across different resolutions (Figure  a).

  For both WT and E46K α-syn, we conducted simulations at six temperatures ranging from 290 to 340 K starting from a preformed high-density phase (Figure  b,c). In both systems, the coexistence of dense and dilute phases is observed at lower temperatures (290–320 K for WT, and 290–330 K for E46K). As the temperature increases, the dense phase gradually dissociates and ultimately evolves into a dispersed phase. At each temperature where phase separation occurs, the E46K mutant α-syn consistently exhibits increased density of the dense phase and decreased density of the dilute phase compared to WT α-syn (Figure  d,e). By fitting to the critical equation for phase separation (see Supporting Information Methods for details), we find that the E46K variant has a higher upper critical solution temperature (UCST) than WT (331.6 K for E46K and 326.9 K for WT) (Figure  f). These results demonstrate that the E46K mutation enhances the phase separation propensity of α-syn, which is in good agreement with recent experimental observations.

  We then characterized the material properties of WT and E46K mutant α-syn condensates. For this purpose, we performed MD simulations on the condensates using a cubic box at the physiological temperature. The material properties were calculated using several parameters, including surface tension and zero-shear viscosity (η0) of the condensates, the mean square displacement (MSD) and the diffusion coefficient (D) of the protein molecules. Our calculations show that the E46K condensates exhibit higher surface tension than the WT condensates (Figure  g), suggesting a greater propensity for forming large droplets. The zero-shear viscosity of the E46K condensate is almost 3-fold larger than that of the WT condensate (Figure  h), indicating a more solid-like viscoelastic behavior. Meanwhile, the E46K molecules within the condensates show smaller MSD and diffusion coefficient values than the WT molecules (Figure  i,j), reflecting their reduced molecular mobility. Together, these results reveal that the E46K mutant α-syn tends to form larger, more solid-like condensates than WT α-syn, and provide molecular-level evidence for experimental observations that the E46K mutation accelerates the LSPT of α-syn condensates.


### CTD-Driven Electrostatic Interactions and β6-Mediated
NAC–NAC Interactions Synergistically Govern α-syn Phase
Separation and Its Enhancement by the E46K Mutation
  Interaction analysis was then performed to elucidate the physical interactions underlying the enhanced phase separation capability and condensate solidity of α-syn induced by the E46K mutation. Our calculations show that the E46K mutation markedly increases the number of interchain contacts (Figure  a), while decreasing intrachain contacts (Figure  b). The enhanced interchain interactions and reduced intrachain contacts lead to more extended conformations of E46K α-syn, as seen from the increased R g of E46K molecules within the condensate compared to WT α-syn (Figure  c). We categorized all residues into five different types (charged, polar, hydrophobic, aromatic, and other residues, denoted respectively as C, P, H, A, and O, see Supporting Information Methods for detailed definition), and quantified their contributions to the enhancement of interchain contacts. It was found that the E46K mutation increases interchain contacts across all residue types, with charged and hydrophobic residues showing the most pronounced contribution (Figure  d). Together, these findings highlight that electrostatic and hydrophobic residues act as the primary drivers of the enhanced LLPS and LSPT observed in the E46K system.

  To identify the key regions underlying the enhanced LLPS and condensate solidification of the E46K mutant α-syn, we calculated the interchain contact numbers for each residue pair (Figure  e,f) and for each pair of regions defined in Figure  a (Figure  g,h), as well as the differences between the E46K mutant and WT systems (Figures S21 and S22). For both systems, the number of contacts between the NTD and the CTD (especially residues 104–140) was substantially higher than that for the other regions, consistent with previous NMR experiments detecting such interchain NTD–CTD interactions in the early stages of α-syn aggregation. In the WT α-syn system, the highest intermolecular contact numbers are observed between the N-terminal region of the NTD and the CTD (Figure  e,g), whereas they are seen between the Mut-region and the CTD in the E46K mutant system (Figure  f,h). Moreover, a differential interchain contact map (E46K minus WT) shows that contacts across most residue pairs are enhanced by the E46K mutation (Figure S21), and the most strongly enhanced contacts are those between the Mut-region and residues 104–140 within the CTD, followed by NAC–NAC contacts involving the β6 motif (β6–β6 and β6–β5) (Figure S22). This finding is supported by previous NMR experiments showing that the E46K mutant α-syn exhibits the most pronounced chemical shift changes for residues 110–137 during the early stages of aggregation. As seen from Figure S23, the increased Mut-region–CTD interactions primarily arise from enhanced electrostatic interactions driven by charged residues (Figure S23a), whereas the strengthened interactions involving the β6 motif are mainly attributed to intensified hydrophobic interactions (Figure S23b). These results suggest that the E46K mutation acts as an electrostatic switch to remodel the interaction modes between the oppositely charged NTD and the CTD, shifting the interaction site of the NTD with the CTD from its N-terminal region to the Mut-region. The electrostatic interactions of the CTD with the different regions of the NTD serve as the key modulator for α-syn phase separation, while β6-mediated NAC–NAC interactions play an auxiliary role in this process.


### Biochemical Experiments Corroborate the Critical Role of the
Negatively Charged C-Terminal Region in α-syn Phase Separation
  To further validate our computational findings, we performed turbidity assays and fluorescence imaging experiments comparing the phase separation of WT and E46K α-syn, as well as a C-terminal truncation variant lacking residues 104–140 (α-syn1–103). Turbidity measurements showed that WT α-syn displayed an increase in turbidity at PEG concentrations above 14%, whereas E46K α-syn exhibited a similar increase at PEG concentrations above 10%, suggesting that the E46K mutation enhances the phase separation propensity of α-syn. In contrast, α-syn1–103 exhibited extremely low turbidity signals across all tested PEG concentrations, markedly lower than those of both WT and E46K α-syn (Figure  a–c), indicating a greatly diminished phase separation capability. Consistent with these results, fluorescence imaging revealed that WT and E46K α-syn formed large numbers of well-defined droplets, with droplet size increasing at PEG concentrations above 14% and 10%, respectively, whereas the α-syn1–103 variant produced almost no detectable droplets under the same conditions (Figure  d). Together, these results demonstrate that deletion of residues 104–140 within the CTD nearly abolishes the phase separation of α-syn, indicating that the CTD is indispensable for the phase behavior of α-syn and thereby corroborating our simulation results.

  Recent experimental studies have shown that, under near-physiological conditions, the LLPS propensity of α-syn increases with increasing salt concentration. −   Our simulations were conducted at 150 mM NaCl. Compared to lower-salt concentrations, our simulation conditions more effectively screen the negative charges within the CTD, thereby weakening CTD–CTD electrostatic repulsion and facilitating intermolecular interactions. This further underscores the pivotal role of the negatively charged C-terminal region in regulating α-syn phase separation.

  We further performed fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments to examine the effect of the E46K mutation on the liquidity of α-syn droplets. Our results showed that E46K α-syn condensates exhibited a markedly prolonged post-bleaching recovery time, almost an order of magnitude longer than that of WT α-syn condensates (Figure  e,f), indicating diminished molecular mobility of E46K α-syn within the droplets. This finding further corroborates our simulation prediction that E46K condensates possess reduced liquidity (Figure  h,j).

  Previous experiments have shown that the amphipathic NTD and the highly hydrophobic NAC are the key domains driving α-syn phase separation, whereas the acidic and disordered CTD was considered to contribute little to α-syn LLPS. ,,  For example, NMR and fluorescence resonance energy transfer studies demonstrated that the NTD and NAC are primary participants in condensate formation. Microscopic experiments on β-synuclein, a protein that lacks several key residues in the NAC region of α-syn, showed a strongly reduced phase separation propensity, indicating the critical role of NAC-mediated hydrophobic interactions. ,  In contrast, recent experiments suggested that the negatively charged CTD plays an active role in modulating α-syn LLPS. −   Our simulations at different spatiotemporal scales show that the electrostatic interactions between the CTD and NTD drive α-syn LLPS, and NAC–NAC hydrophobic interactions play an auxiliary role in this process, indicating the intricate interplay of different domains in dictating α-syn phase separation. These results offer thorough and precise mechanistic insight into α-syn LLPS, and help reconcile the aforementioned inconsistent experimental data on the molecular determinants of the process. The familial mutation E46K reinforces the electrostatic crosstalk between the CTD and NTD (especially the Mut-region) and the NAC–NAC interactions involving the β6 motif, thus promoting the phase separation of α-syn.


## Conclusion
  In this work, we combine all-atom and coarse-grained simulations with biochemical experiments to elucidate how the E46K mutation enhances α-syn phase separation and condensate solidification. All-atom REMD simulations show that the E46K mutation rewrites the electrostatic crosstalk between the oppositely charged NTD and CTD, shifting the interaction with the CTD from the N-terminal region of the NTD to the Mut-region. This alteration in intramolecular interactions is accompanied by increased hydrophobic exposure of the aggregation-promoting β1 motif in the N-terminal region and the LLPS-promoting β6 motif in the tail of the NAC, which may collectively favor intermolecular association and subsequent phase separation. In support of this hypothesis, coarse-grained phase-coexistence simulations demonstrate that the E46K mutation elevates the critical temperature of α-syn phase separation and increases the viscosity of α-syn condensates. These changes, together with our turbidity and FRAP experiments, clearly indicate an E46K-induced enhancement in the propensity for condensate formation and solidification. Interaction analysis reveals that the electrostatic interactions between the NTD and CTD subtly modulate α-syn LLPS, and the NAC–NAC hydrophobic interactions play an assisting role in this process. The E46K variant reshapes the NTD–CTD electrostatic crosstalk by significantly enhancing the interchain interactions between the Mut-region and the CTD, and increases the β6-mediated NAC–NAC hydrophobic interactions, thus promoting α-syn LLPS and LSPT. Our results demonstrate that electrostatic crosstalk between the Mut-region and CTD is the primary driver of E46K α-syn LLPS and condensate solidification, and the NAC–NAC hydrophobic interactions involving the β6 motif play an auxiliary role in this cascade, underscoring the complex interplay of different domains in orchestrating α-syn phase separation. Together, our study offers a comprehensive and detailed mechanistic framework for understanding how the E46K mutation promotes α-syn phase separation, providing new prospects for therapeutic intervention by targeting the pathological phase separation process of α-syn.
