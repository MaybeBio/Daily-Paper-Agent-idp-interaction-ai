# Going high-throughput: Dynamic conformations and interactions in vivo


## Abstract
  Proteins are intrinsically dynamic molecules that continuously explore conformational ensembles to execute biological functions. Conventional structural biology methods rely on in vitro reconstitution of purified components and therefore capture predominantly static snapshots, often overlooking the regulatory roles of the cellular microenvironment, such as molecular crowding, weak interaction networks, and post-translational modifications. This limitation has driven an urgent need to transition from in vitro reconstruction to in vivo characterization within living cells. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides atomic-resolution insights into structure and motions spanning multiple timescales, yet its application is constrained by molecular weight limits, isotopic labeling requirements, and inherently low throughput. Cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS) complements NMR by delivering sparse but long-range spatial restraints without an upper molecular weight limit. The integration of NMR and XL-MS establishes a powerful synergistic framework that bridges atomic-resolution local structures and large-scale interaction topologies, thereby enabling comprehensive characterization of protein dynamic conformations and interaction networks in native environments. Here, we review how this integrative strategy advances the understanding of intrinsically disordered proteins, multi-domain proteins, and dynamic protein-protein interaction networks in native cellular environments. We further discuss emerging technological frontiers, including hyperpolarized NMR, photo-cross-linking, organelle-resolved analysis, and artificial intelligence-guided integrative modeling, which together promise to transform our ability to resolve the true functional states of proteins inside cells.


## Introduction
  As the molecular executors of life, proteins have long stood at the core frontier of life sciences. While structural biology methods such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) capture high-resolution snapshots of steady-state structures, mounting evidence reveals that proteins are not rigid entities but rather dynamic molecules that continuously explore conformational ensembles across diverse energy landscapes [1,2]. Proteins exhibit diverse flexible architectures, ranging from the conformational heterogeneity of intrinsically disordered proteins and large-scale domain motions in multi-domain proteins, to molecular machines assembled from multiple subunits and dynamic complexes stabilized by weak, transient interactions. These highly flexible states often represent the true functional conformations through which proteins execute their biological roles [3,4].

  The protein function is strictly dependent on its physiological environment. However, structural biology methods rely on recombinant expression of highly purified protein monomers or high-affinity complexes. This in vitro reconstitution method overlooks critical microenvironmental factors in the cell, including molecular crowding, networks of weak interactions, and post-translational modifications. Consequently, many biologically relevant structures that are transient, weakly interacting, or highly dynamic remain difficult to capture and resolve. In recent years, artificial intelligence-based structure prediction methods, exemplified by AlphaFold, have achieved large-scale modeling of folded proteins with considerable accuracy [5,6]. Nevertheless, the training data for these artificial intelligence (AI) methods are predominantly derived from high-resolution crystal structures, essentially perpetuating an in vitro structural essence. The AI algorithms cannot integrate the regulatory information imparted by the cellular environment on protein conformations. Therefore, current AI prediction models do not depict the true functional states within cells. This reality urgently demands that structural biology complete the transition from in vitro to in vivo analysis. Capturing structures, dynamic changes, and interactions in their native states has become a critical breakthrough point for deciphering the complexity of cellular signaling networks and revealing the molecular mechanisms of disease.

  Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is inherently compatible with diverse environments, ranging from solutions and membrane mimics to living cells. It is uniquely sensitive to molecular motions spanning multiple timescales. These properties have long established NMR as a pivotal method for characterizing protein dynamic structures at the atomic level [7]. However, NMR method dependence on isotopic labeling, strict molecular weight limitations (typically <50–100 kDa for conventional solution NMR), and inherently low sensitivity severely constrain its application in large protein complex systems and high-throughput studies.

  On the other hand, cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS) has achieved breakthrough advances in recent years. This method captures spatial proximity relationships within or between proteins through various cross-linkers, providing distance restraints [8]. XL-MS offers several distinct advantages. It is unconstrained by an upper molecular weight limit, enables high-throughput and parallel analysis of thousands of interactions, and can be applied directly to cell lysates as well as intact living cells. With improved cross-linking specificity and computational methods, XL-MS has moved beyond proteomic identification to become a powerful platform for resolving protein topology and interaction networks [9]. Recent proteome-scale XL-MS studies have revealed a substantial gap between annotated interactomes and experimentally validated interactions. This observation highlights the need for methods that directly capture protein contacts in complex biological systems and provide structural information beyond association-level evidence [10]. However, because XL-MS typically yields sparse and unevenly distributed distance restraints, it is generally insufficient for de novo structure determination on its own and is most effective when integrated with prior structural models or complementary methods such as NMR [11].

  In this context, the integration of NMR and XL-MS offers a unique technical pathway for high-throughput resolution of protein dynamic conformations and interaction networks in cellular environments [12]. This review first outlines the technical principles of NMR and XL-MS. We highlight their complementary strengths in temporal resolution and spatial scale. We then discuss integrated strategies across three classes of protein systems: intrinsically disordered proteins, multi-domain proteins, and protein complexes. We examine their applications in characterizing dynamic structures and interactions, with particular emphasis on advances in cellular environments. Finally, we look ahead to future developments for both methods and the emerging paradigm of their deep integration.


## Technical foundations and complementary synergies of NMR and XL-MS

### NMR: probing structural and dynamic ensembles at atomic resolution
  NMR-based protein structure determination relies on capturing spatial correlations and motional properties of atomic nuclei. For structural restraints, NMR primarily derives distance information from the nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) and its precisely quantified version, the exact NOE (eNOE) [13]. The effective range of NOE effects is typically less than 6 Å. This short-range characteristic is ideally suited to the compact hydrophobic cores of folded proteins. A single experiment can generate thousands of high-density distance restraints. From this short-range and high-density local information, NMR can de novo determine the three-dimensional structures from the amino acid.

  To overcome the distance limitations of NOEs, paramagnetic approaches introduce exogenous paramagnetic probes. These include nitroxide radicals or lanthanide and transition metal ions (e.g., Gd3+, Mn2+, Cu2+). Through paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE) and pseudocontact shift (PCS) effects, the detectable distance extends to approximately 40 Å [14]. The number of restraints obtained is comparable to that of the residues (e.g., backbone amides excluding prolines). Yet their high distance sensitivity (PRE scales as r−6 and PCS as r−3) makes them powerful tools for capturing transient structures, weak interaction interfaces, and dynamic ensembles [15]. Notably, when proteins exist in multiple conformational equilibria, paramagnetic effects can amplify the signal contributions from lowly populated states. This enables the characterization of rare conformations that are inaccessible to conventional methods [[16], [17], [18]] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1NMR and XL-MS characterize protein structures across distinct spatial (top) and temporal (bottom) scales. NMR techniques such as NOE, PRE, and PCS provide abundant distance restraints for high-resolution structure determination. XL-MS utilizes cross-linkers of varying lengths and conformational states to acquire high-throughput intra- and intermolecular distance information.

  In addition to distance-based restraints, residual dipolar couplings (RDCs) provide complementary long-range orientational information for protein structural analysis. Measured under partial molecular alignment, RDCs report the average orientation of internuclear bond vectors relative to the alignment frame and are therefore highly informative for both structure determination and dynamic characterization [19]. More recently, RDCs have been increasingly applied to multi-domain proteins to define inter-domain orientations and quantify multi-state conformational equilibria [20]. In combination with other NMR observables and computational modeling, RDCs have now become an important tool for modeling multiple conformational states of proteins [21].

  However, NMR imposes strict sample requirements, typically including >95% purity, protein concentrations above ∼0.1 mM, and stable isotope labeling (15N, 13C, and often deuteration for larger systems). Site-specific introduction of paramagnetic probes further requires a unique and chemically accessible modification site, such as a single cysteine, which increases sample preparation complexity. In addition, increasing molecular weight leads to longer rotational correlation times, resulting in severe line broadening and spectral overlap. These features make NMR fundamentally a high-resolution but relatively low-throughput method. It excels in providing residue-level structural and dynamic information, but is less suited to the independent analysis of very large complexes or broad high-throughput characterization.

  In cellular environments, NMR provides a unique approach for monitoring protein conformational states, interaction-induced perturbations, and post-translational modifications directly in living cells. At the same time, in-cell NMR faces additional challenges beyond those encountered in purified in vitro systems, including low intracellular protein concentration, inefficient or heterogeneous isotope labeling, difficulties in protein delivery or intracellular expression, signal broadening caused by high viscosity and nonspecific interactions, and limited experimental time due to loss of cell viability [22,23]. These constraints often reduce sensitivity and spectral resolution, particularly for large folded proteins. Addressing these challenges requires both methodological advances in NMR itself, including improvements in labeling strategies, pulse sequences, and detection schemes, and integration with complementary techniques.


### XL-MS: an in vivo ruler for protein topology and interaction networks
  XL-MS utilizes bifunctional cross-linkers to react with nucleophilic groups on protein side chains, covalently trapping pairs of spatially proximal residues. After proteolytic digestion and mass spectrometric identification, these cross-linked peptides are converted into inter-residue distance restraints. The cross-linker length is highly tunable: zero-length cross-linkers like 1,1′-carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) capture directly contacting residue pairs; longer cross-linkers such as trehalose disuccinimidyl ester (TDS) probe distance ranges of ∼20–30 Å [24]; and some cross-linkers like protein interaction reporter (PIR) can span distances up to 60 Å [25]. In addition to their varying lengths, cross-linkers exhibit distinct flexibilities and conformational dynamics, thereby enabling the capture of diverse protein structural information in complex cellular environments [26]. This tunability enables XL-MS to capture multi-scale information ranging from secondary structures to entire complex topologies. Furthermore, cross-linker scaffolds can be extended with enrichment moieties. This improves enrichment efficiency and identification coverage of cross-linked peptides.

  Beyond cross-links, mono-links also carry structural information. These mark residues where only one end of the cross-linker has attached. They label surface residues with high solvent accessibility and help define protein surface properties [27]. This detection logic shares conceptual similarity with solvent PRE (sPRE) in NMR, which probes amino acid exposure to study protein structure and dynamics [28]. Moreover, quantitative analysis of mono-link sites generates protein surface accessibility maps. This provides a complementary dimension for identifying dynamic regions during conformational changes and distinguishing rigid structures from flexible loops. The information cross-validates NMR-derived relaxation parameters. More importantly, high-resolution mass spectrometry accurately measures cross-linked peptide masses. This allows XL-MS to effectively distinguish intra-links from inter-links. Intra-links reflect intramolecular conformational dynamics, whereas inter-links reflect protein-protein interactions.

  Compared with NMR, XL-MS requires no isotopic labeling and tolerates a certain degree of sample impurity. It can directly process cell lysates or even living cell samples. It has no upper molecular weight limit and inherently supports high-throughput analysis. Recent proteome-scale XL-MS work has further demonstrated the structural utility of cross-link-derived restraints [10]. In particular, experimentally observed cross-links were shown to expose inconsistencies in AI-predicted complex models for poorly characterized protein pairs, highlighting the importance of experimental restraints in model assessment. These findings reinforce the role of XL-MS not only in supporting direct protein-protein interaction identification, but also in providing physically grounded information for structural modeling. However, cross-linking reactions strictly depend on residue chemical reactivity and spatial accessibility. Lysine-targeting cross-linkers are the most widely used; others target cysteine, aspartate, or glutamate [29]. This results in an uneven and overall sparse distribution of distance restraints across the sequence, typically yielding only dozens of restraints per structural domain. Consequently, XL-MS is generally insufficient for de novo structure determination on its own. Instead, it is typically combined with prior structural models, including NMR structures or AlphaFold predictions, for integrative modeling and structural filtering.

  Therefore, NMR and XL-MS are systematically complementary in the nature of their structural restraints. NMR provides high-density, short-range restraints and atomic-level local structures. XL-MS contributes sparse yet long-range, cross-scale global topological restraints. These anchor domain orientations and complex assembly modes. This combination of local-dense and global-sparse restraints enables integrative modeling: NMR refines intra-domain conformations, while XL-MS assembles domains or filters conformational ensembles. Together, they accomplish comprehensive structural characterization from atomic coordinates to large-scale assembly (Fig. 1).

  The two methods are also complementary in the temporal dimension. NMR techniques such as chemical shift perturbation (CSP) [30] and Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) relaxation dispersion [31] resolve conformational exchange dynamics from picoseconds to seconds. These methods do not directly yield spatial distances. Instead, they precisely determine key thermodynamic parameters, including exchange rates (kex), excited-state populations (pB), interaction interfaces, and dissociation constants (KD) [[32], [33], [34]]. XL-MS captures structural snapshots at specific time points through chemical or photo-cross-linking. These snapshots fall within millisecond-to-second windows and reflect ensemble-averaged topological features [35,36].

  Crucially, both NMR and XL-MS can be applied inside living cells. Their integration overcomes individual limitations in molecular weight, throughput, and resolution. More importantly, it opens a new paradigm for high-throughput analysis of dynamic interactomes in native cellular environments. This spans from atomic mechanisms to network topology, and from in vitro reconstitution to cellular contexts. In the following sections, we discuss the technical implementation of this integrative strategy across three dimensions: intrinsically disordered proteins, conformational dynamics in multi-domain proteins, and protein-protein interactions.


## Intrinsically disordered proteins: from in vitro dynamic ensembles to in vivo conformational heterogeneity
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) constitute 30%–40% of the human proteome [37]. Enriched in charged and polar residues but lacking a hydrophobic core, these proteins cannot fold into stable three-dimensional structures under physiological conditions. Instead, they adopt highly dynamic conformational ensembles. From an energy landscape perspective, IDPs possess a flat free energy surface with low barriers between conformational states, enabling rapid interconversion in response to thermal fluctuations or environmental perturbations [38]. This structural flexibility allows IDPs to mediate molecular recognition and signal transduction through multivalent, weak, and transient interactions, making them important regulators in processes such as transcription, cell-cycle control, and signal transduction [39,40].

  Because IDPs lack a single dominant conformation, methods that rely on well-ordered steady-state structures, such as X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM, are often limited in their ability to capture their structural features directly. Disordered regions frequently appear as missing or weak density in structures obtained by these methods. In addition, AlphaFold and related prediction algorithms remain limited in describing the conformational heterogeneity of IDPs [41]. NMR is currently the most powerful experimental approach for residue-resolved characterization of IDP conformational behavior in solution [42]. Through chemical shifts, scalar couplings, RDCs, and relaxation parameters, combined with molecular simulation sampling methods, NMR can reveal local secondary-structure propensities, transient long-range contacts, compaction tendencies, and population shifts among conformational substates [43]. These experimentally supported features are particularly informative for connecting sequence, environment, and function in disordered proteins.

  Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and biomolecular condensates are widely involved in various biological processes. Many IDPs and IDRs make important contributions to condensate formation because their conformational plasticity and multivalent weak interactions can promote phase separation [44]. However, LLPS is not unique to disordered proteins; structured proteins and protein-nucleic acid assemblies can also undergo phase separation. In this context, IDPs provide especially informative systems for understanding how conformational heterogeneity is coupled to condensate formation and regulation.

  Studying the structural behavior of IDPs in condensates is therefore of considerable interest, but it also poses substantial technical challenges. Condensates often exist in highly dynamic liquid-like, gel-like, or partially solidified states, which can limit the applicability of traditional structural methods. Liquid-state NMR offers unique advantages in this setting. By monitoring chemical-shift perturbations before and after phase separation, NMR can detect local conformational changes and altered dynamics of IDPs within droplets [45]. Intermolecular filter-NOEs [46] or intermolecular PREs [47] probe interaction patterns inside droplets and reveal the microscopic nucleation mechanisms of phase separation. When condensates undergo gelation or pathological fibrillation, solid-state NMR (ssNMR) provides residue resolution, mapping rigid domains and dynamic regions to resolve the structural transition pathways from liquid to gel to amyloid fibrils [48]. Nevertheless, whether in droplets, gels, or fibers, these systems remain large and structurally complex, which substantially restricts the range of condensates that can be studied in detail.

  Within this context, XL-MS provides a complementary dimension for probing phase-separated systems. Unrestricted by molecular size or sample viscosity, XL-MS can distinguish homotypic and heterotypic molecules within condensates through isotope labeling strategies, such as mixing 14N and 15N samples. It captures intramolecular long-range contacts and intermolecular interaction networks, thereby mapping multivalent interaction topologies inside condensates. Such information complements the residue-resolved dynamic and conformational readouts obtained by NMR [49].

  Beyond the complex physical environments of phase separation systems, moving from in vitro reconstitution to the living cells poses greater challenges for studying IDP structure and dynamics. The high conformational flexibility of IDPs makes their ensembles extremely sensitive to the environment. Macromolecular crowding, nonspecific interactions, ionic fluctuations, and post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation and methylation, can reshape their conformational preferences and interaction behavior in ways that are difficult to reproduce fully in vitro [50]. Examining IDP conformational behavior directly in cells is therefore essential for understanding how the cellular environment modulates their structural and functional states.

  NMR enables structural studies of proteins directly in living cells. Because NMR detection generally requires isotopically labeled samples, labeled proteins can be produced by cellular expression using isotope-enriched precursors [51,53,54], or expressed and purified in vitro before delivery into living systems such as Xenopus oocytes through microinjection [55,56] (Fig. 2A). Importantly, the conformational flexibility of IDPs confers unique NMR observational advantages inside cells. The backbone and side chain motions of IDPs are significantly faster than those of folded proteins of comparable molecular weight, so their rotational diffusion restriction in the high-viscosity intracellular environment is weaker.   NMR studies show that many IDPs (such as α-synuclein and tau protein) remain highly disordered inside cells, with ensemble characteristics generally consistent with in vitro observations [22,52,58]. However, subtle conformational preferences caused by the local chemical environment may have important functional implications. NMR can distinguish the different behaviors of folded and disordered proteins inside cells. For folded proteins, interactions cause broadening of all residue signals, whereas for IDPs, only residues involved in interactions show broadening or disappearance. This difference provides critical information anchors for studying IDP interactions inside cells [59,60]. In addition, this technique can track post-translational modifications inside cells in real time, elucidating dynamic conformational switch mechanisms in signal transduction [61] (Fig. 2B).

  Despite the rich dynamic information provided by NMR, severe spectral overlap and reduced resolution in the cellular environment make direct application of NOE- or PRE-based distance measurements difficult for many IDPs. XL-MS offers an alternative route for obtaining structural restraints in such settings. Because IDPs are often enriched in charged residues, including lysines, they can provide abundant anchor points for cross-linking reactions. More importantly, XL-MS is not limited by molecular weight and can capture long-range intramolecular contacts of IDPs even in complex cellular environments. For IDPs such as HMG-I/Y, conventional structure prediction tools also fall short—AlphaFold2 assigns low per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) to most of the sequence, a typical signature of disorder (Fig. 3A). By integrating XL-MS-derived restraints with molecular dynamics simulations, researchers have characterized various conformational states of IDPs such as HMG-I/Y and HMG-17 directly in cells [62] (Fig. 3).   In the cellular environment, the integration of NMR and XL-MS provides a practical framework for dissecting functionally relevant features of IDP conformational heterogeneity. NMR sensitively reports on residue-specific environmental changes, local conformational preferences, and dynamics, whereas XL-MS supplies long-range intramolecular restraints and interaction maps that are difficult to obtain in cells by NMR alone. Together, these methods can reveal changes in chain compaction, transient long-range contacts, and interaction-dependent reweighting of conformational substates. Integrative analyses of this kind are especially valuable for identifying structural features relevant to post-translational regulation, phase separation, binding specificity, and disease-associated transitions [63].


## Multi-domain proteins: from in vitro physical mimicry to in vivo conformational remodeling
  Whether intrinsically disordered or well-folded, structural dynamics are central to protein function. Multi-domain proteins represent a paradigmatic case. These proteins link independently folded domains through flexible linker, and their biological functions often depend on the relative orientation and dynamic rearrangement of these domains. Compared with single-domain systems, multi-domain proteins exhibit more complex conformational distributions and dynamic behaviors. These range from closed states with compact domains to fully extended open states, with multiple functionally relevant metastable conformations distributed in between. NMR methods can obtain distance restraints within domains or between proximate domains through NOEs. PRE and related techniques can capture longer inter-domain distances, resolving the spatial state distributions and motion patterns in solution [65,66].

  Because the dynamic conformational distributions of multi-domain proteins are also subject to environmental influences and regulation, in vitro reconstitution experiments serve as an important bridge to understanding cellular environmental control. By adding macromolecular crowders to mimic excluded volume effects in cells, researchers have found that the conformational equilibrium of canonical multi-domain proteins such as calmodulin shifts markedly toward compact states. Crowded environments not only increase the population of closed states but also enhance substrate binding activity [67]. Introducing further confinement, such as that created by reverse micelles, produces even more dramatic conformational remodeling. Confined space forces the two domains of calmodulin to approach each other in a manner distinct from crowded conditions, forming a unique compact conformation that cannot bind certain substrates. This reveals the molecular mechanism by which confinement effects regulate function through reshaping conformational distributions [68]. However, it must be noted that in vitro mimicry relies primarily on hard repulsion from hard-sphere models, whereas soft interactions comprising electrostatic, hydrophobic, and van der Waals forces in real cells often play more important roles. This cognitive leap from physical hard constraints to biological soft environments urgently demands a shift toward in vivo characterization in living cells.

  In-cell NMR investigation of multi-domain proteins remains technically demanding. Studies of representative systems such as phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK) and tyrosine phosphatase 1E (PDZ) have established that, although overall folding states are generally preserved in the cellular environment, the local loop flexibilities can be subtly yet functionally remodelled [69]. Nevertheless, using in-cell NMR to determine high-resolution structures of these proteins remains technically challenging. Their extensive engagement in specific and nonspecific interactions causes severe line broadening and signal overlap, while inter-domain motions produce chemical shift averaging that undermines conventional NOE experiments [64] (Fig. 4A and B).   Here, XL-MS provides a critical complement to NMR. Free from molecular weight limitations and spectral resolution constraints, XL-MS can capture long-range inter-domain restraints at high throughput in living cells. The integration of NMR and XL-MS for multi-domain proteins follows a common principle: NMR provides high-resolution structures of full-length proteins, major conformational states, or individual domains, whereas XL-MS contributes in-cell inter-domain restraints for conformational refinement or ensemble reconstruction. When full-length structures or major conformational states are already available from in vitro studies, these models can be directly refined against cellular cross-linking data. This approach has successfully characterized the structural states of calmodulin and hnRNP A1 in cells (Fig. 4C and D). It has also been revealed that phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) adopts markedly different domain arrangements in the cytoplasm and nucleus, demonstrating that subcellular localization can specifically reshape conformational distributions [71]. When full-length structures are inaccessible because of long linkers or extensive flexibility, as in nucleic acid-binding proteins containing multiple RNA recognition motifs (RRMs), individual domains can first be resolved by NMR and then used as structural modules to reconstruct full-length conformational ensembles using XL-MS-derived restraints (Fig. 4E and F) [62].

  In summary, the combination of atomic-level intra-domain dynamic information from NMR and long-range inter-domain topological restraints from XL-MS enables us to map the conformational distributions of multi-domain proteins in cellular. This integration clarifies how crowding, confinement, and weak interactions synergistically reshape the energy landscape to regulate substrate recognition and signal transduction.


## Protein complexes: from local interactions to large-scale assemblies
  In cells, protein complexes are formed and regulated within a crowded and heterogeneous intracellular environment, where interactions range from local and transient contacts to stable multi-subunit assemblies. Beyond specific binding events, proteins also engage in weak or nonspecific associations that can influence folding, stability, and interaction networks, a phenomenon referred to as quinary structure [73]. These features make it essential to examine protein interactions directly in their native cellular context in order to capture functionally relevant structural states, interaction dynamics, and modes of complex organization.

  In-cell NMR provides a unique means of characterizing such interactions at residue-level resolution in living systems. For example, the STINT (STructural INTeractions) [74] approach enables selective observation of binary protein interactions in cells through sequential expression and labeling strategies [75,76]. In more complex intracellular environments, 19F NMR is particularly valuable because of its high sensitivity, large chemical shift dispersion, and lack of endogenous cellular background signals [77]. Researchers have successfully used 19F probes to quantitatively characterize the thermodynamic stability of GB1 dimerization in bacteria, mammalian cells, and Xenopus oocytes [72,78] (Fig. 5A).   Despite these strengths, the application of NMR to protein complexes in cells remains limited by intracellular line broadening, spectral overlap, and molecular weight effects. These factors often restrict analysis to small proteins, localized interaction interfaces, or selected regions of larger systems, making structural characterization of large, heterogeneous, or transient assemblies particularly challenging. NMR alone is therefore often insufficient to define the overall architecture of multi-subunit protein complexes in cells, underscoring the need for complementary approaches that can provide longer-range structural information in native environments.

  Within this framework, XL-MS fills critical gaps that NMR alone cannot address. By covalently capturing spatially proximal residues directly in living cells, XL-MS preserves weak, transient, and environment-dependent interactions, including short-lived encounter complexes [79]. Its scalability further enables high-throughput mapping of intracellular interaction networks, offering system-level views of cellular signaling and regulation. XL-MS is therefore particularly powerful for studying protein interactions in native environments and for defining the assembly topology of mega-protein machines that exceed the practical molecular weight range of NMR (Fig. 5B and C).

  The proteasome provides a representative example. In vivo XL-MS, when integrated with available structural models of individual subunits, has enabled integrative analysis of differences in proteasome assembly between the nucleus and cytoplasm, while also providing insight into its overall architecture and conformational organization [80]. However, many mechanistic details within such large assemblies remain unresolved, including subunit-specific dynamic changes and the roles of key residues in conformational regulation and function. Addressing these questions represents an important future direction, where NMR could contribute residue-level insight into local structural rearrangements and dynamic processes within selected proteasomal components.

  This complementarity is especially evident in the study of membrane proteins. NMR is particularly well suited for probing the intrinsic conformational dynamics of membrane proteins and their substrate-binding events [81], whereas XL-MS is especially powerful for mapping their interactions with intracellular signaling partners. The interaction between GPCRs and G proteins represents a classic example of this combined application [82]. More broadly, only through deep integration of complementary information, anchored by one or more primary techniques, can we connect atomic-level motions with the organization of protein complexes in living cells, extending structural analysis from local dynamics to cellular assemblies.


## Conclusions and perspectives
  The integration of NMR and XL-MS offers a unique technical pathway for high-throughput, in vivo studies of protein dynamics. Two complementary strategies can be pursued. One is a divide-and-conquer approach: beginning with high-resolution NMR structures of key monomers or domains, long-range XL-MS restraints are used for domain assembly or complex docking to reconstruct the overall conformation of full-length proteins or interaction networks, supplemented by NMR-derived dynamic characterization. The other is a reverse, global-to-local strategy: XL-MS first maps a panoramic view of global interaction networks and conformational heterogeneity inside cells, and key nodes are then selected for in-depth mechanistic analysis by in-cell NMR. This bidirectional integration of global topology and local dynamics overcomes the limitations of individual methods in resolution, throughput, and molecular weight. It also provides a multidimensional information foundation for understanding the authentic behavior and functional regulation of proteins under physiological conditions (Fig. 6).Fig. 6The combination of NMR and XL-MS enables high-throughput structural studies of intrinsically disordered proteins, multi-domain proteins, and protein interaction complexes in living cells. Future advances in 19F-labeling and dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP) will further enhance NMR detection sensitivity. Meanwhile, the development of novel cross-linkers will broaden the temporal window accessible to XL-MS, and emerging targeting strategies will extend protein structural studies to subcellular resolution.

  Continued innovation in NMR and XL-MS will expand the frontiers of cellular research. In the NMR field, the deployment of 1.2 GHz and higher-field spectrometers will significantly boost detection sensitivity, enabling studies of lower-concentration samples inside cells. This is particularly beneficial for dynamic systems such as IDPs. The combination of novel non-uniform sampling schemes with deep learning reconstruction algorithms permits the acquisition of high-dimensional experiments within the time window that maintains cell viability. Furthermore, photochemically induced dynamic nuclear polarization (photo-CIDNP) with various 19F probes promises to enhance sensitivity by several orders of magnitude [22]. This may enable direct observation of transient intermediates and lowly populated conformational states in living cells [83,84].

  On the other hand, XL-MS is advancing along three major directions: expanding information coverage, improving spatiotemporal resolution, and strengthening structural determination power. Regarding information coverage, the design of novel cleavable, enrichable, and multiplex-labeling cross-linkers has markedly improved the capture efficiency and quantitative comparison accuracy for weak interactions and transient complexes. This extends applicability to more complex systems and dynamic processes. In spatiotemporal resolution, photo cross-linkers enable snapshot capture of millisecond-scale biological events, while the integration of ultrahigh-resolution mass spectrometry with artificial intelligence algorithms has substantially improved the accuracy, throughput, and automation of cross-link site identification. Currently, the field is pushing toward single-cell-level applications, aiming to map protein interactions and conformational landscapes while preserving cellular heterogeneity.

  Looking ahead, NMR and XL-MS are expected to achieve deep integration in the following dimensions to address core challenges in life sciences and innovative drug discovery.


### Rational cross-linker design: from empirical screening to precision engineering
  The physicochemical properties of cross-linkers, including polarity, rigidity, and arm length, directly determine their reaction efficiency and spatial sampling capacity in cells. NMR studies combined with computational simulation have shown that both the polarity and rigidity of cross-linkers influence the outcome of in-cell cross-linking experiments [26,85]. Therefore, integration of NMR and XL-MS can provide a theoretical basis for the rational design of smart cross-linkers tailored to specific protein classes or responsive to particular microenvironments. This will improve the precision and specificity of in vivo structural analysis.


### Reconstructing enzymatic catalytic cycles: from static snapshots to dynamic trajectories
  Enzymatic catalysis involves multiscale dynamic processes including substrate encounter, recognition, transition-state stabilization, and product release. Leveraging its high-throughput nature, XL-MS can systematically map global conformational rearrangements induced by substrate binding and the spatial positions of cofactors. NMR, in turn, can monitor picosecond-to-millisecond conformational exchange and chiral recognition at the active site in real time. The integration of both methods holds promise for constructing complete catalytic cycle maps in various time scales, revealing the structure-dynamics relationships that govern transition-state stabilization and catalytic efficiency.


### Organelle-resolved cellular structural biology
  Conventional in-cell NMR typically yields averaged structural features of proteins at the whole-cell level. In recent years, through organelle isolation techniques, NMR has been applied to directly detect the structural states of proteins inside mitochondria and to investigate how the cellular environment regulates protein structure [86]. In vivo XL-MS can achieve compartment-specific distance restraint capture through various fractionation methods and organelle-targeted cross-linking, transporting cross-linkers specifically to mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and other locations. This overcomes the averaging limitation of traditional whole-cell cross-linking. Such integration will elucidate how the same protein exhibits structural heterogeneity and functional specialization in different cellular compartments.


### Multimodal integration and artificial intelligence augment
  Experimental constraints from NMR and XL-MS will serve as physical priors to be deeply coupled with AI prediction models such as AlphaFold. Experimental data will be used not merely for validation but, more importantly, to guide deep learning correction of prediction errors through restraint-driven training, particularly in disordered regions and dynamic interfaces. Concurrently, integration with cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) will establish a cross-scale analytical paradigm. Furthermore, combining NMR and XL-MS with single-molecule Förster resonance energy transfer and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy will enable validation of ensemble-averaged results at the single-cell level. Ultimately, these heterogeneous data will be fused through a unified Bayesian integrative computation platform to construct in vivo, multi-omic atlases of dynamic structure, interaction, and function. This will inaugurate a new era of spatiotemporally resolved functional proteomics.
