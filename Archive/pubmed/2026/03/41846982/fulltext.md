# Intrinsically disordered SERBP1 regulates translation through topology-driven G-quadruplex recognition


## Abstract
  Serpine mRNA-binding protein 1 (SERBP1) is an intrinsically disordered RNA-binding protein that regulates translation and ribosome biogenesis through interactions with ribosomes and other molecular complexes. Despite its regulatory importance and implication in cancer development, the molecular basis of SERBP1 RNA recognition remains poorly understood. Here, we characterize the G-quadruplex (G4)-binding properties of SERBP1. Using NMR spectroscopy and biophysical assays, we show that SERBP1 binds parallel G4s, both RNA and DNA, with low micromolar affinity through a conserved mechanism. Molecular dynamics and docking simulations reveal an encircling mechanism in which the RGG box wraps around the G4 while downstream C-terminal serine residues stabilize the complex through hydrogen bonding. Phosphomimetic mutations of key serines disrupt this stabilization and reduce binding affinity, identifying phosphorylation as a regulatory switch for SERBP1 activity. Recognition is driven by G4 topology rather than nucleotide sequence, establishing SERBP1 as a broad-specificity G4-binding protein. We further demonstrate that SERBP1 regulates mTOR expression in glioblastoma cell lines through G4 elements in the mTOR 5’ UTR, and that SERBP1 depletion synergizes with mTOR inhibition to reduce cell growth. These results establish SERBP1 as a G4 adaptor protein and represent, to our knowledge, the first detailed characterization of G4 recognition by a fully disordered domain, providing a molecular framework for targeting SERBP1–G4 interactions in cancer.


## Introduction
  RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are critical gene expression regulators, orchestrating process such as RNA processing, splicing, RNA modification and translation. They perform diverse functions including enzymatic catalysis, nucleic acid transport and protection, and the recruitment or inhibition of key regulatory factors1. Alterations in the function or structure of these proteins can severely disrupt cellular machinery and contribute to cancer development2. Among these RBPs, SERBP1 (Serpine mRNA-binding protein 1) is an intrinsically disordered RBP involved in a broad array of cellular processes. Structurally, SERBP1 is largely disordered except for two stable α-helices located in its N-terminal3 and C-terminal4 regions, and it harbors an RG-rich region and an RGG box that constitutes its principal RNA-binding region5. It is devoid of any of the known, folded RNA binding motifs like RRMs or KH domains.

  We previously showed that SERBP1 uniquely binds GC-rich RNA sequences5, consistent with recent reports describing it as a G-quadruplex (G4) binder6,7. Predominantly cytoplasmic8, SERBP1 localizes to ribosomes at the interface of the 40S and 60S subunits in both translationally active9,10 and dormant states11, where it regulates the translation of proteins involved in diverse processes, including CtIP-mediated DNA repair12 and fibrinolysis through stabilization of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 mRNA13–15. Beyond translation, SERBP1 localizes to stress granules16,17, the nucleolus upon arginine methylation8, and PML nuclear bodies18, and interacts with PARP1 to influence PARylation of DNA damage response factors in a reciprocal feedback loop19,20. SERBP1 also plays a central role in neurogenesis and neuro-differentiation21 and is overexpressed in a wide range of cancers, including glioblastoma21,22, ovarian carcinoma23, breast cancer24, and lymphocytic leukemia25.

  G-quadruplexes, the structures recognized by SERBP1, are non-canonical nucleic acid structures formed by stacked guanine tetrads bound together through Hoogsteen base pairs and coordinated by a monovalent cation. They are present in DNA and RNA genomes, suggesting broad biological functions26–29, and are also associated with many diseases30–33. G4 regulatory functions and their impact on diseases are typically mediated by G4-binding proteins, which form, stabilize, or disrupt G4 structures34. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of G4–protein interactions has therefore become highly relevant for developing small molecules that could compete with, mimic, or inhibit these interactions. While structural details of G4 recognition have been reported for proteins containing folded RNA-binding domains35, how intrinsically disordered proteins engage G4 structures remains poorly understood.

  Here, we report the G4-binding properties of SERBP1. Using fluorescence polarization, NMR, molecular dynamics, and in silico docking, we characterize the atomic details of SERBP1–G4 recognition and reveal an encircling mechanism in which the RGG box and adjacent C-terminal serine residues lock around the G4 structure. We show that this interaction is topology-driven rather than sequence-specific, with SERBP1 binding all parallel RNA G4s tested, including sequences from validated targets such as HDAC3 and mTOR. Phosphomimetic mutations of key C-terminal serines disrupt binding, identifying phosphorylation as a potential regulatory switch. We further demonstrate that SERBP1 regulates mTOR expression in glioblastoma cells through G4 elements in the mTOR 5’ UTR, and that SERBP1 depletion synergizes with mTOR inhibition to reduce cell growth. Together, these findings establish SERBP1 as a G4 adaptor protein and provide a molecular framework for targeting SERBP1–G4 interactions in cancer.


## Materials and Methods

### Protein expression and purification:
  All mutant constructs were cloned from a plasmid containing the SERBP1189−400 sequence; primers and mutation details are listed in Supplementary Table 1, along with corresponding Addgene accession numbers.

  All protein samples were prepared as previously described5. Briefly, plasmids containing the different SERBP1 constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) Star strains. The pAG8Ha vector encodes an N-terminal 8xHistidine tag followed by a TEV protease cleavage site. A single colony was used to inoculate a 5 mL starter culture in rich medium (LB) at 37°C for 6–8 hours. 2 mL of this starter culture was then inoculated into 50 mL of M9 minimal medium supplemented with 15NH4Cl as the sole nitrogen source and grown at 37°C overnight. The next day, the preculture was used to inoculate 950 mL of M9 minimal medium, also supplemented with 15NH4Cl, and grown with shaking at 37°C. All cultures contained 100 μg/mL ampicillin for selection. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG when the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. After four hours, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,500 × g for 20 minutes and stored at −80°C.

  For protein purification, cell pellets were thawed, resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole), sonicated, and cleared by centrifugation at 45,000 × g for 30 minutes. Supernatants were applied to a 5 mL HisTrap column (Cytiva, MA) equilibrated with lysis buffer and washed with the same buffer for 20 column volumes. Protein was eluted with 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole buffer. Eluted protein was incubated with TEV protease and dialyzed overnight against 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA buffer in a 6–8 kDa cutoff dialysis membrane for cleavage of the 8xHistidine tag. The next day, the sample was applied again to the HisTrap column equilibrated with lysis buffer, and tag-free protein was collected in the flow-through fractions before being concentrated using a 10 kDa cutoff Amicon centrifugal concentrator (Merck, NJ). The sample was further purified by size exclusion chromatography as a final polishing step using a Superdex 75 16/60 column (Cytiva, MA) equilibrated with 20 mM NaPi pH 6.9, 60 mM NaCl (NMR buffer).


### Nucleic acid sample preparation:
  All oligonucleotides were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Supplementary Table 2). Samples were resuspended in nuclease-free water (Qiagen, MD) and stock concentrations were measured by A260 absorbance using molar extinction coefficients at 260 nm. Samples were then aliquoted to appropriate amounts, speed-vacuum dried, and resuspended in either 20 mM Tris pH 7.5 or 20 mM KPi pH 6.5 in the presence of either 50 mM KCl or 50 mM LiCl.


### Circular dichroism (CD):
  All dichroic spectra were recorded on 20 μM samples in 20 mM Tris buffer at pH 7.5 with either 50 mM KCl or LiCl on a Jasco 810 spectropolarimeter (Jasco, OK) at 20°C with a scan speed of 50 nm/min. Each spectrum was acquired in triplicate, subjected to buffer baseline subtraction, and converted to molar residual ellipticity using a previously described relationship36.


### Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC):
  ITC experiments were performed at 25°C on an Affinity ITC (TA Instruments, MA). Protein samples were dialyzed overnight at room temperature against 20 mM KPi pH 6.8 and 50 mM KCl. The next day, protein concentration was measured by absorbance at 280 nm, and the dialysis buffer was filtered and degassed. The sample cell was equilibrated with buffer, then loaded with 400 μL of protein sample at a concentration of 20 μM. Oligonucleotide ligands were dried and resuspended in dialysis buffer to a concentration of 200 μM. They were annealed at 95°C for 5 minutes followed by slow cooling at room temperature for 15–30 minutes. A volume of 100 to 150 μL was then loaded into the instrument syringe, and each experiment consisted of an initial delay of 200 seconds prior to 20 injections of 2.5 μL with a stirring rate of 125 rpm and a delay of 200 seconds between each injection. Experimental data underwent baseline correction and were fitted with a one-site independent binding model provided by the instrument software.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA):
  Experiments consisted of loading 20 μL of 1 μM Pu20m2 samples in the absence or presence of increasing ratios (10:1, 50:1, and 100:1) of different SERBP1 constructs onto a 20% polyacrylamide gel. Electrophoresis was run for 100 minutes at 100 V in 0.5x Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer, and the gel was subsequently incubated with SYBR Safe staining solution (Invitrogen) for 30 minutes prior to UV detection.


### NMR spectroscopy:
  All NMR experiments were performed at 278 K, unless stated otherwise, on a Bruker spectrometer operating at a proton Larmor frequency of 700.13 MHz equipped with a 5 mm TCI z-axis gradient cryogenic probe. All titration experiments were acquired on protein samples at a concentration of 50 μM in NMR buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and 10% D2O. Titrations consisted of the acquisition of 1H,15N-HSQC spectra with increasing concentrations of the different ligands at ligand:protein ratios of 1:4, 1:2, 1:1, and 2:1. 1H,15N -HSQC spectra were recorded with 64* × 1024* complex data points in the indirect (15N) and direct (1H) dimensions, respectively, corresponding to acquisition times of 37.6 and 112.6 ms. Spectra were apodized with a sine bell function, zero-filled to twice the number of acquired points, and phase corrected with NMRPipe37 and analyzed with CCPNMR38. Normalized chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) were calculated with respect to the 1H and 15N gyromagnetic ratios using the relationship:  (1) Δδ=δ1H2+0.1δ15N2

  G-quadruplex 1H 1D spectra were recorded at 278 K on 100 μM samples, unless stated otherwise, prepared in 20 mM KPi pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl. Experiments consisted of the acquisition of 256 scans, and water suppression was achieved either by a Watergate sequence or by gradient excitation sculpting39. Spectra were processed with baseline and phase correction and analyzed with TopSpin 4.0.


### Fluorescence polarization (FP) assays:
  5’6-FAM-labeled Pu20m2 was purchased from (IDT, NC) and purified by size exclusion chromatography on an S75 10/300 column (Cytiva, MA) equilibrated with 20 mM NaPi pH 6.8, 200 mM NaCl. Purified SERBP1189–400 and SERBP1 mutants were buffer exchanged using 3 kDa Amicon ultracentrifugal filters (Merck) against the same buffer. Assays were conducted in 384-well microplates (Corning, MA) and data were measured on a BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader equipped with a green Agilent BioTek filter cube. Each well contained a 30 μL reaction mixture with 10 nM 5’6-FAM-Pu20m2 and increasing concentrations of the different SERBP1 constructs. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 485 nm and 528 nm, respectively, and experiments were performed in triplicate. Background noise from the oligonucleotide alone was subtracted for data analysis, and adjusted data were fitted to a one-site binding model according to the following:  (2) r=rmin+Δr[P]Kd+[P]  Where r is the anisotropy, Dr = rmax−rmin and [P] is the concentration of SERBP1.


### Molecular dynamics and in silico docking:
  Simulations of SERBP1366–400 and SERBP1366–400,4SE were performed with GROMACS 2022.340,41 patched with PLUMED 2.8.142, installed on NMRbox43 servers. Initial structures were generated with AlphaFold344, and the four serine-to-glutamate mutations in SERBP1366–400,4SE were introduced with PyMOL. Protein parameters and water molecules were generated with the a99SB-disp force field and the a99SB-disp water model, respectively45. The system was solvated in a dodecahedron unit box of 7 nm diameter with water molecules and neutralized with a salt concentration of 50 mM NaCl. System energy was then minimized with a steepest descent algorithm, which terminates when the maximum force reaches a value lower than 1000 kJ·mol−1·nm−1. Temperature equilibration was performed for 100 ps at 300 K using an NVT ensemble with a Berendsen thermostat, and pressure equilibration was performed for 100 ps at 300 K using an NPT ensemble with a target pressure of 1 bar. MD production was then run for 1 μs with a 2 fs timestep, saving coordinates every 10 ps, using a Particle Mesh Ewald algorithm for long-range electrostatics and a 1.0 nm cutoff length for van der Waals interactions.

  Analysis of the simulation was performed with the MDAnalysis Python library46,47. Intramolecular contact maps were plotted based on Cα distances with a cutoff distance of 8.0 Å, and snapshots of the simulation were taken randomly every 5% of the trajectory. These snapshots were then used as an ensemble input for HADDOCK docking simulations48, where active residues were defined as all arginines from the RGG box (namely arginines 367, 370, 373, 376, 378, and 381), as well as residues S391, S392, A393, S394, and A395. Passive residues were defined as residues surrounding the active ones. The input structure of Pu20m2 was modified from the structure deposited in the PDB (code 7WGW) by deleting the cGMP ligand and changing the 8-oxoguanine to a regular guanine nucleotide at position 18 using PyMOL. All nucleotides of the quadruplex were defined as active residues for docking with HADDOCK48.


### Cell culture:
  U251 and U343 glioblastoma cell lines were obtained from the University of Uppsala, Sweden. They were maintained in a 37°C, 5% CO2 atmosphere in DMEM/High glucose medium (Cytiva, MA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, MO) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (ThermoFisher, MA). Growth medium was changed every two to three days, and cells passaged when reaching >90% confluency.


### siRNA transfection:
  U251 and U343 cells were transfected in 96-well plates (Corning, MA) using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, CA) and Opti-MEM (Gibco, MA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Silencer® Select Negative Control siRNA (siCTRL; Invitrogen, CA) and siGENOME SMARTpool Human SERBP1 siRNA (siSERBP1; Dharmacon, CO) were used as negative control and targeting constructs, respectively.


### Analysis of drug synergism:
  Four conditions were tested in triplicate: siCTRL + vehicle, siSERBP1 + vehicle, siCTRL + Torin 1 (MedChemExpress, NJ), and siSERBP1 + Torin 1. SERBP1-knockdown was performed via transfection with 20 nM (U251) or 15 nM (U343) siRNA and 0.2 μL (U251) or 0.15 μL (U343) RNAiMAX in 10 μL Opti MEM per well, incubated for 20–30 min at room temperature, to which 1,500 U251 or 2,500 U343 cells in 40 μL DMEM+10%FBS were added. Subsequently, either 50 μL of pure medium as negative control or 50 μL of 100 nM Torin 1 in DMEM+10% FBS (50 nM final concentration) were added to the wells. Cell proliferation was followed over seven days using an Incucyte SX5 and accompanying Incucyte 2022A Rev1 Software (Sartorius, Germany). Statistical significance of differences in cell confluency between single and combined treatments at 150 hours were calculated by ordinary one-way ANOVA using Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, MA). The synergistic effect of the combined treatment of siSERBP1 and Torin 1 was evaluated with the combination index (CI)49,50 with the following formula, where E is the measured cell confluency for the given condition normalized to the control:  (3) CI=E(siSERBP+Torin1)E(siSERBP1)×E(siCTRL=Torin1)

  A CI less than 1 (<1), equal to 1 (=1), or greater than 1 (>1) indicates a synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effect of the combination treatment, respectively.


### Western blot:
  Cell pellets were re-suspended and sonicated in Laemmli sample buffer, separated on SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in TBS-T + 5% milk and then probed with the primary antibodies. HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was used after washing with TBST. Bound antibodies were detected using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore). Primary antibodies used: SERBP1(Santa Cruz, SC100800), mTOR (Cell Signaling, 2972S), Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T8328).


### Reporter vectors and luciferase assays:
  The 5’UTR of mTOR was cloned by PCR into pLenti-5’UTR - Luciferase vector from ABM (CAT# m036). The two G4 sequences were deleted to generate the ΔG4 construct. 293T cells were plated into 96-well plates and transfected with the mTOR 5′UTR wild type or ΔG4 luciferase reporter vectors using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, MA) along with SERBP1 siRNA or control siRNA. Firefly luciferase luminescence was measured 48 h later using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, MA).


## Results

### SERBP1 binds RNA G4s through C-terminus RGG box and successive residues.
  SERBP1 has recently been reported to bind RNA G-quadruplexes (rG4s)6,7, although the detailed mechanisms of these interactions were not investigated. We previously showed using an RNA-compete assay that SERBP1 binds GC-rich RNA sequences21 and that this binding occurs through its C-terminal RGG domain, as well as through key C-terminal residues5. To confirm that the 7mer oligonucleotide sequence used in that study (GCGCGGG) folds as a G4, we recorded circular dichroism (CD) spectra showing characteristic negative and positive peaks at 230 nm and 260 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The NMR 1H spectrum showed imino peaks between 10 and 12 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 1B), which are indicative of Hoogsteen base pairs, although extra peaks around 13 ppm suggest the presence of other species such as double-stranded oligonucleotide. These results confirm that this 7mer oligonucleotide contains a population of parallel G4 under the experimental conditions. To further characterize SERBP1–G4 interactions, we compared binding to a model DNA G4 called Pu20m2 and its RNA homolog rPu20m2. Pu20m2 is derived from the BLM gene promoter and its structure was previously solved by NMR spectroscopy51 (Fig. 1A). Both Pu20m2 and rPu20m2 formed parallel G4s, as confirmed by CD spectroscopy and 1H NMR (Supplementary Fig. 2), although transitioning from DNA to RNA yielded significant broadening of the imino peaks, suggesting greater structural heterogeneity for the RNA sequence. We then carried out 1H,15N-HSQC titrations with these two G4s. Similar to the 7mer5, we observed CSPs in C-terminal residues T388, D389, A393, S394, A395, D397, and V398, with comparable Δδmax amplitude for Pu20m2 and rPu20m2, and severe broadening of the RGG motif residues (Fig. 1B), suggesting a conserved binding mechanism.

  Next, we measured the thermodynamic parameters of the two complexes by ITC and obtained dissociation constants of the same order of magnitude for both G4s, with Kd values of ~ 2 μM and 1 μM for Pu20m2 and rPu20m2, respectively (Fig. 1C), confirming that SERBP1 binds DNA and RNA G-quadruplexes with similar affinity. Interestingly, we observed different enthalpic and entropic behavior for the two complexes, with ΔH values of −45 kJ·mol−1 and −100 kJ·mol−1, as well as ΔS values of −42 J·mol−1·K−1 and −221 J·mol−1·K−1 for DNA and RNA, respectively. These results suggest that, although the affinities for the two parallel G4s are similar, interactions between SERBP1 and RNA G4s are more thermodynamically favorable than with DNA G4s, perhaps due to additional hydrogen-bonding interactions with the 2’OH group in RNA. However, measurements under unfolding conditions in the presence of Li+ (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed a dramatic loss of affinity of more than two orders of magnitude (Kd ~ 696 μM), confirming previous reports that SERBP1 prefers binding structured oligonucleotides5,6.


### Phosphorylation of key serine residues modulates SERBP1–G4 interactions.
  To further characterize SERBP1–G4 interactions, we designed mutants to assess the relative contribution of different regions to Pu20m2 binding using EMSA and fluorescence polarization (FP) (Fig. 2A). Deletion of the entire binding region (SERBP1ΔRGG+ΔC) abolished the band shift (Fig. 2B), confirming that the C-terminal domain is essential for the interaction. Conversely, single point mutation of serine 394 (S394G), which consistently shows the strongest CSP with all G4s during NMR titrations, did not significantly alter gel shift (Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating that this serine alone is insufficient to drive the interaction. Deletion of all C-terminal residues after the RGG box (SERBP1ΔC) did not abolish the interaction with Pu20m2 by gel shift (Fig. 2B), although FP assays revealed a higher dissociation constant (36 μM compared with 19 μM for SERBP1189–400, Table 1), indicating reduced affinity (Fig. 2C). In contrast, deletion of the RGG box while retaining the C-terminal domain (SERBP1ΔRGG) resulted in a substantial drop in affinity (Kd ~ 658 μM). Together, these results are consistent with our NMR data and demonstrate that electrostatic interactions mediated by the RGG box arginines constitute the primary binding mode between SERBP1 and G4s, while C-terminal residues play a secondary role in stabilizing the interaction.

  Next, we assayed the effect of phosphorylation on SERBP1–G4 interactions. SERBP1 has been reported to be phosphorylated on several serine residues by protein kinase Cε17, including S386, one of the residues showing significant CSPs in our NMR titrations. Using PhosphoSitePlus52, we identified additional phosphorylation sites in this region: T388, S391, S392, and S394. To mimic phosphorylation, we designed two constructs with serine-to-glutamate mutations: SERBP12SE (S386, S394) and SERBP14SE (S386, S391, S392, S394) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 1). Both mutants showed reduced shifts in EMSAs compared to SERBP1189–400 (Fig. 2D), as well as decreased affinity with Kd of ~ 46 and 84 μM for the 2SE and 4SE mutants, respectively (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrate that introducing negative charge at these positions reduces SERBP1 affinity for G4s, suggesting that phosphorylation may serve as a mechanism for modulating SERBP1–nucleic acid interactions.


### SERBP1 G4-binding domain wraps around G-quadruplexes.
  To obtain structural details of SERBP1–G4 interactions, we performed MD and in silico docking simulations on SERBP1366–400, the C-terminal 35 amino acids that constitute the primary G4-binding site. We simulated a 1 μs trajectory for both SERBP1366–400 and SERBP1366–400,4SE to assess the effects of phosphorylation on domain architecture. SERBP1366−400 showed multiple transient intramolecular contacts between residues within and adjacent the RGG motif (Fig. 3A), while over time, the ensemble progressively favored more compact conformers (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, SERBP1366–400,4SE remained extended for the course of the simulation, with only a single contact observed between R384 and D389 (Supplementary Figs. 6A,B). The average radius of gyration (~ 12 Å for SERBP1366–400 vs. ~ 20 Å for SERBP1366–400,4SE) confirms that the four negative charges introduced by phosphomimetic mutations prevent ensemble compaction (Fig. 3B).

  We then used HADDOCK48 to dock SERBP1 to Pu20m2, whose structure was previously solved by NMR51. MD trajectories were input as conformational ensembles with NMR CSP data as restraints and all nucleotides were defined as active residues. HADDOCK generated 10 clusters, all showing SERBP1366–400 encircling the G4 at varying positions (Fig. 3C), indicating that SERBP1 recognizes G4 topology rather than specific nucleotides. Electrostatic interactions between arginine side chains and phosphate groups were present in all clusters (Fig. 3D), with R378 being the only arginine consistently engaged. Hydrogen bonds also formed between serine residues and phosphate groups (Fig. 3E). In the SERBP1366–400,4SE–Pu20m2 complex, the negative charges promote an extended conformation that prevents SERBP1 from encircling the G4, reducing affinity (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Although electrostatic contacts persisted (Supplementary Fig. 6D), hydrogen bonds between C-terminal residues and nucleotides were absent, further contributing to the reduced affinity of this mutant. These results reveal a binding mechanism in which the RGG domain and C-terminal residues together encircle the quadruplex. The RGG domain drives recognition through electrostatic interactions, while C-terminal serines provide additional stabilization through hydrogen bonding with the phosphate backbone. Phosphorylation of these serines promotes extended conformations that prevent encircling and eliminates hydrogen bonding, reducing overall affinity.


### SERBP1 adopts a general mechanism for endogenous G4 targets.
  To determine whether this binding mechanism applies to other SERBP1 targets, we selected G-rich sequences from the 5’ UTRs of mTOR and HDAC3 mRNAs, which were previously identified as endogenous SERBP1 targets by RIP, or CLIP analysis12,17,21 (Supplementary Tables 2, 3). CD and 1H NMR confirmed that these sequences adopt G4 conformations (Supplementary Fig. 7). We then performed 1H,15N-HSQC titrations of SERBP1189–400 with these rG4s, as well as with artificial rG4s previously reported to interact with SERBP1 (TPG4, rAGA6). All spectra showed severe broadening of the RGG region and significant CSPs in the C-terminal residues (Fig. 4A). Consistent with titrations using Pu20m2 and rPu20m2 (Fig 1B), perturbed residues include hydrophilic residues capable of hydrogen bonding (S394, D397) and surrounding hydrophobic residues (A393, A395, V398). S394 and A395 showed the highest Δδmax values, and the magnitude and direction of their peak shifts were nearly identical across all rG4s, indicating a common binding mode (Fig. 4B). Notably, severe broadening occurred at G4:protein ratios as low as 1:4 (Supplementary Fig. 8), not only in the RGG repeat region but also in other regions previously shown to have fast transverse relaxation (R2) rates5. This broadening may result from slower rotational correlation times due to larger complex size or reduced flexibility of disordered regions upon binding, or both. These results demonstrate that SERBP1 recognizes G-quadruplexes through a general mechanism involving two types of interactions: electrostatic contacts between RGG arginines and phosphate groups, and hydrogen bonds from C-terminal residues.


### SERBP1 regulates mTOR expression by recognizing G-quadruplexes in the 5’UTR.
  mTOR is a key regulator of cell growth and metabolism, and activation of its pathway is frequently observed in cancers, particularly glioblastoma53. Given that SERBP1 is overexpressed in glioblastoma and contributes to its development21, we investigated whether SERBP1 regulates mTOR expression. SERBP1 knockdown in glioblastoma U251 and U343 cells reduced mTOR levels (Figs. 5A and B), yet previous RNA-seq analyses showed no changes in mTOR mRNA levels21, suggesting that SERBP1 regulates mTOR at the translation level. G4 sequences in the mTOR 5’UTR were predicted by different platforms and experimentally identified using RG4-seq54,55 (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 3). To test whether this regulation depends on G4 sequences in the mTOR 5’ UTR, we performed luciferase assays using reporters containing either the wild-type 5’UTR or a version with G4 sequences deleted (ΔG4). SERBP1 knockdown reduced the luciferase activity of the wild-type reporter but had no significant effect on the ΔG4 reporter, demonstrating that SERBP1 requires the G4 elements in the mTOR 5’UTR to regulate its expression (Fig. 5D).

  To assess the relevance of mTOR regulation by SERBP1 and possible therapeutic applications, we in-quired whether SERBP1 knockdown increases sensitivity to mTOR inhibitors. We performed a partial SERBP1-knockdown in U251 and U343 glioblastoma cells and treated cultures with either DMSO (control) or 50 nM Torin 1, a well-characterized mTOR inhibitor, monitoring cell proliferation over 7 days. In both cell lines SERBP1 knockdown reduced cell growth similarly to Torin 1, with confluence at 150 h between 60% and 80%. However, combining Torin 1 with SERBP1 knockdown reduced confluence to 30–40% significantly below treatment alone (Figs. E-H). To quantify this interaction, we calculated the combination index (CI) at 150 h, where values <1, =1, or >1 indicate synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effects, respectively50. Both cell lines yielded CI <1, confirming that SERBP1 reduction and mTOR inhibition act synergistically. Consistent with this, SERBP1 knockdown significantly decreased mTOR protein levels in both lines (Figs. 5A and B), suggesting that SERBP1 promotes mTOR expression and that its depletion sensitizes glioblastoma cells to mTOR-targeted therapy.


## Discussion
  Here, we characterized the molecular mechanisms of SERBP1–G4 recognition and demonstrated its functional relevance in translational regulation, one of several cellular processes in which SERBP1 participates. Building on our previous finding that SERBP1 binds RNA primarily through its second RGG box and C-terminal region5, this work reveals the structural and biological features that underpin SERBP1–G4 interactions. Dissociation constants for RNA and DNA versions of the same parallel G4 were comparable, though thermodynamic parameters suggest a preference for RNA G4s. This apparent equivalence in affinity may reflect the structural heterogeneity of rG4s observed by 1H NMR, multiple protein–G4 complex conformations with varying stabilities could yield an aggregate affinity similar to that of more homogeneous dG4–protein complexes, while masking underlying differences in binding kinetics. Regardless, these findings are consistent with the nature of SERBP1 as an RNA-binding protein.

  Deletion and mutagenesis experiments established the critical role of C-terminal serine residues in mediating G4 interactions. Conservative point mutations of these serines had little effect on binding, whereas phosphomimetic substitutions decreased affinity, indicating that the contribution of these residues depends on their modification state rather than their mere presence (Fig. 6). Notably, these same serines have been identified as phosphorylation sites in phosphoproteomic studies of breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer tumors56–58, suggesting that phosphorylation-dependent modulation of SERBP1–G4 binding may be operative in disease contexts.

  MD and docking simulations of the SERBP1 C-terminal binding domain (SERBP1366–400) with a G4 revealed an encircling mechanism in which SERBP1 locks around the G4 without selectivity for specific nucleotides. This wrapping mechanism is uncommon among RNA-binding proteins, despite the prevalence of RGG repeat domains as RNA-binding motifs. One explanation may lie in the size of the RGG repeats themselves. The SERBP1 RGG motif comprises approximately 20 residues, whereas other proteins harbor considerably larger RGG regions. For instance, FUS (Fused in Sarcoma), contains three RGG motifs of roughly 100, 50, and 40 residues, respectively, and requires RRM domains to achieve high-affinity RNA binding59. The comparatively short length of the SERBP1 RGG domain, combined with a dense hydrogen bond network contributed by C-terminal serines, may enable a locking mechanism that yields low-micromolar affinity without the need for additional RNA-binding domains. The preference for compact conformers likely enables SERBP1 to form multiple contact points with the G4, increasing complex stability. Such behavior has been observed for several IDP–DNA complexes60 but rarely with quadruplexes. Although intrinsically disordered regions have been shown to bind G-quadruplexes61,62 and drive liquid-liquid phase separation63, this study is the first to describe molecular details of G4 recognition by a single intrinsically disordered domain across a range of G4 sequences. From these data, we propose that the organization of this motif, including the defined length of the RGG box and its close proximity to the C-terminal serine residues has evolved to specifically recognize parallel-folded G4s (Fig. 6).

  Phosphorylation of the C-terminal serine residues may act as a regulatory switch for SERBP1-mediated translation. Many IDPs undergo phosphorylation that leads to structural changes, altered function, or modulation of phase separation64,65. In the case of SERBP1, the introduction of phosphomimetic substitutions promotes an extended conformation that disrupts the encircling mechanism while simultaneously introducing charge repulsion between the phosphorylated residues and the G4 backbone. Notably, because SERBP1 binds G4s with low-micromolar affinity, even modest perturbations to the binding interface may be sufficient to tip the bound/unbound equilibrium toward dissociation, amplifying the biological impact of phosphorylation beyond what the measured affinity change alone might suggest.

  NMR titrations confirmed that SERBP1 engages rG4s through the combined action of its second RGG box and adjacent C-terminal residues, achieving a binding affinity that is uncommon for disordered regions, which typically exhibit only transient interactions66. Although folding-upon-binding has been reported for other disordered RGG domains, notably in FMRP61, neither our NMR data nor our MD simulations provide evidence for such a transition in SERBP1, suggesting that the encircling mechanism we describe represents an entirely distinct mode of G4 recognition by a disordered domain. Extensive line broadening in the NMR spectra, likely arising from intermediate exchange and/or conformational heterogeneity of the bound states, is consistent with SERBP1 remaining disordered upon binding. Importantly, binding was largely independent of nucleotide sequence composition across all rG4s tested, with remarkably similar patterns of broadened peaks. This lack of sequence selectivity is consistent with our docking analysis and supports recent findings that non-canonical RNA-binding domains like those of SERBP1 rarely exhibit sequence specificity67. The small variations in chemical shift perturbation amplitude observed between sequences likely reflect differences in G4 topology, such as loop size or number of guanine tetrads, rather than sequence-dependent recognition. This interpretation also reconciles our previously reported titrations with small RNA sequences5, in which AU-rich sequences failed to interact due to their inability to form quadruplexes, while GC-rich sequences formed stable intermolecular quadruplexes that were readily bound.

  These findings position SERBP1 as a regulator of mTOR translation through direct recognition of G-quadruplex structures in the mTOR 5’ UTR. Although G4 sequences had been predicted in mTOR mRNA68, this is the first identification of mTOR G4s as direct targets of an RNA-binding protein, linking SERBP1 overexpression in glioblastoma21 to the elevated mTOR signaling characteristic of this cancer type69. G4s in the 5’ and 3’ UTRs of mRNAs are known to influence translation, and their resolution by helicases such as DHX36 and DHX9 represents a key regulatory step70–72. Notably, proteomic studies have shown that the primary interactors of SERBP1 are helicases, including DDX5, DDX21, DDX3X, and DHX919. Taken together, these observations suggest that SERBP1 functions as a G4 adaptor protein, recognizing and binding G4 structures in target mRNAs and recruiting helicases to resolve them, thereby licensing translation. In the case of mTOR, this adaptor model would explain how SERBP1 facilitates translation from a G4-containing 5’ UTR and why its depletion reduces mTOR protein levels without altering mRNA abundance (Fig. 6).

  The regulatory implications of SERBP1–G4 recognition likely extend well beyond mTOR. G4 structures in the 5’ UTR regulate translation of numerous genes, including NRAS, VEGF, and BAG-1, through both cap-dependent inhibition and cap-independent initiation73–75. Indeed, the mTOR 5’ UTR itself functions as a highly folded RNA scaffold capable of driving cap-independent translation under conditions of translational stress76. Given that SERBP1 localizes to the 40S–60S ribosomal interface9–11, SERBP1–G4 interactions may facilitate this non-canonical mode of initiation in addition to helicase-mediated resolution. More broadly, SERBP1’s direct role as a PCIF1 cofactor in m6Am modification77, its identification as a putative m6A reader78, and the extensive colocalization of G4-forming sequences and m6A sites in the transcriptome79,80 suggest a link between G4 binding and epitranscriptomic regulation. These observations, together with its dynamic binding to rRNA and snoRNAs across the cell cycle19, indicate that topology-driven RNA recognition is a general feature of SERBP1 biology (Fig. 6).

  G-quadruplexes are increasingly recognized as key regulators of translation and attractive therapeutic targets, with small molecules that stabilize G4s27,74,81–83 and aptamers that mimic G4 structures and compete for protein binding84,85 both showing therapeutic promise. By revealing the molecular basis of SERBP1–G4 recognition and establishing SERBP1 as a G4 adaptor in mTOR regulation, this work provides a foundation for developing strategies that target SERBP1–RNA interactions in glioblastoma and other cancers.
