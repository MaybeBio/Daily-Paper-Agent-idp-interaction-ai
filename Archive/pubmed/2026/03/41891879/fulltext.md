# Poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) exhibits ion-dependent structural properties distinct from RNA


## Abstract
  Poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) is a highly charged, intrinsically flexible nucleic-acid-like homopolymer composed of ADP-ribose units. It serves as a critical post-translational modification that is rapidly synthesized in response to DNA damage and participates in diverse important cellular processes, including DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and RNA biogenesis, often by promoting biomolecular phase separation. However, PAR’s conformational heterogeneity coupled with limited experimental characterization has hindered a mechanistic understanding of these processes. To address this challenge, we develop a five-bead coarse-grained model of PAR and perform molecular dynamics simulations to compare its conformational ensemble and ion atmosphere with those of RNA across diverse ion environments. Our simulations reveal that PAR undergoes a markedly stronger structural transition than RNA in response to divalent ions, driven by its preferential ion binding and effective ion-mediated bridging between phosphate groups. These interactions facilitate cooperative conformational changes and produce a compact, ion-rich atmosphere that is fundamentally distinct from RNA. Together, our work provides a physically grounded model for PAR, uncovers molecular features that differentiate PAR from RNA, and offers mechanistic insight into how PAR nucleates phase separation and organizes protein interactions at sites of DNA damage.


## Introduction
  Poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) is a nucleic acid-like homopolymer consisting of repeating adenosine diphosphate ribose (ADP-ribose) units (Fig. 1A), first identified in the seminal work of Chambon and colleagues [1, 2]. PAR is a rapidly synthesized post-translational modification induced in response to DNA damage, [3, 4] where it orchestrates a wide range of cellular processes including DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, RNA biogenesis, protein condensation, intracellular organization, and cell-cycle regulation [5–14]. Dysregulation of PAR metabolism is associated with numerous pathological conditions such as neurodegeneration (e.g. Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s) [15–20], cancer [21], and autoimmune diseases [22]. Under cellular stress, PAR chains of varying lengths and branching are synthesized through a process known as PARylation [6, 23–25], enabling cells to adapt to changing physiological conditions. Significant efforts have been made to understand PARylation, PAR-mediated signaling, stress responses, and the cellular roles of PAR, including its involvement in both normal and aberrant functions [4, 6, 25–29].

  Remarkably, PAR shares a striking chemical similarity with classical nucleic acids, particularly polyriboadenylic acid (poly-rA) (Fig. 1A). This resemblance raises a fundamental biological question: given that RNA is orders of magnitude more abundant in cells (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim \mu$\end{document}M) than PAR (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim$\end{document} nM [30]) and shares nearly identical chemical building blocks, why does the cell synthesize a distinct polymer, PAR, instead of repurposing RNA for the same functions? Although the high cellular availability of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^+$\end{document}) as a precursor and the inducible nature of PAR synthesis during stress provide important biochemical context, the extent to which PAR’s unique structural and physicochemical properties distinguish its functions from those of RNA remains poorly understood.

  Although existing experimental techniques [31–34] have provided valuable insights into PAR’s structure across different lengths and ionic conditions, they remain challenged by PAR’s intrinsic dynamics and the difficulties associated with its synthesis. Moreover, because PAR contains twice as many phosphate groups as an RNA of equivalent length (Fig. 1A), its conformation is expected to be highly sensitive to ionic conditions. Experimental studies [31, 32] have indeed observed chain-length and ion-dependent structural transitions in PAR, yet these measurements are limited by spatiotemporal resolution. As a result, the microscopic features of PAR structure and the molecular mechanisms by which PAR-ion interactions drive these transitions remain elusive. Computer simulations offer a powerful route to interrogate PAR-ion interactions and capture its structural behavior at atomic detail; however, conventional atomistic simulations face two major limitations: (i) existing force fields are insufficiently accurate for PAR’s unusual chemical topology [35], and (ii) the broad, heterogeneous conformational landscape of PAR is difficult to sample within feasible timescales [32].

  To overcome these challenges, we employ a coarse-grained (CG) modeling strategy. We introduce the first CG model that explicitly incorporates ion-PAR interactions using the Reference Interaction Site Model (RISM) [36] with an implicit solvent representation. This framework reduces the degrees of freedom of both PAR and the surrounding solvent while accurately accounting for ions and their solvation structure, enabling efficient exploration of PAR’s large conformational landscape under diverse ionic environments (monovalent ions, Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}, and Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}). Using this approach, we systematically compare PAR chains of varying lengths, uncovering a striking length-dependent structural transition near physiological ion concentrations: short chains adopt extended conformations, whereas increasing chain length drives a progression from extended to Gaussian coil to compact globular states. These results indicate that PAR’s structural and functional behaviors are governed primarily by its length and interactions with ions rather than sequence [32], distinguishing it from classical nucleic acids. By tuning PAR length, cells may thus generate a continuum of PAR architectures that modulate PAR’s affinity and specificity for distinct DNA-repair and signaling proteins. Moreover, the corresponding changes in ion atmosphere, ion binding, and ion bridging have direct implications for PAR’s phase separation propensities [7, 13, 37], and ultimately for the physicochemical properties of the PAR-rich condensates.

  Given PAR’s close chemical relationship to nucleic acids, we further compare its structure and ion atmosphere with those of homopolymeric RNAs such as poly-rA and polyribouridylic acid (poly-rU), all sharing the same net charge. Despite carrying the same charge, PAR features a heterogeneous charge distribution (two phosphate groups clustered per monomer) whereas RNA carries a single phosphate per nucleotide. This variation in charge blockiness promotes ion-mediated long-range bridging in PAR and results in distinct ion sensitivities, ion–phosphate interaction patterns, counterion distributions, and structural transitions. These differences help explain why cells preferentially deploy PAR, rather than RNA, for specific biological functions [9, 11]. Our work thus establishes a framework for understanding how PAR serves as a highly charged, multivalent scaffold [37–39] that can effectively compete with DNA and RNA for interacting with nucleic acid-binding proteins central to DNA damage repair and other essential cellular processes.

### PAR is devoid of helical structure

In contrast, medium sized chains such as PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{20}$\end{document} behave as Gaussian random coils (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\nu \approx 0.5$\end{document}), while longer PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document} and PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{60}$\end{document} even fold up to globular structures at elevated Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} conditions (Fig. 2B and D). The Kratky plots show the same trend (Supplementary Fig. S3B). As a result, the degree of collapse becomes highly dependent on PAR length. The greater compaction in longer chains observed here is in line with experiments [32] and likely arises from intrinsic chain stiffness [70]. In summary, our result suggests that PAR compaction is strongly influenced by ion concentration, valency, and chain length.

  Collapse of nucleic acids is typically driven by base stacking interactions [51, 61, 71]. However, due to its lengthening backbone compared to RNA, the bases in PAR are relatively far away from each other, which weakens stacking interactions. To explore the origin of PAR collapse, we first check the potential for base stacking by calculating the relative distance and orientation between consecutive bases in PAR. Figure 2C shows the consecutive bases indeed remain far away from each other (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R_\mathrm{base-base}$\end{document} is primarily between 10 and 20 Å), and are randomly oriented (almost uniform angle distribution). This indicates that PAR forms minimal base stacking, and thus their contributions to PAR collapse at high ion concentrations are minor. In addition, the contact map (Fig. 2F) shows that most contacts are along the diagonal, local and uniform, suggesting these contacts are non-specific and Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} dependent (Fig. 2E).


### Comprehensive mapping of PAR ion atmosphere
  Since the collapse of PAR is not driven by base stacking and highly dependent on Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentration, we posit that ion-mediated interactions likely promote these contact formation, ultimately leading to PAR compaction. To quantify ion-mediated interactions in PAR, we calculate the radial distribution function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_\mathrm{Mg-P}$\end{document}, between Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} and phosphate groups. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_\mathrm{Mg-P}$\end{document} exhibits two peaks at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r \approx$\end{document} 2.5 and 5.0 Å (Fig. 3A), indicating two distinct binding modes: direct ion-phosphate interactions (inner-sphere, IS) and water-mediated interactions (outer-sphere, OS) [36, 52, 72] (illustrated in Fig. 3B). Notably, the populations of both IS and OS bindings increase with PAR length, indicating a stronger ion binding affinity for longer chains (Fig. 3A). As a result, the number of excess ions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document}, which quantifies how many ions are attracted toward the chain [73], is consistently higher for longer chains (Supplementary Fig. S4). Additionally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document} increases at high Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations for all PAR lengths. Thus, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_\mathrm{Mg-P}$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document} confirm that longer PAR chains attract more ions, likely contributing to their higher degree of collapse. Moreover, the ion concentration converges to bulk values (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_{Mg-P}(r) \rightarrow 1$\end{document}) at larger distances for longer PARs (Fig. 3A, inset), suggesting that the ion atmosphere of longer chain is more extended.

  In addition to these IS/OS ions shown in Fig. 3A, a significant population of diffusely interacting ions exists, which contributes to the long range decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_\mathrm{Mg-P}$\end{document} and is often referred to as diffuse ions (Fig. 3B, right). As in previous work [52], we partition the PAR ion atmosphere into IS (0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $< r_{Mg-P} <$\end{document} 3.2 Å), OS (3.2 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\le r_{Mg-P} <$\end{document} 6.1 Å), and diffuse ions (Fig. 3E). The number of diffuse ions are then calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $N_{Mg^{2+}}^D = \Gamma _{Mg^{2+}} - N_{Mg^{2+}}^{IS} - N_{Mg^{2+}}^{OS}$\end{document}. At low Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations (0.5–1 mM), ions mostly bind in the IS and OS regions across all PAR lengths (Fig. 3 E), suggesting a compact ion atmosphere. At higher ion concentrations (2–5 mM), the ion binding pattern becomes length-dependent. For short PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{10}$\end{document}, diffuse ions contribute the most (D \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $>$\end{document} OS \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $>$\end{document} IS), supporting the idea that the ion atmosphere expands at higher ion concentrations. Remarkably, longer chains such as PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document} still show a slight preference for OS ions, indicating a smaller shift from IS to OS (instead of diffuse) as PAR length increases, reflecting a stronger affinity for bound ions of longer chains.

  Due to a large number of bound ions in IS and OS, some of them could bind simultaneously to two (or more) phosphate groups, acting as bridges and thus facilitating secondary and tertiary interactions, leading to the collapse (Fig. 3C and D). Such bridging ions have been found to play important roles in folding and function of many nucleic acids such as ribozymes [36, 41, 52, 65, 69, 74]. Indeed, we find that the number of bridging ions increases with PAR chain length (Fig. 3D), with PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{20}$\end{document} having roughly twice the bridging ions of PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{10}$\end{document} while PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document} having 7–8 times more. This trend is observed across all bridging modes (IS–IS, IS–OS, and OS–OS). In PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{10}$\end{document}, where the number of bridging ions is low, the chain remains extended (Fig. 2B). By contrast, higher numbers of bridging ions in PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{20}$\end{document} and PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document} drive structural transitions to Gaussian and globular states (Fig. 3D), reinforcing that ion bridging drives structural collapse of PAR.


### PAR collapse is distinct from RNA
  Although PAR shares the same chemical building blocks as poly-rA (Fig. 1A), and poly-rA is abundant in cells [75], nature selectively uses PAR for a wide range of specialized functions [13, 20, 39, 76]. To understand why, we compare the structural properties of PAR with poly-rA and poly-rU. Given that PAR contains twice the number of phosphate groups (Fig. 1A), we select rU40 and rA40 to match the phosphate count in PAR20.

  Similar to PAR, both rU40 and rA40 collapse at high Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations (Fig. 4A) [51, 61]. However, the extent of compaction is much greater for PAR, as the change in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R_g$\end{document} drops from 34% for PAR to 22% for the two RNAs. Moreover, the transition is steeper for PAR, indicating a higher degree of cooperativity relative to RNA. Importantly, PAR collapses at a much lower critical Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentration than RNA (0.1 mM versus 1 mM), highlighting PAR’s increased sensitivity to divalent ions (Fig. 4A). The Kratky plots further demonstrate the sharp structural difference between PAR and RNA at high Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S5). Even at 5 mM Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}, while both RNA sequences remain in an unfolded/extended state, PAR adopts a Gaussian/globule-like structure at the same condition. Similarly, the Flory exponent reveals that PAR collapses to a Gaussian random coil (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\nu \approx$\end{document} 0.5) [33, 35], while the two RNAs can only reach a SAW-like state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\nu \approx$\end{document} 0.6) at high Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations (Fig. 4B and C). This suggests a more pronounced structural transition in PAR compared to RNA. Contrary to PAR, which shows minimal base-stacking, both RNAs populate stacked conformations where two consecutive bases are close and aligned to each other (Supplementary Fig. S6) and thus display transient helical conformations, especially for rA\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document} (Supplementary Fig. S7).

  Remarkably, the structural differences between PAR and RNA are highly dependent on ion valency (Na\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^+$\end{document}versus Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}). While PAR remains more extended than RNA in monovalent ions (Supplementary Fig. S8), it collapses to a much more compact state under the presence of Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} (Fig. 4A). This contrasting behavior between PAR and RNA with respect to ion valency aligns with experimental findings showing that PAR requires lower Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} but higher monovalent ion concentrations than RNA to achieve the same degree of collapse [31].


### PAR attracts more ions than RNAs with distinct ion atmosphere
  Due to its much smaller size compared to RNA, the ion atmosphere of PAR is also highly compact. This can be seen by significantly higher peaks for both IS and OS regions compared to RNA (Fig. 4D, top), suggesting that PAR attracts more ions. As a result, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document} per phosphate for PAR is consistently higher than RNAs (Fig. 4D, bottom). This behavior can be attributed to the two phosphate groups next to each other in PAR, boosting its charge blockiness compared to RNA.

  Strikingly, despite attracting more ions, the overall ion atmosphere of PAR is more compact than RNA, with a crossover between them occurring at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r \approx 45$\end{document} Å (Fig. 4D, inset). The local Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentration around PAR decays to bulk values (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $g_{Mg-P} \rightarrow 1$\end{document}) more rapidly than around RNA, indicating that ions are confined closer to the PAR backbone. PAR achieves this primarily through enhanced IS and OS binding, with diffuse ions playing a minor role except at high concentrations (Fig. 4E, left). In contrast, for RNA sequences, Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} predominantly interacts via diffuse ions, consistent with previous studies [36, 52, 53] (Fig. 4E, right and Supplementary Fig. S10C, right). Moreover, the number of IS and OS ions is significantly higher for PAR compared to RNA, emphasizing the role of sequence in shaping the ion atmosphere. Our previous simulations [53] have shown that the extent of the RNA ion atmosphere depends strongly on its structural complexity, with disordered RNAs exhibiting a more expanded ion atmosphere than folded RNAs. In this regard, PAR’s ion atmosphere resembles that of folded RNAs, even though PAR itself is structurally disordered. We attribute this behavior to the different charge blockiness of PAR, which enhances electrostatic attraction and draws counterions closer to the chain compared to disordered RNAs.


### Ion identity affects PAR and RNA differently
  We next examine how monovalent ion concentration (from 20 to 100 mM) and divalent ion identity (Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}versus Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}) modulate the ion atmosphere of PAR and RNA. As monovalent ion concentration increases, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document} decreases for both molecules due to enhanced screening of phosphate charges (Supplementary Fig. S9B, left). In RNA, this leads to reduced IS binding (Supplementary Fig. S9A, left). In contrast, PAR shows the opposite behavior: despite a lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma _{Mg^{2+}}$\end{document}, IS binding increases at higher monovalent ion concentrations. This 1–2 fold increase is coupled with a twofold reduction in diffuse ions (Supplementary Fig. S10B, left), reflecting a redistribution from diffuse to IS binding. This shift arises from a modest collapse induced by monovalent ions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx$\end{document}15%; Supplementary Figs S11 and S12A), which enhances nucleotide accessibility for ion bridging and increases the fraction of bound ions. By comparison, RNA shows a uniform decrease in all ion populations (IS, OS, and D) at elevated Na\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^+$\end{document} concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S10B, middle-right). The observation that IS binding increases in PAR but decreases slightly in RNA reflects intrinsic differences in size and thus charge density between the two molecules. PAR, being smaller in size and consequently more charge-dense, attracts a larger number of bound ions for charge neutralization (Supplementary Fig. S12A), whereas the larger and less charge-dense RNA requires fewer. Moreover, the charge blockiness in PAR further favors IS binding with ions, in contrast to RNA, which prefers a diffuse ion atmosphere.

  Switching the divalent ion from Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} to Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} leaves the overall ion binding, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Gamma$\end{document}, unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S9B, right). However, Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} displays a stronger preference for IS over OS binding in both PAR and RNA (Supplementary Fig. S10D). Unlike monovalent ion, this enhancement in IS binding occurs without structural collapse (Supplementary Fig. S12B). Instead, it stems from the lower free energy barrier [36, 52, 53, 65] for the OS-to-IS transition in Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} relative to Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}. Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}’s larger size and lower charge density facilitate water release from its first solvation shell, making direct phosphate coordination more favorable.


### Ion-mediated long-ranged bridging underlies PAR collapse
  We suspected that the preference for IS/OS binding in PAR, as opposed to diffuse binding in RNA, is due to the ion bridging. We indeed observe that PAR has 4–6 times more bridging ions than RNA (Fig. 5B, left), indicating that despite carrying the same net charge, PAR’s blocky charge pattern facilitates bridging more effectively than RNA. Bridging ions are categorized as IS–IS, IS–OS, or OS–OS based on ion-phosphate distances (Fig. 5A). Across all Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations and both sequences, OS–OS bridging is most abundant, followed by IS–OS and IS–IS (Fig. 5B, left). Bringing two phosphate groups into close proximity to the ion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lesssim 3.2$\end{document} Å) incurs an entropic penalty, making IS–IS bridging less favorable than IS–OS and OS–OS modes. We also observe a small fraction of ions that bridge more than two phosphate groups in PAR, whereas such higher-order bridging is negligible in RNA (Supplementary Figs S13 and S14).

  Not only does PAR have a larger number of bridging ions, but these ions also bridge phosphate groups over greater contour distances compared to RNA. To quantify this, we calculate the contour separation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $L_c$\end{document}) between bridged phosphate pairs (Fig. 5C). In PAR, Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} can bridge phosphate groups separated by >60 Å away, whereas bridging in RNA is limited to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx 40$\end{document} Å (Fig. 5C and D). For PAR, the populations of long-range bridging are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx$\end{document}15%, 27%, and 32% for IS–IS, IS–OS, and OS–OS modes, respectively, while the corresponding RNA values are substantially lower (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx$\end{document}5%, 23%, and 22%). These results demonstrate that Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document}-mediated long-range bridging is significantly more favorable in PAR, owing to its blocky charge distribution, and that these interactions strongly facilitate chain collapse. Increasing number of bridging ions progressively shift the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R_g$\end{document} distribution toward smaller values, confirming their central role in driving PAR collapse (Supplementary Fig. S15). RNA, on the other hand, accommodates far fewer bridging ions overall (Supplementary Fig. S16), resulting in a substantially weaker global collapse (Fig. 4A).

  Replacing Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} with Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} increases the total number of bridging ions for both PAR and RNAs (Fig. 5B). For PAR, this increase arises mainly from enhanced IS–IS and IS–OS bridging, while OS–OS bridging remains largely unchanged. Strikingly, the opposite trend is observed for two RNAs, where the increase in bridging occurs predominantly through OS–OS bridging. Despite these differences in the magnitude and mode of bridging, the overall bridging pattern remains nonspecific and uniformly distributed along the backbone across all examined conditions (Supplementary Figs S17–S19).


## Discussion and conclusions
  PAR, often attached to proteins as a post-translational modification, is widely available in the cell [77], including the nucleus, cytoplasm, and even extracellular matrix. It is often considered as a “friend in need,” assisting cellular recovery from stress through the phase separation of DNA/RNA-binding proteins. However, excessive PAR synthesis (the so-called hyper-PARylation) is detrimental to the cells and could lead to cell death through a pathway called parthanatos [78–81]. In addition, PAR also promotes the aggregation of several proteins [15, 37, 82, 83], including FUS, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\alpha$\end{document}-synuclein, and TDP-43, which are implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Huntington’s disease. Consequently, a detailed understanding of the structural heterogeneity of PAR and its adaptive responses to binding partners under varying physicochemical conditions (ionic strength, temperature, and pH) is essential and may reveal new opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

  In this study, we introduce the first accurate CG model to investigate the structure and ion atmosphere of PAR under diverse ionic conditions. Our simulations provide molecular insights into PAR’s structural transitions and enable direct comparison with RNA, shedding light on two key questions: (i) How can PAR, present only at nM concentrations, effectively compete with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}M RNA to drive protein phase separation, and (ii) Why does the cell synthesize PAR specifically for certain functions rather than repurposing abundant RNA?

  We find that PAR undergoes a sharp structural collapse at ion concentrations nearly ten-fold lower than RNA, demonstrating its greater sensitivity to multivalent cations. Functionally, PAR facilitates protein condensation by acting as a multivalent, highly negatively charged scaffold that strongly engages intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (IDPs/IDRs) through electrostatic interactions [38]. This increased sensitivity allows PAR to trigger phase separation at far lower concentrations than RNA. Consistent with this, cellular PAR level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim$\end{document}nM) [30] is substantially lower than RNA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim \mu$\end{document}M), yet PAR is sufficient to induce phase separation: nM PAR drives FUS droplet formation, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}M RNA is required under comparable conditions [37]. Moreover, PAR can compete with DNA/RNA for binding to nucleic acid-binding proteins. Due to its strong response to multivalent ions, PAR can displace DNA/RNA and recruit proteins that are normally bound to these nucleic acids, thereby facilitating its functions. For example, PAR interacts with DNA-bound histones, disrupting histone–DNA contacts and rendering damaged DNA accessible for repair [39].

  Our work reveals that RNA’s ion atmosphere is far more extended, facilitating long-range interactions that may recruit distant or non-essential proteins. In contrast, PAR’s compact ion atmosphere restricts interactions to nearby, functionally relevant targets, providing a mechanistic explanation for its selective deployment despite its much lower abundance.

  Interestingly, we also demonstrate that Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} exhibits a stronger preference for binding to PAR via the IS coordination. Because IS-bound ions directly contact with phosphate groups, these interactions are substantially stronger than those of OS or diffusive ions. Consequently, we surmise that PAR will display a higher affinity for Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} in a mixed environment. This prediction aligns with in vivo and in vitro observations showing that PAR preferentially recruits Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} to promote biomineralization in the bone extracellular matrix, especially around collagen fibrils where PAR facilitates the formation of spheroid mineral deposits [84, 85]. Such mineralization does not occur with canonical nucleic acids like DNA or RNA, consistent with our finding that RNA exhibits much weaker IS binding. Beyond mineralization, the stronger IS preference of Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} also enhances PAR’s phase separation propensity: in vitro, PAR forms larger liquid-like droplets at much lower Ca\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentrations than Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} [63, 64], similar to trends observed for RNA [86].

  Our analyses across PAR chain lengths reveal that Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} induces different degrees of collapse depending on polymer length. At the same Mg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{2+}$\end{document} concentration, PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{10}$\end{document} remains extended, PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{20}$\end{document} behaves as a random coil, whereas longer PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{40}$\end{document}/PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{60}$\end{document} adopt globular structures. This chain length-dependence is not observed in RNA or DNA, whose structural responses are governed primarily by sequence [51]. Interestingly, no cellular analogs of PAR with alternative nucleotide compositions are known [such as poly(GDP), poly(CDP), or poly(UDP)]. Based on our findings, we propose that PAR tunes its function primarily through the coupling of its chain length and ion interactions. This is supported by experimental evidence showing that PAR’s binding specificity depends strongly on its length [14, 54, 87–92]. Recent work shows that short PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{5}$\end{document} exhibits strong binding preferences for specific DNA- and RNA-binding proteins, while longer PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{20}$\end{document}-PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{50}$\end{document} prefers distinct classes [93].

  We also demonstrate that bridging ions in PAR increases with chain length. Because ion-mediated bridging is a major driver of nucleic acid phase separation [67], longer PAR chains should exhibit a higher propensity for phase separation. Experiments support this view: monomeric and dimeric PAR are unable to induce condensation [37]. PAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_5$\end{document} is the minimum length required, and condensate formation becomes increasingly pronounced with longer chains. This length dependence also influences condensate dynamics and material properties: shorter chains form dynamic, liquid-like droplets, whereas longer chains form more solid-like aggregates [37].

  In cells, PAR is typically branched or hyperbranched, with branching occurring at intervals of 20–50 units [87, 94, 95]. Branching enhances PAR’s role as a multivalent scaffold, presumably facilitating condensate formation for its cellular functions. Understanding how ions influence the structure and ion binding of branched PAR is crucial for correlating these properties with function. Despite its importance, systematic investigation of PAR branching remains experimentally challenging due to the difficulty of synthesizing PAR with well-defined lengths and controlled branching architectures. In this context, our model presented here provides a robust foundation for computationally exploring the structural, ionic, and functional consequences of PAR branching, enabling future studies that would be difficult to access experimentally. It could also provide a tractable framework to explore PAR–protein interactions and the competition between PAR and RNA in the future. Collectively, these results will provide a blueprint for designing sequences with tailored charge blockiness for novel biomaterials, including ion sensors and artificial ion-responsive systems.
