# Manifold of
Polyampholyte Necklaces: From Charge Migration
to Hierarchical Structure


## Abstract
  Single-chain conformations of charge-imbalanced polyampholytes are controlled by an interplay of nonelectrostatic interactions between monomers, defined by the solvent quality, and Coulomb forces between charged monomers, which are sensitive to their primary sequence. Electrostatic interactions manifest themselves as effective short-range attractions between opposite charges and, simultaneously, as long-range repulsions owing to a net global charge of the chain. As a result, polyampholytes can adopt globular, stretched, or intermediate necklace conformations. To provide a complete description of their conformational behavior, we consider chains with Markov charge statistics and construct a scaling diagram of states in the coordinates of charge blockiness and solvent quality. Ten scaling regimes of various necklaces are identified, which can be classified into three types: (i) “charge-in-beads” necklaces form at moderate charge blockiness, with the bead size defined by the Rayleigh instability criterion; (ii) “charge-in-strings” necklaces are stable at higher blockiness of like charges, which enables the net charge imbalance to migrate from the beads to the strings; (iii) “hierarchical” necklaces, which are necklace-in-necklace conformations identified herein for the first time, comprise beads of two different sizes that coexist when the charge blockiness is high and the solvent quality is poor. For all three types of necklaces, scaling predictions are quantitatively confirmed by molecular dynamics simulations. Our findings on the conformational statistics of charge-imbalanced polyampholytes may contribute to improved prediction and classification of the structure of intrinsically disordered proteins/regions (IDPs/IDRs).


## Introduction
  Polymer pearl-necklace conformations are often referred to as partially globular because the chain consists of small globules (“pearls” or “beads”) ordered along a straight line and connected by highly stretched fragments (“strings”). Necklace structures were first reported by Kantor and Kardar in the context of random polyampholytes (PAs) carrying an excess global charge. ,  It was originally suggested that the spherical globule of the PA undergoes Rayleigh instability and falls apart into daughter globules that repel each other. However, these globular beads cannot move infinitely away from each other because they are chemically connected parts of the same polymer molecule. This results in necklace-like conformations, in which the charge of each bead is on the order of the Rayleigh threshold value, and the distance between them (referred to as the string length) is much larger than the diameter of the bead, l str ≫ D bead. Necklace formation is thermodynamically advantageous because it significantly reduces the Coulomb free energy of the PA compared to the spherical globule state, albeit at the cost of increased surface energy of beads and strings.

  Subsequent work by Dobrynin, Rubinstein, and Obukhov (DRO) demonstrated that necklaces are also equilibrium conformations for hydrophobic polyelectrolytes (PEs), in which long-range Coulomb repulsions are balanced by short-range non-Coulomb attractions. A scaling theory demonstrated that the sizes of the structural elements of the necklace are defined by the lengths associated with these interactions. In the DRO necklace shown in Figure  , the size of the bead is on the order of the electrostatic blob, D bead ≃ ξe ≃ a(uf 2)−1/3, and the string thickness is approximately equal to the thermal blob size, d str ≃ ξT ≃ a|v| –1. Here, a denotes the monomer size, u is the dimensionless Bjerrum length of the solvent, f is the fraction of charged monomers in the PE, and v is the dimensionless second virial coefficient (see Section   for detailed definitions). Owing to the long-range nature of Coulomb interactions, the length of the PE necklace scales linearly with its length and charge, L nec ∼ Q = efN. This approach was later extended to PAs with ideally random charge statistics, where the global charge and hence the necklace size scale with the chain length as N 1/2.

  The formation of the PE necklace was also described within a variational approach by Olvera de la Cruz and co-workers. A cylindrical globule of PE in a poor solvent, earlier suggested by Khokhlov, was shown to be unstable with respect to the stretching modulations, generating highly and weakly extended regions that give rise to strings and beads. This instability analysis is conceptually reminiscent of the random phase approximation (RPA) calculations of the microphase separation spinodals for semidilute solutions of hydrophobic PEs. ,  Given this analogy, the necklace formation can be treated as an electrostatic microphase separation within the single chain, with the structure period equal to the length of the necklace string. The similarity between the two phenomena was later used to develop the scaling theory of microphases in semidilute solutions of hydrophobic PEs and to describe their dynamics. ,  More recently, self-consistent field theory (SCFT) has also been applied to describe single-chain PE necklaces, −   and the scaling relationship L nec ∼ fN has been reproduced. A more detailed discussion on the universality underlying the formation of different electrostatically stabilized microstructures, ranging from single-chain necklaces to bulk microphases, can be found in the recent Viewpoint.

  The formation of the PE necklace has been experimentally validated by small-angle neutron scattering, ,  nuclear magnetic resonance, and atomic force microscopy. ,  Recently, hydrogels comprising PE necklaces as effective subchains have been engineered to create hyperelastic materials. Their unique elastic properties have been attributed to the unfolding and refolding of the necklace beads under cyclic mechanical strain, with the beads acting as an effective analogue of sacrificial bonds.

  Renewed interest in PA necklaces has been driven largely by the need to understand the conformations of intrinsically disordered proteins/regions (IDPs/IDRs), which are biological analogs of synthetic PAs. −        Special attention has been paid to the role of the primary sequence, ,−   which controls macroscopic liquid–liquid phase separation in solutions of IDPs. −

  It is important to highlight two recent achievements in computational studies of necklace-like PAs and/or IDPs. First, for PAs with ideally random charge statistics, it was found theoretically and in simulations that the necklace undergoes strong size and shape fluctuations. The precise structure of the necklace is governed by the quenched disorder in the primary monomer sequence in a way that causes unequal splitting of the spherical globule into daughter beads of unequal size to bethermodynamically favorable. −   These results provide a refinement of the mean-field model of the PA necklace with equal beads, which assumes a net PA charge to be homogeneously smeared along the chain and neglects inhomogeneities in the charge distribution. Note that the scaling predictions remain unchanged.

  Second, to go beyond ideally random PAs and systematically consider the role of anionic and cationic monomer clustering, we have recently examined conformations of PAs with Markov charge statistics in a Θ solvent under salt-free conditions. Because the strength of Coulomb attractions and repulsions due to PA charge imbalance both depend on the sequence, the size and structure of the necklace are sensitive to the average length of blocks of similar charges. Moreover, when the charge blockiness is sufficiently high, the mean-field description of the PA as a polymer with smeared excess charge becomes inappropriate and fails to predict equilibrium PA conformations. The excess PA charge begins to migrate from the beads to the strings, which reduces the Coulomb energy of the necklace. A new type of necklace has almost neutral beads and excess charge in strings, whose length is on the order of the length of the charge block. This structure resembles an array of tadpoles formed by asymmetric diblock PAs, where each consists of a collapsed head and a stretched PE tail.

  Recently, the theory of single chain PA necklaces has been extended to the case of salt-added solutions. A comprehensive scaling diagram of conformational regimes has been constructed in the coordinates of salt concentration and net charge per monomer. The point where the regimes of highly swollen conformations, necklaces, and globules meet was identified as the single-chain analogue of the Lifshitz multicritical point, where fluctuations in size/shape of the PA/IDP are maximized.

  In summary, necklace formation occurs in ionic polymers with a nonzero global charge and short-range attractions between the monomers, which are provided by poor solvent quality for hydrophobic PEs and Coulomb attractions between the opposite charges for PAs. Additionally, in the case of PAs, the necklace structure is affected by the sequence. The questions that then arise are as follows. What happens in a system such as a hydrophobic PA, in which both types of attractionshydrophobic and Coulombicoperate simultaneously? Should one expect a usual, DRO-type necklace with the net charge residing on the beads, or a necklace with charge imbalance in strings, provided that the clustering of the opposite charges is high? Similarly, does the interplay between these two tendencies lead to the formation of new, hybrid necklaces that combine features of the “mean-field” DRO necklace with homogeneously smeared excess charge and that with the structure controlled by the charge block length?

  This work aims to address these questions. To that end, we consider conformations of Markov PAs with arbitrary sequence blockiness in solvents of arbitrary quality. This analysis is necessary to understand the interplay between sequence-dependent Coulomb interactions and sequence-independent non-Coulomb interactions, which defines the conformations of IDRs/IDPs and synthetic PAs in solution.

  The paper is organized as follows. The theoretical model used to describe Markov PAs and scaling diagram of their conformational regimes are presented in Sections   and 3. Section   provides several concluding remarks.


## Theoretical Model
  We consider a highly dilute solution of statistically neutral polyampholytes (PAs) in the absence of salt. PAs are assumed to be long flexible chains containing N monomers, each of size a. Nonelectrostatic interactions between the monomers are described by the second and third virial coefficients B = va 3 and C = wa 6, respectively, where v and w ≃ 1 are dimensionless parameters. The solvent quality is controlled by the sign of v: positive and negative values correspond to good and poor solvent quality, respectively, and v = 0 at the Θ-point. The regimes of good/Θ/poor solvent are denoted by +/Θ/–, respectively.

  The total fraction of positively and negatively charged monomers in PAs is equal to f. These monomers are distributed equidistantly along the chain, and each carries charge ±e. Their sequence is governed by a first-order Markov process. ,,,  The type of an ionic monomer depends solely on the preceding one along the chain, as represented by the following transition matrix: P=[p++p+−p−+p−−] 1 In this matrix, p  ij  = p(i|j) denotes the conditional probability that the next charged monomer is of type i given that the previous one is of type j, with i, j ∈{+, – }. Probability conservation dictates p ++ + p –+ = 1 and p –– + p +– = 1. In this work, we focus on statistically neutral PAs, that is, their ensemble-average global charge is zero. This condition is provided by the symmetry of the transition matrix, where p ++ = p –– and p +– = p –+. The three constraints imposed on the transition matrix P lead to a single parameter λ that controls the Markov process, λ = p ++ + p ––– 1. This parameter quantifies correlations in the primary PA sequence. The particular values of λ = – 1 and λ = 0 correspond to alternating and ideally random PA sequences, respectively. As λ → 1, each PA is either a polyanion or a polycation, which we refer to as PEs; the statistical neutrality is maintained by their equal number in the ensemble.

  The parameter λ also controls the “blockiness” (clustering) of the similar charges, that is, the average charge of the block comprising consecutive charges of the same sign. This average block charge is equal to 1 + Λ with the other blockiness measure Λ given by Λ=1+λ1−λ 2 As λ increases from −1 to 1, Λ increases from zero to infinity, and the average block length changes from unity for alternating PAs to Λ → ∞ for PEs (polyanions and polycations).   The strength of electrostatic interactions between the ionic monomers of PAs is quantified by the Bjerrum length, l  B  = e 2/ϵk B T, which corresponds to the distance between charges when the energy of the Coulomb interactions becomes equal to the thermal energy, k  B  T; here ϵ denotes the dielectric constant of the solvent. For convenience, the Bjerrum length is expressed in units of the statistical segment length, u = l  B /a. All energies in this work are expressed in units of k B T.


## Scaling Diagram of States
  In this Section, we describe the conformations of PAs with different charge blockiness in solvents of different quality. Figure   presents a scaling diagram of the PA conformational regimes, which contains 15 distinct regimes.

  When both charge blockiness and the global charge imbalance are small, the PA conformation is not affected by the net charge. In Regime IΘ, a PA with an almost alternating sequence forms a globule stabilized by dipole–dipole attractions. In Regime I–, globule formation is mainly driven by two-body monomer attractions in a poor solvent. In Regime I+, these interactions are repulsive and the PA is a swollen coil.

  By increasing the charge blockiness, the PA adopts necklace conformations of type II and III, with the net charge homogeneously distributed along the chain. These structures emerge from the interplay between long-range Coulomb repulsions and various kinds of short-range attractions. In Regime III–a, attractions are provided by non-Coulomb two-body interactions. In other regimes of type II and III, it is the Coulomb attractions that promote the formation of the necklace beads. In Θ and good solvents, i.e., in the Θ and + regimes, they are balanced by three-body and two-body short-range repulsions, respectively. Regimes IIΘ and IIIΘ are distinguished based on the different types of Coulomb correlation attractions, and the same applies to their good solvent counterparts, II+ and III+.

  Once the block length is sufficiently high, excess charge migrates from the beads to the strings to minimize the PA Coulomb energy. This results in the formation of necklaces of type IVΘ and IV+, which carry the net charge in their strings. In Regimes III–b and IV–, due to the additional non-Coulomb short-range attractions, these strings become unstable with respect to necklace formation. As a result, the PA forms nested necklace-in-necklace structures.

  When the charge block length exceeds the total chain length, the Markov PA becomes a PE, and classical PE conformations are expected. In Regime VΘ and V+, the chain is a highly stretched PE. In a poor solvent, the PE forms classical DRO necklaces, and this regime is denoted by V–.

  We start our detailed analysis with PAs of low charge blockiness Λ. On the scaling diagram, this region corresponds to the regimes of type I.

## Polyampholyte Necklaces with Homogeneous Charge
Distribution: General Analysis
  Polymer chains can form necklace structures when short-range attractions between monomers operate on par with long-range repulsions. Repulsions are caused by long-range Coulomb interactions between the monomers that provide a nonzero global charge of the chain, while attractions may have various origins and types. They may be hydrophobic, as for PE necklaces in a poor solvent, or Coulombic, as for necklaces of charge-imbalanced PAs. In this section, we present a general analysis of the necklace structure. To provide the highest level of generality, we do not specify the type of short-range attractive interactions but only identify two parameters, φatt and ξatt, controlling their strength and the respective length scale.

  Short-range attractions lead to (at least locally) globular polymer conformations, with the concentration blob size within the globule denoted ξatt and referred to as the “attraction blob.” Its size is defined by local interactions only, i.e., the competition between short-range attractions and short-range repulsions. ## Distribution and Migration of Global Charge
in Polyampholyte Necklaces: General Analysis
  In Sections   and 5, it was assumed that the charge is homogeneously distributed along the PA chain. This mean-field approximation, initially suggested for PE necklaces within the DRO theory, can be violated for Markov PAs due to the blocky distribution of the net charge along the chain. To define the applicability range of the general framework of Section  , the redistribution of the global PA charge between the beads and the strings should be considered. This mechanism is referred to as the migration of the global charge. In this section, we examine when the charge distribution in the necklace becomes inhomogeneous due to the charge migration and derive the respective boundaries.

  In regimes IIIΘ, III+ or III–a, most of the mass of the necklace is concentrated in the beads, since m bead ≫ m str according to eq  . The same applies to the net charge of the necklace, Q bead ≫ Q str. Given that the length of the string substantially exceeds the size of the bead, l str ≫ D bead (see eq  ), the Coulomb self-energy of the string and its electrostatic potential are much lower than those of the bead. The difference in potentials, Q bead/D bead ≫ Q str/l str, drives the migration of the net charge from beads to strings, which is provided by “refolding” of the PA necklace and diminishes its Coulomb energy.

  The global charge is always transferred from the bead to the string, and weak charge migration occurs even in regimes IIIΘ, III+, and III–a. As a result, the net charge of the string is not equal to its mean-field charge δfm str, but instead corresponds to its maximal possible net charge Qstr≃fmstrΛ 65 which is provided by the PA “refolding”. The latter is higher than the former and grows with sequence blockiness. Equation   defines the characteristic global charge of the Markov PA fragment containing m str monomers provided that this fragment is longer than one charge block, m str > Λ/f. The string carries the net charge Q str, matching the sign of the global charge imbalance, but determined by the local charge statistics, that is, the values f and Λ, rather than by δf quantifying the global charge of the entire PA. All type III necklaces are described by the DRO model as Q bead ≫ Q str, so the redistribution of the charge does not substantially diminish the bead charge. Hence, the necklace structure is not affected by charge migration, which is therefore termed weak.

  As the blockiness of the charge Λ increases, the amplitude of the charge migration increases. Above the respective crossovers, this potentially leads to two different effects:1.The block length reaches the string length, i.e., the string ceases to carry the opposite charges and becomes a PE instead of a Markov PA. ## Molecular Dynamics Simulations
  In this section, we perform coarse-grained simulations of Markov PAs to test two key theoretical predictions: (i) the mechanism of charge migration accompanied by the transition from charge-in-beads necklace IIIΘ to charge-in-strings necklace IVΘ in solvent Θ and (ii) the formation of hierarchical necklaces of type IV– under poor solvent conditions.

  To test these regimes, we perform coarse-grained MD simulations of Markov PAs in an implicit solvent in a broad range of charge blockiness Λ. A set of different Bjerrum lengths, u, and fractions of ionic monomers in the chains, f, are considered to demonstrate the universality of the scaling predictions. Because sampling over Markov chains with different charge imbalance is very computationally demanding, simulations are limited to PAs carrying the characteristic net charge  Qtot=fNΛ . This is completely theoretically justified because chains with the characteristic charge imbalance dominate the ensemble-average size of Markov PAs, and the scaling laws remain unchanged. ,,  For each charge blockiness Λ, simulations are performed for many different realizations of the Markov process, each with the same net charge, to provide appropriate averaging over sequences. To ensure that the PAs are not kinetically arrested, simulations for each sequence realization are run from two distinct starting configurations: one is extended rod-like and the other compact globular, generated at a low u value in a poor solvent. The final PA conformations are considered equilibrated if the necklace size R g and the number of beads in it N bead are (within the statistical error) independent of the initial configuration. This procedure demonstrates that, for u = 5 and f = 0.5, necklaces relax to the equilibrium as long as their blockiness is not very high, Λ/ΛIIIΘ/IVΘ < 20. At higher Λ, PA conformations are kinetically trapped, and these results are not included in the following. Further simulation details can be found in the SI.


### Charge Migration and Charge-in-Strings Necklaces
  The developed scaling theory predicts that, in Θ solvent, Markov PAs experience transitions from the spherical globule (Regime IΘ) to a charge-in-beads necklace of IIΘ and IIIΘ types, then to a charge-in-strings necklace IVΘ, and finally to the stretched PE conformations of Regime VΘ.

  For Regime IΘ, recent coarse-grained MD simulations have confirmed the scaling law R ∼ u –2/3 for the globule radius. ,  The ideal-coil-like dependence for the size of the necklace formed by ideally random PAs, R ≃ aN 1/2 for Λ = 1, was previously observed in extensive Monte Carlo simulations. This result is consistent with the scaling predictions for Regime IIΘ of charge-in-beads necklaces, ,  cf. eq  . Therefore, our main focus is on Regime IIIΘ of the charge-in-beads necklace and Regime IVΘ of the charge-in-strings necklace, where we expect to observe the charge migration and the concomitant reorganization of the necklace structure.

  First, we examine the dependence of the PA size R g on charge blockiness Λ. Figure  a presents the respective scaling predictions and suggests that, in Regimes IIIΘ and IVΘ, the power laws for the PA dimensions coincide, R  g  ∼ Λ1/2, as follows from eqs   and . The slope of 1/2 is confirmed by the simulation data shown in Figure  c. Here, the Λ-axis is normalized by the crossover value ΛIIΘ/IIIΘ ≃ u –2/3 f –1/3 to collapse all the R  g  values in Regimes IIIΘ and IVΘ into a universal master curve. (However, this normalization does not ensure the collapse of the data in the five regimes because the crossovers between them are different functions of the two parameters, u and f. The nonuniversal behavior in regimes IΘ and IIΘ will be discussed later.)

  Analysis of the size of the necklace R g does not allow us to distinguish between the charge-in-beads necklace IIIΘ and the charge-in-strings necklace IVΘ. However, their different internal structure implies that the number of beads N bead should be a much better descriptor of the necklace type. Scaling predicts that N bead will change in a remarkable nonmonotonic fashion, as shown in Figure  b. According to eqs   and , it increases as N bead ∼ Λ before complete charge migration (Regime IIIΘ) and decreases according to N bead ∼ Λ–1/2 when the net charge transfers from the beads to the strings (Regime IVΘ). To corroborate this necklace reorganization, cluster analysis has been performed to calculate the number of globular beads in the necklace. The number of necklace beads found in the simulations was normalized to the theoretical maximal value, N bead max = u 2/9 f 4/9 N 1/3, to provide the universal value of N bead/N bead max ≃ 1 at IIIΘ/IVΘ crossover. An analogous representation of blockiness in units of Λ/ΛIIIΘ/IVΘ with ΛIIIΘ/IVΘ = u –4/9 f 1/9 N 1/3 should theoretically collapse all simulation data for N bead(Λ) into the universal master curve in regimes IIIΘ and IVΘ. The resulting dependence is demonstrated in Figure  d, where only the points with u –2/3 f –1/3 < Λ < Nf, that is, corresponding to these two regimes, are retained.

  Figure  d shows that the number of beads in PA necklaces exhibits nonmonotonic behavior, in agreement with the scaling predictions. For both charge-in-beads necklaces and charge-in-strings necklaces, the simulation slopes are close to the predicted values of 1 and −1/2. The slope of 1 in Regime IIIΘ is more evident for data sets with high uf 2 values because this parameter defines the regime width, ΔΛIIIΘ ≃ ΛIVΘ/IIIΘ/ΛIIIΘ/IIΘ ≃ (uf 2)4/9 N 1/3. The gray curve provides the simplest interpolating formula unifying the two neighboring scaling regimes:  Nbead=αNbeadmax[βΛIIIΘ/IVΘΛ+(ΛβΛIIIΘ/IVΘ)1/2]−1 97 Here α and β are numerical coefficients of the order of unity, which cannot be predicted within the scaling analysis. They are usually hidden in ≃ sign, but here we exactly define N bead max and ΛIIIΘ/IVΘ in order to process simulation results. Extracting α and β values would enable specifying the numerical coefficients in the derived scaling law. One can see that the interpolating formula provides an adequate fit for all simulation results with α = 1.17, β = 1.08, thereby confirming the theoretical hypothesis on charge migration. This is further supported by visualization of the charge-in-beads and charge-in-strings necklaces provided in Figure  e,f. In Regime IIIΘ, most of the net PA charge is accumulated on the beads while the strings are almost neutral and contain both positively and negatively charged monomers. In Regime IVΘ, positive net charges migrate to the strings, which become PE rather than PA.

  Finally, we should comment on the nonuniversal behavior of PAs, which is observed in Figure  c in the range of low blockiness, i.e., in regimes IΘ and IIΘ. The simulation results are close to the scaling predictions of Figure  a only for the highest values of u = 5 and f = 0.5 studied. For other pairs u and f, the PA dimensions decrease at low Λ. This occurs because, in simulations, PAs are not sufficiently long and/or electrostatic interactions are not strong enough to provide globular conformations of alternating PA in Regime IΘ and the globular structure of the necklace beads in Regime IIΘ; for this reason, they remain coils or exhibit crossover coil–globule and coil-necklace conformations in the respective Λ-ranges, so that the chain gradually shrinks at increasing Λ. In contrast, the scaling diagram in Figure   was constructed for the limiting cases of long chains.

  To support this statement, one compares the size of the alternating globule given by eq   to the ideal coil size aN 1/2. Globule formation is ensured when u is much higher than the threshold value of u coil/glob alt, Θ ≃ f –3/4 N –1/4. The crossover position can be found exactly using the simulation results from our previous work, which employed the identical coarse-grained model of the PA. Therein, neutral alternating PAs with N = 256 and f = 1 were found to collapse at u coil/glob alt, Θ ≈ 3. This enables us to specify the numerical prefactor in the scaling law ucoil/globalt,Θ≈12f−3/4N−1/4 98 and explain why, for most pairs of u and f, alternating PAs with N = 1024 monomers are not truly globular in the simulations performed. Only for magenta points, u = 5 noticeably exceeds the threshold of u coil/glob alt, Θ ≈ 3.56. Recall that the choice of moderate u and f values was motivated by the necessity to avoid kinetic trapping of necklaces in regimes IIIΘ and IVΘ, when charge blockiness is higher and Coulomb attractions are much stronger than for alternating sequences. A thorough computational study of the globule-to-necklace transition, which is similar to the present IΘ/IIΘ crossover, can be found elsewhere.


### Hierarchical Necklaces
  To demonstrate the formation of hierarchical necklaces, we consider a Markov PA of very high blockiness in a poor solvent. The particular realization was generated using the Markov process with blockiness Λ = 156, and its primary structure (sequence) and characteristic conformation are shown in Figure  . The entire chain contains N = 2498 monomers with the total fraction of charged monomers f = 0.5. The global charge imbalance Q tot = 543 is close to the characteristic net charge  fNΛ=441 , which corresponds to δf ≈ 0.22.

  As theoretically anticipated, the resulting hierarchical necklace contains beads of two types, which differ not only in size but also in the “local” fraction of monomers that carry the excess charge within the bead. Cluster analysis shows that three major beads contain both positively and negatively charged monomers, with δf maj = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.18, respectively. Each of them is formed by two polycation blocks and one or two polyanion blocks. The global charge imbalance in the major beadsQ bead maj = 138, 89, and 106is comparable to the charge blockiness Λ = 156. In contrast, in the minor beads, all ionic monomers have the same (positive) sign, which leads to δf min = f = 0.5. Thus, the hierarchical necklace considered can be classified as IV– type, and Q bead maj close to Λ suggests that it is near the crossover III–b/IV– associated with the complete charge migration threshold, Λ**. These results qualitatively support theoretical predictions of novel necklace-in-necklace structures. A more quantitative test of the respective scaling laws remains for future work.


## Relevance to Intrinsically Disordered Proteins
  To apply the results of the developed theory to real IDPs, their primary sequences can be approximated by Markov sequences and their conformations can be predicted based on the scaling diagram shown in Figure  . Within this approach, the charge blockiness is quantified by the average block length ⟨Λ⟩an alternative to the patterning parameter κ and the sequence charge decoration (SCD) metric used in earlier works. ,  Averaging over the IDPs from the DisProt Database yields the mean value of ⟨Λ⟩ ≈ 1.27, suggesting that the average primary sequence of ionic monomers in IDPs only weakly deviates from an ideally random one. (A similar result is also known for folded proteins.) Depending on the solvent quality, i.e., the IDP hydrophobicity, ,,−   this corresponds to the necklace IIΘ and II+, swollen coil I+, and globular II– regimes of the conformational diagram constructed in Figure  ; the new types of hierarchical necklaces (regimes IV) may be only relevant to a few IDPs due to the requirement of high charge blockiness.

  This scaling diagram is limited to salt-free conditions and statistically neutral sequences. However, our results can be extended to PAs carrying a nonzero net charge and to salt-added solvents following the approach of ref , where the case of ideally random charge statistics has been considered. It was conjectured that, for real IDPs, the physiological salt concentrations are close to the single-chain “multicritical Lifshitz point”the crossover point between globular, necklace, and coil-like conformationsand that this contributes to their strong shape and size fluctuations. It should also be noted that the diagram in Figure   is constructed for sufficiently long PAs. Real IDPs may not always satisfy this condition and may exhibit coiled conformations instead of globules or necklaces, which their longer counterparts would form; scaling estimates for these crossovers can be found elsewhere. ,,

  Finally, it is worth mentioning that our previous scaling theories for globally neutral PAs are qualitatively consistent with recent small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) studies of IDPs under salt-free conditions. In ref , residues of the nearly neutral PNt sequence were swapped to increase the charge blockiness, and SAXS measurements showed the conformational change from a coil to a globule, in agreement with the theoretical predictions. A similar trend has also been reported in simulations of ref  (see Figure  b therein).


## Conclusions
  In this paper, we have developed a scaling theory to describe single-chain conformations of statistically neutral Markov polyampholytes (PA) in poor, Θ, and good solvents. The scaling diagram of the PA conformational regimes has been constructed in the coordinates of the second virial coefficient v and the charge blockiness Λ. It is presented in Figure   and contains 15 regimes, 10 of which correspond to different types of necklaces. The respective scaling laws for the chain dimensions and structural properties of the PA chain can be found in Table  . The number of the scaling regime increases from I to V with increasing charge blockiness, while the sign +, −, or Θ reflects the solvent quality. The key findings provided by the scaling analysis can be summarized as follows:1.At low charge blockiness Λ, the chain’s global charge imbalance is not sufficient to perturb conformations typical for the globally neutral PA. The chain is a swollen coil in good solvent (Regime I+) and forms globules in Θ or poor solvents (Regimes IΘ and I−). These globules are stabilized by Coulomb and two-body nonelectrostatic attractions, respectively.2.As Λ increases, the net charge becomes sufficient to trigger the Rayleigh instability of the spherical globule and the formation of the PA necklace (Regimes II+, IIΘ, III+, IIIΘ, and III–a). In PA necklaces of these types, the global charge is almost homogeneously distributed along the chain. These necklaces are referred to as charge-in-beads because the majority of the mass, and hence the electric charge, is concentrated in the beads. Regimes are distinguished based on the type of short-range attractions, which are non-Coulomb in Regime III–a and Coulomb (correlation) in the four others. In Θ and + regimes, the local PA statistics within the correlation blobs are ideal-coil and swollen-coil, respectively. Correlation attractions increase with the charge blockiness in regimes II and, once the block charge Λ exceeds that of the electrostatic blob, reach a plateau in regimes III.3.A further increase in the charge blockiness leads to the migration of the global PA charge from necklace beads to the strings. It is achieved by “refolding” of the PA and provides a reduction in the Coulomb energy of the necklace. Charge migration starts to affect the necklace structure at Λ ≃ Λ*, when the necklace string becomes polyelectrolyte (PE), that is, comprises charges of only one sign. The crossover indicating the complete transfer of the global charge from the beads to the string is denoted Λ**.4.In Θ and good solvents, these two crossovers coincide: Λ* ≃ Λ** because both attractive and repulsive interactions are of Coulombic origin. Regimes IVΘ and IV+ correspond to the charge-in-strings necklaces with net-neutral beads and PE strings carrying the entire global charge of the chain.5.Under poor solvent conditions, the interplay between Coulomb repulsions and non-Coulomb attractions leads to Λ* < Λ < Λ**. This opens the window for Regime III-b, in which most global charge belongs to the necklace beads, but strings are PE rather than PA. Strings strive to absorb more charge imbalance to diminish the net Coulomb energy of the chain, so they become minor PE necklaces themselves. These necklace-in-necklace conformations are referred to as hierarchical necklaces. In Regime III-b, minor PE necklaces are stretched by Coulomb repulsions between the beads of the major necklace.6.The other type of hierarchical necklace is formed in a poor solvent at Λ > Λ**, when the entire global charge is transferred to the strings (Regime IV−). In this regime, the beads of the major necklace are neutral while the major strings represent classical (unstretched) necklaces of hydrophobic PEs. It is the combination of the charge migration mechanism and instability of the PE string to the PE necklace formation that enables hierarchical necklace conformations of both III–b and IV– types. This explains why they can only exist in poor solvents and at sufficiently high PA blockiness.7.Finally, Regimes V are implemented at Λ > fN, when the entire chain consists of one charge block and hence represents PE. Regimes VΘ and V+ correspond to highly swollen PEs. In Regime V–, chains are classical PE necklaces in a poor solvent.

  The most important theoretical predictions on the structure of necklaces and their transitions between different necklace types were corroborated by coarse-grained MD simulations. First, the formation of charge-in-strings and charge-in-bead necklaces has been confirmed using the cluster analysis algorithm to determine the number of necklace beads, N bead. A monotonic increase in the length of the PA necklace L nec accompanied by the nonmonotonic dependence of N bead on the charge blockiness Λ in Regimes IIIΘ–IVΘ is a theoretical prediction, which was quantitatively corroborated in simulations. The detected scaling slopes for L nec and N bead are consistent with the scaling laws for the respective regimes. Second, for highly blocky PAs in a poor solvent, our simulations demonstrated the formation of the hierarchical necklace. It comprises major neutral beads and minor charged beads, in agreement with the theoretical description of Regime IV–.
