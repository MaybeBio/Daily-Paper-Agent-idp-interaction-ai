# A survey of predicted protein-protein interactions involving disordered regions in humans


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in proteins play a pivotal role in protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Using AlphaFold2 and enriched multiple sequence alignments, we predicted and investigated PPIs across the human proteome, focusing on those involving disordered regions. Our predictions show that disordered regions predominantly interact with ordered domains, whereas predicted disordered–disordered interactions are relatively rare. Although disordered regions typically lack annotated domains, certain regions—such as the keratin type II head domain and the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB)—mediate specific interactions. In contrast, their predicted binding partners frequently feature diverse Pfam domains, including protein kinase, WD40 repeat, and nuclear hormone receptor domains. These domains are enriched in nuclear localization and α-helical repeat motifs. Disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs exhibit higher sequence conservation than non-interacting disordered regions, suggesting evolutionary constraints at interaction interfaces. Moreover, certain posttranslational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation and acetylation) in disordered regions are enriched within predicted interaction interfaces, likely modulating binding affinities. Notably, we identified a significant enrichment of disease-associated mutations in predicted PPI interfaces involving disordered regions, underscoring their functional and pathological relevance. Together, these findings highlight the intricate interplay between disordered and ordered regions in mediating PPIs and provide insights into their structural and functional contributions to human health and disease.


## Introduction
  Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) are protein segments that lack a stable three-dimensional structure and instead exist as dynamic ensembles of conformations [1]. These regions have emerged as critical players in cellular function and regulation. In contrast to structured domains, IDRs do not form a well-organized hydrophobic core, allowing them to adopt diverse conformations and interact with multiple binding partners [1–3]. The prevalence of IDRs in eukaryotic proteins highlights their importance in diverse cellular processes. Rather than serving merely as linkers between structured domains, IDRs actively participate in protein-protein interactions (PPIs) and expand the functional versatility of proteins [3]. Their inherent flexibility and adaptability enable them to engage in complex molecular interactions that are essential for signaling, regulation, and other critical cellular functions.

  One of the most intriguing aspects of IDRs is their ability to undergo disorder-to-order transitions upon binding to their partners [4]. This coupled folding and binding mechanism allows IDRs to achieve high specificity with moderate affinity, making them ideal for reversible interactions in regulatory and signaling processes. For instance, the disordered regions of E-cadherin transition to ordered conformations upon binding β-catenin, illustrating the dynamic nature of these interactions [5]. IDRs often exhibit binding promiscuity, using the same region to interact with multiple partners [1]. This versatility allows them to function as hubs in protein-protein interaction networks, integrating various cellular signals [6]. The promiscuous nature of IDRs is particularly advantageous in regulatory proteins, enabling responses to diverse stimuli and mediate complex cellular responses [7].

  The functional versatility of IDRs is further enhanced by their ability to undergo posttranslational modifications (PTMs), such as phosphorylation and acetylation [8, 9]. These modifications can act as molecular switches, altering the binding properties of IDRs and enabling precise regulation of protein-protein interactions. For instance, phosphorylation within an IDR can modulate the conformational ensemble of the region, either promoting or inhibiting interactions with specific partners [10]. This capacity for fine-tuned regulation underscores the importance of IDRs in signaling pathways and cellular decision-making processes.

  In addition to their biological relevance, IDRs are increasingly recognized as key players in pathological conditions [2, 11]. Aberrant protein-protein interactions involving IDRs have been implicated in diseases such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and viral infections. For example, the aggregation of disordered proteins like tau and α-synuclein is a hallmark of diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s [12]. Understanding the molecular basis of IDR-mediated interactions provides not only fundamental insights into protein function but also avenues for therapeutic intervention. By targeting the unique properties of IDRs, it may be possible to design strategies to modulate protein-protein interactions, offering hope for addressing complex diseases.

  Despite their functional significance, studying IDRs and their involvement in protein-protein interactions poses significant challenges [13]. Their inherent flexibility renders them difficult to characterize using traditional structural biology techniques, such as X-ray crystallography. Instead, methods like nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and molecular dynamics simulations have proven invaluable in uncovering the conformational landscapes of IDRs [14]. These approaches, combined with high-throughput proteomics, have shed light on the mechanisms underlying IDR-mediated interactions and their roles in health and disease.

  Recent research has focused on developing methods to predict protein-protein interaction sites within IDRs, recognizing their critical role in cellular function [15–17]. These efforts are crucial for understanding the mechanisms of molecular recognition and the principles governing IDR-mediated interactions. AlphaFold2 [18] has significantly impacted the definition and prediction of IDRs in proteins, offering new perspectives on protein structure and function [19, 20]. AlphaFold2, although trained on folded proteins, has shown remarkable ability in predicting disordered regions. The relative solvent accessibility and predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score from AlphaFold2 serve as excellent measures of residue-wise disorder, outperforming many traditional sequence-based disorder predictors [21].

  Identifying protein complexes and determining their 3D structures are critical for understanding protein functions. Computational methods, particularly coevolutionary analysis combined with deep learning (DL), have emerged to complement experimental studies. Recent large-scale modeling efforts have advanced the structural resolution of the human interactome, revealing both the promise and limitations of deep-learning approaches for mapping protein–protein interfaces. A comprehensive prediction of experimentally supported human interactions using AlphaFold2 produced thousands of high-confidence models that illuminated disease-linked and post-translationally modified interface residues, demonstrating the feasibility of a structurally resolved human interaction network while emphasizing that highly ordered interfaces are modeled with greater confidence than those involving disordered regions [22]. Expanding this perspective to interactions mediated by intrinsically disordered regions, fragment-scanning and domain–motif modeling approaches have shown that prediction accuracy improves markedly when binding regions are isolated from full-length proteins, achieving near 90% success in identifying correct interfaces and revealing the prevalence of short linear motifs and small disordered segments in transient interactions [23]. Combined computational and experimental studies further confirmed that accurate domain–motif interfaces can be systematically identified across the human proteome and that structural modeling of full-length disordered proteins remains limited by competing intramolecular contacts and sequence context effects [24]. Complementary benchmarking of AlphaFold-Multimer demonstrated that the method can reproduce diverse modes of IDR binding, including heterogeneous and fuzzy complexes, and that its intrinsic metrics—particularly the predicted aligned error and interface pTM—correlate with interaction dynamics and disorder content [25]. Together, these advances establish that deep-learning structure prediction can effectively capture both ordered and disordered interaction architectures while highlighting the need for dedicated, proteome-scale analyses centered on intrinsically disordered regions—an objective addressed in the present study.

  To improve the accuracy of PPI predictions, we recently developed omicMSAs by mining extensive genomic data, significantly enhancing evolutionary signals [26]. Our method introduced RoseTTAFold2-PPI, a DL network trained with a large dataset of domain-domain interactions to distinguish true PPIs from random pairs efficiently [26]. Integrating omicMSAs and RF2-ppi enabled a proteome-wide PPI screen, identifying thousands of high-confidence human PPIs [26]. This approach represents a scalable and accurate method for mapping the human interactome by leveraging computational power and vast genomic resources. In this study, we analyzed predicted PPIs involving disordered regions in the human proteome predicted with enriched evolutionary signals in structural modeling. We examined the domain composition of disordered regions involving predicted PPIs and their interaction partners. Sequence conservation of disordered residues was assessed to compare their evolutionary constraints with ordered residues. We surveyed PTMs, including phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation, that were mapped onto disordered PPI interfaces to determine their prevalence and potential regulatory roles. Additionally, we investigated putative disease-causing mutations in disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs.


## Results and discussion

### Prediction of PPIs involving disordered regions in the human proteome
  We predicted PPIs for human proteins and protein domains using enriched multiple sequence alignments derived from genome sequences [26]. Among these predicted PPIs, we focused on those involving residues within disordered regions, as identified by consensus predictions from AlphaFold2 and Metapredictor (see Materials and Methods). We mapped disordered residues to predicted PPI interfaces and investigated sequence conservation, domain contents, posttranslational modifications, and diseasing causing mutations for these predicted interfaces (Figure 1A). A total of 1,362 human proteins were found to engage in 2,877 predicted PPIs by using their disordered regions. Notably, some proteins exhibited multiple interactions via disordered regions: 20 proteins participated in 10 or more predicted PPIs, while 74 proteins were involved in at least five. Proteins with the highest number of predicted PPIs involving disordered regions included TAF1L, NUP98, ATRX, EGFR, and MED1. We further examined the structural state of residues that interact with disordered residues at PPI interfaces. Approximately 99% (172043 out of 173863) of these residues were classified as ordered, suggesting that most disordered regions engage in predicted interactions with structured globular domains. It should be noted that collagens were excluded from the analysis because they can skew statistics of disorder regions [27]. Although collagen chains form highly ordered triple-helical trimers, they are frequently misclassified as disordered due to their low sequence complexity and the absence of stable tertiary structure in their monomeric state.

  Our observation that disordered regions predominantly engage in predicted interactions with ordered domains, rather than with other disordered regions, likely reflects both biological principles and methodological factors. Biologically, intrinsically disordered regions often rely on structured partners to provide a stable scaffold for binding, enabling coupled folding–binding mechanisms that achieve specificity with moderate affinity [28]. In contrast, interactions between two disordered regions are typically transient and dynamic, characteristics that are well suited for processes such as liquid–liquid phase separation but less compatible with stable complex formation [29, 30]. As a result, these interactions may be underrepresented in structural datasets and less likely to appear in predictive models. From a methodological perspective, tools such as AlphaFold2 are trained primarily on experimentally resolved complexes, where IDR–ordered interactions are common, whereas disordered–disordered interactions are rarely captured. Moreover, the multiple sequence alignments used to guide predictions carry stronger evolutionary constraints for ordered domains than for IDRs, leading to higher-confidence predictions at IDR–ordered interfaces. Together, these factors suggest that the dominance of IDR–ordered interactions in our predictions could reflect both the inherent binding preferences of IDRs and the current limitations of computational structure prediction.

  We analyzed the pLDDT scores of disordered residues located at predicted protein–protein interaction (PPI) interfaces, both before and after complex formation. Their score distributions are shown in Figure 1B. In the AlphaFold2 models of individual proteins (without complex modeling), these disordered residues have an average pLDDT of 44.9 and a median of 42.5, with 96.6% of them considered unreliably modeled (pLDDT < 70). In contrast, in the AlphaFold2 models of protein complexes, the average and median pLDDT scores increase slightly to 48.3 and 45.2, respectively. Notably, the fraction of high-confidence disordered residues (pLDDT ≥ 90) at PPI interfaces rises to 2.0% in complex models, compared to less than 0.1% in the same residues before complex formation. Similarly, the fraction of confidently modeled interface residues (pLDDT ≥ 70) increases from 3.4% to 17.7% upon complex formation. These results suggest that a subset of disordered residues become more structured in the complex state, likely due to the stabilizing effects of intermolecular interactions.


### Domain contents of predicted PPIs involving disordered regions
  We analyzed the domain composition of disordered regions engaging in predicted PPIs and their interaction partners, retrieving known protein domains (Pfam domains) from the InterPro database [31]. In most cases, disordered regions associated with predicted PPIs had no assigned Pfam domains. The most frequently occurring Pfam domain in disordered regions is Keratin_2_head (Keratin-type II head domain, Pfam: PF16208), reflecting the abundance of keratin paralogs in the human proteome. This intrinsically disordered domain facilitates keratin interactions by binding to the coiled-coil regions (Intermediate Filament protein domain, Pfam: PF00038) of other keratins (Figure 2A). One example is the interaction between KRT75 and KRT3, which shows a high RF2-PPI contact probability of 0.995 and an AlphaFold-Multimer contact probability of 0.973, despite exhibiting low contact probabilities (<0.1) when modeled using AlphaFold2 or ColabFold. The second most frequent domain is the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB, Pfam: PF01352), a small α-helical domain with a disordered N-terminus. Disordered regions within KRAB domains frequently mediate dimerization (Figure 2B, ZNF225 and ZNF221 with an AF2 contact probability of 0.97). Several Pfam domains, such as EPL1 (Enhancer of polycomb-like, Pfam: PF10513, Figure 2C, BRD1 and KAT6A with an AF2 contact probability of 0.993) and CADH_Y-type_LIR (Cadherin, Y-type LIR-motif, Pfam: PF01049, Figure 2D, CDH4 and JUP with an AF2 contact probability of 0.995), are predominantly disordered. CADH_Y-type_LIR domains interact with Armadillo repeats, facilitating cadherin heterodimerization. Similar to the KRAB domain, the BTB/POZ domain (Pfam: PF00651, Figure 2E, ZBTB7B and ZBTB5 with an AF2 contact probability of 0.998) is a mostly ordered globular domain that contains an N-terminal disordered region involved in heterodimerization.

  While disordered regions often lack known Pfam domains, their interaction partners exhibit a diverse array of known domains. Among the 3,768 Pfam domains in the dataset of predicted human protein PPIs, 862 are involved in predicted interactions with disordered regions. The protein kinase domain (Pkinase, Pfam: PF00069) is the most frequently observed, with 92 predicted interactions with disordered regions. This is followed by the intermediate filament rod domain (Pfam: PF00038) with 80 interactions, the WD40 repeat domain (Pfam: PF00400) with 76 predicted interactions, various Ankyrin repeat domains (Pfam: PF12796, PF00023, PF13637, PF13857 and PF13606) with 66 predicted interactions and the nuclear hormone receptor ligand-binding domain (Hormone_recep, Pfam: PF00104) with 51 predicted interactions.

  To assess the enrichment of these domains in predicted interactions with disordered regions, we calculated the fraction of their interactions involving disordered regions relative to their total interactions (Figure 3A). This fraction indicates a domain’s preference for binding disordered regions versus globular domains. Among the domains with the highest propensity for interacting with disordered regions are the Armadillo repeat (Pfam: PF00514), LIM-type zinc finger (Pfam: PF00412), SH3 (Pfam: PF07653, PF00018, and PF14604), and TAFII28-like protein domain (Pfam: PF04719). In each case, over half of their predicted interactions involve disordered regions. Domains enriched in predicted interactions with disordered regions tend to feature α-helical repeats, including Armadillo, Ankyrin repeat, and tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains (Figure 3B). Additionally, many of these domains are nucleus-associated, such as LIM, TAFII28-like, Helicase_C (Pfam: PF00271), Hormone_recep (Pfam: PF00104), Cyclin_N (Pfam: PF00134), and DEAD/DEAH-box helicase domains (Pfam: PF00270) (Figure 3C). Among 521 predicted PPIs involving a protein kinase domain, 92 occur through predicted interactions with disordered regions (Figure 3D). Notably, SH3 and PDZ domains, known for recognizing short linear motifs in disordered regions, are highly represented. Similarly, β-propeller fold domains, such as WD40 (Figure 3E) and FG-GAP (Pfam: PF01839), are enriched in predicted interactions with disordered regions, further highlighting the diversity of these structural motifs in mediating PPIs with disordered regions.

  These findings highlight how IDRs exploit modular binding domains to mediate diverse protein–protein interactions, often by presenting short linear motifs or flexible binding surfaces that complement the structural adaptability of repeat-containing domains. The enrichment of α-helical repeat domains (e.g., Armadillo, Ankyrin, and TPR) and β-propeller folds (e.g., WD40, FG-GAP) underscores the role of repetitive structural frameworks in providing extended binding interfaces well-suited for accommodating the conformational variability of IDRs. Similarly, the prominence of SH3 and PDZ domains, which canonically recognize short peptide motifs, supports the idea that IDRs frequently serve as recognition platforms for modular signaling and scaffolding proteins. The preferential association of nucleus-related domains, such as LIM and TAFII28-like, suggests that IDR-mediated interactions are particularly important in transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, and other nuclear processes where dynamic, multivalent interactions are required.


### Sequence conservation of disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs
  Disordered regions generally exhibit lower sequence conservation than ordered regions. We assessed positional conservation using AL2CO, which assigns integer conservation scores from 0 to 9 (see Materials and Methods). As expected, the proportion of highly conserved residues (scores 7, 8, or 9) is lower in disordered regions compared to ordered regions (Figure 4). However, disordered residues involved in predicted PPIs show higher sequence conservation than disordered residues overall. In fact, within the highest conservation categories (scores 7, 8, and 9), PPI-associated disordered residues display similar or even slightly higher conservation fractions compared to ordered residues (Figure 4). These findings suggest that conserved residues in disordered regions are more likely to participate in predicted PPI interfaces, potentially due to strong evolutionary constraints on residue composition. Another contributing factor may be AlphaFold2’s reliance on co-evolutionary signals from multiple sequence alignments, which are weaker in non-conserved regions. This bias could partly explain the limited PPI predictions for non-conserved disordered regions.

  While these observations emphasize the functional importance of conserved residues within disordered regions, it is important to recognize the limitations of current approaches for detecting interactions in less conserved contexts. Weak or transient interactions, which can be mediated by short linear motifs within poorly conserved stretches of disorder, may be underrepresented due to the reliance of methods such as AlphaFold2 on co-evolutionary signals and multiple sequence alignments. Because such motifs can evolve rapidly while maintaining functional recognition properties, sequence conservation alone may not capture their contribution to protein–protein interactions. Consequently, the predicted PPI landscape may be biased toward more stable or conserved disordered interfaces, underestimating the prevalence and biological relevance of transient, low-affinity contacts that are central to signaling and regulatory networks.

  To further examine how evolutionary signal varies across predicted interfaces involving disordered regions, we quantified the effective MSA depth (Neff) for each interaction type. All Neff measurements were derived exclusively from predicted interfaces that include at least one disordered residue. Positional Neff values were computed using a Henikoff weighting scheme [32] and normalized by the maximum Neff within each alignment to allow comparison across proteins. Positions forming ordered–ordered contacts within these interfaces exhibited deeper evolutionary support, with an average normalized Neff of 0.834 (SD = 0.175). In contrast, positions participating in contacts that include disordered residues—encompassing both disordered–ordered and the relatively rare disordered–disordered contacts—showed notably shallower alignments, with an average normalized Neff of 0.671 (SD = 0.196). These results indicate that coevolutionary information is systematically weaker at interface positions involving disordered residues, consistent with their generally lower sequence conservation and structural heterogeneity. The reduced alignment depth likely contributes to the lower confidence of predicted interfaces involving disorder, underscoring the importance of MSA quality when interpreting PPI models for intrinsically disordered regions.


### PTMs in disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs
  We extracted post-translational modification (PTM) information from the MOD_RES feature annotations in UniProt [33], which specify residues that undergo chemical modifications. Each MOD_RES entry provides the modified amino acid position and the type of modification, such as phosphoserine, N6-acetyllysine, or N6-methyllysine. We filtered these annotations to focus on phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation sites. The positions of these modified residues were then mapped onto IDRs. This allowed us to analyze how these PTMs are distributed within disordered regions, providing insight into the functional roles of modifications in disordered regions at PPI interfaces.

  Approximately 10% of the disordered regions in our dataset participate in predicted PPIs, serving as a baseline for assessing the enrichment or depletion of specific modification types in PPI interfaces. Phosphoserine is the most prevalent modification in disordered regions, with 4,903 modified residues in proteins with disordered residues at predicted PPI interfaces. Among them, 602 phosphoserines (12.3%) occur in predicted PPI interfaces, slightly exceeding the 10% baseline for disordered residues in such interactions. Similarly, phosphothreonine and phosphotyrosine show enrichment in PPI interfaces involving disordered regions. Of 830 total phosphothreonines, 119 (14.3%) are found in interfaces, while 48 of 202 phosphotyrosines (23.8%) occur in these regions (Figure 5A). A notable example of phosphorylated residues in predicted PPIs is the interaction between the C-terminal disordered tail of CDH1 and the α-helical repeats of β-catenin (CTNNB1), which has been experimentally verified [34]. This tail contains multiple phosphoserine and phosphotyrosine sites within the interaction interface (Figure 5B).

  Lysine acetylation (N6-acetyllysine), commonly found in histones and transcription factors [35], is slightly enriched in disordered regions involved in PPI interfaces. Notably, 17 of the 19 proteins with lysine acetylation in disordered interfaces are nuclear. Some proteins feature multiple acetylated lysines within their interaction interfaces. For example, the NCOA family proteins are known to interact with estrogen receptors [36]. We predicted that NCOA3 interacts with the estrogen receptor ESR1 through a disordered region (residues 609–631) containing three lysine acetylation sites (Lys616, Lys619, and Lys620) (Figure 5C). Lysine acetylation may influence the binding affinity between these proteins. In contrast, three other types of acetylation—N-acetylalanine, N-acetylmethionine, and N-acetylserine—are depleted in disordered regions at PPI interfaces (Figure 5A).

  Arginine methylation and lysine methylation are known to modulate protein-protein and protein-nucleic acid interactions [37, 38]. The most frequent methylation modifications in our dataset are ω-N-methylarginine and asymmetric dimethylarginine, with 25 and 9 occurrences, respectively, in disordered regions involved in PPI interfaces, compared to 215 and 106 total cases in disordered regions. Other methylation types on positively charged residues, including N6-methyllysine, demethylated arginine, and N6,N6-dimethyllysine, are less abundant in disordered regions but appear enriched in PPI interfaces (Figure 5A). An example of arginine methylation is SNRPN (small nuclear ribonucleoprotein-associated protein N), a small protein that was predicted to interact with the WD40 domain of SNRNP40 (U5 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein 40 kDa protein), a component of the active spliceosome involved in pre-mRNA splicing [39] (Figure 5D). This predicted PPI appears to be novel in terms of structural characterization, yet it is supported by two high-throughput interaction assays reported in the BioGRID database [40]. The disordered region of SNRPN at the PPI interface contains two arginines that can be methylated (Arg108 and Arg112), suggesting that arginine methylation may play a role in modulating spliceosomal interactions, potentially influencing protein binding affinity or structural dynamics. Further investigation is needed to determine whether methylation at these sites directly affects spliceosome assembly or function.

  These results underscore the central role of PTMs in regulating protein–protein interactions mediated by intrinsically disordered regions. The enrichment of phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation sites within predicted PPI interfaces suggests that IDRs frequently act as tunable regulatory hubs where reversible chemical modifications modulate binding affinity, specificity, and interaction dynamics. The preferential occurrence of phosphorylated serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues in interaction sites highlights how multisite phosphorylation can act as a molecular switch, as seen in the CDH1–CTNNB1 interaction. Similarly, the enrichment of lysine acetylation in nuclear proteins, particularly transcriptional regulators, suggests the importance of acetylation in chromatin-associated signaling networks. Methylation of lysine and arginine residues, although less abundant overall, appears selectively enriched in IDR-mediated interfaces, consistent with their roles in fine-tuning RNA-binding and spliceosomal functions. A key limitation, however, is that PTM annotations are often incomplete and biased toward well-studied proteins in curated databases such as UniProt, raising the possibility that many functionally relevant modification sites in IDRs remain undetected. Moreover, transient or condition-specific PTMs that could strongly influence weak or dynamic interactions may not be captured in the current dataset, suggesting that the regulatory impact of PTMs on IDR-mediated PPIs may be even broader than observed here.


### Putative disease-causing mutations mapped to disordered regions involved in predicted PPI interfaces
  Disease-causing mutations are often found in disordered regions [11, 41]. In our dataset of predicted PPIs involving disordered regions, we identified 1517 putative disease-causing mutations within these regions (see Materials and methods). Notably, 403 mutations (27%) were found in these PPI interfaces, a significantly higher fraction compared to the 10% of residues in disordered regions that form interfaces. This enrichment suggests that many diseases may arise from PPI disruptions. Among the putative disease-causing mutations in predicted PPI interfaces of disordered regions, aromatic residues (W, Y, H, and F) were among the most prevalent amino acid types (Figure 6A). Threonine (T) and aspartic acid (D) were also notably enriched. In contrast, isoleucine (I), cysteine (C), glutamic acid (E), lysine (K), and glutamine (Q) were the least frequent in these interfaces.

  We identified a predicted interaction between a disordered region (1206-1240) of the tumor suppressor protein BRCA1 [42] and ANAPC5 (Anaphase Promoting Complex Subunit 5) [43], both of which regulate cell cycle, although their interaction has not been previously reported. This disordered region harbors putative disease-causing mutations of two phenylalanine residues (F1226L/S and F1231L) (Figure 6B). Notably, F1226, embedded in the interaction interface, establishes the most contacts with ANAPC5, suggesting that its mutation may have the greatest impact on the predicted BRCA1-ANAPC5 interaction.

  ANKS1B (Ankyrin Repeat and Sterile Alpha Motif Domain-Containing Protein 1B) and APP (Amyloid Precursor Protein) have been shown to interact [44, 45]. This interaction affects the ANKS1B’s subcellular localization, leading to its partial exclusion from the nucleus when APP is overexpressed [46]. We predicted their interaction involving a disordered region (residues 743-770) in APP and the C-terminal PID domain (residues 1031-1200, Pfam: PF00640) of ANKS1B (Figure 6C). Several putative disease-causing mutations (G756D, T761A, Y762C/F) in APP map to the predicted interface (Figure 6C). This interaction may be modulated by PTM, as the interface residue APP residue Y757, located at the interface, is a known phosphorylation site.

  Putative disease-causing mutations were also found in the interaction partners of disordered regions at the interaction interfaces. For example, WAS (Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome protein) and ACTG1 (actin gamma 1), both integral to the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton [47], were predicted to interact through the disordered region of WAS and the globular domain of ACTG1, similar to experimental structures of their homologs [48, 49]. Within their predicted interface, three putative disease-causing mutations (L434F, Q437P, and R439P) were found in WAS, and seven mutations (G23R, A144T, S145F, T149A, S338L/W, L346V and V370A) were identified in ACTG1 (Figure 6D). Another example is the predicted interaction between the disordered region of TMEM237 (Transmembrane protein 237) and the globular domain of CC2D2A (Coiled-coil and C2 domain-containing protein 2A). Both proteins are key components of the cilium transition zone and are required for cilium biogenesis [50, 51]. Because a direct interaction between TMEM237 and CC2D2A has not been documented, this prediction likely reflects a novel protein–protein interaction. Mutations of TMEM237 and CC2D2A have been associated with ciliopathies such as Joubert syndrome and Meckel syndrome [50, 51]. Six putative disease-causing mutations (C420W, K507E, K551N, P652S, S802P and W513R) were found on CC2D2A in the interaction interface (Figure 6E).


## Conclusion
  Intrinsically disordered regions are critical modulators of protein function, owing to their structural flexibility, capacity for dynamic interactions, and sensitivity to regulatory modifications. Our large-scale predictions of PPIs in the human proteome reveal that disordered regions operate as critical mediators of protein–protein interactions. Rather than interacting extensively with other disordered regions, disordered regions are predicted to predominantly engage with structured globular domains, supporting their role as adaptable partners that fold upon binding. Despite often lacking known Pfam domains, disordered regions interact with a diverse range of structured domains, including α-helical repeats, β-propeller domains, and nucleus-associated domains, highlighting their functional versatility. The conservation of residues within disordered PPI interfaces, despite the generally lower conservation of disordered regions, supports their functional importance. The enrichment of PTMs within disordered PPI interfaces, such as phosphorylation and certain types of acetylation and methylation, suggests that these modifications may regulate interaction dynamics and protein function. Furthermore, the significant presence of disease-associated mutations in disordered PPI interfaces suggests that disruptions in these interactions could contribute to disease mechanisms. Together, these findings provide insights into the structural and functional significance of disordered regions in protein interactions in humans, emphasizing their potential as targets for therapeutic interventions and advancing our understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying cellular regulation and disease.

  At the same time, our findings must be considered in light of the limitations of computational approaches. Current structure prediction methods, including AlphaFold2-based modeling, are trained primarily on folded proteins and stable complexes, which may bias predictions toward interactions involving structured domains while underrepresenting transient or disordered–disordered interactions. Similarly, reliance on multiple sequence alignments favors conserved regions, potentially overlooking rapidly evolving short motifs that mediate weak but biologically important interactions. In addition, PTMs and pathogenic mutations are derived from curated databases, which remain incomplete and biased toward well-studied proteins. Future efforts that enhance the sensitivity of computational methods and couple these predictions with rigorous experimental validation will be critical for refining our understanding of interactions mediated by disordered regions and for unlocking their full potential as therapeutic targets.


## Materials and methods

### Prediction of PPIs for disordered regions in the human proteome
  All protein–protein interaction (PPI) models analyzed in this study were generated previously [26]. The procedures for building interaction models and evaluating AlphaFold2 (AF2) model confidence are described in detail in our prior work [26]. In brief, we used newly developed RoseTTAFold2-PPI and AF2-based modeling with MSAs enriched with omics data such as those from NCBI genome and Sequence Read Archive (SRA) databases. The final set of predicted protein–protein interactions (PPIs) were selected by applying a hierarchical evaluation framework across eight datasets—ranging from pairs sharing subcellular localization or genetic interaction evidence to experimentally supported sets from UniProt, STRING [52], and BioGRID [40]. Five computational pipelines combining DCA, RF2-PPI, and AlphaFold2 (AF2) with varying confidence cutoffs were applied, producing 16 screening strategies in total (details described in section M5.6 in supplementary information of our previous work [26]).

  We identified predicted human PPIs involving disordered regions that are defined by combining the results of AlphaFold-RSA [21], AlphaFold-pLDDT [21] and Metapredict [15]. AlphaFold-RSA and AlphaFold-pLDDT results were retrieved from the MOBID database [53]. We used a conserved approach to define disordered regions: a residue is considered disordered if it is predicted by all three methods. Collagen triplet repeats are frequently predicted to be disordered due to their amino acid composition while they form ordered trimer complexes. As collagens have been found to affect statistics involving disordered regions [27], we removed all predicted interactions involving collagens.


### Analysis of domains, conservation and PTMs of disordered regions
  Protein domains were mapped to human proteins by InterPro [31]. We focused on Pfam domains [54] in InterPro such that each residue can be assigned to no more than one Pfam domain. We calculated sequence conservation for positions in the proteins of our dataset by using the program AL2CO [55] with default parameters. The AL2CO program reported an integer conservation value normalized between 0 and 9. Positions with scores between 0 and 3 are considered least conserved, scores between 4 and 6 are considered moderately conserved, and scores between 7 and 9 are considered most conserved. PTMs were retrieved from features of “MOD_RES” in protein entries the UniProt database [33].


### Analysis of putative disease-causing mutations in predicted PPI interfaces involving disordered regions
  In order to identify disease-associated mutations on the predicted PPI interfaces, we downloaded files containing information about single amino acid variations (SAVs) in humans and their association with diseases from UniProt [33]. We compared the wildtype amino acid at each SAV position against the amino acid in our sequences to ensure these SAVs matched our protein sequences. In addition, we downloaded AlphaMissense [56] predictions from https://console.cloud.google.com/storage/browser/_details/dm_alphamissense/ . We checked if the reference sequences used by AlphaMissense matched our sequences, and discarded 3 proteins (0.01%) showing >5% mismatched residues. We considered three types of evidence that support the pathogenicity of a SAV, including: 1) predicted as pathogenic by AlphaMissense; 2) annotated as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” by UniProt, and 3) linked to diseases by UniProt. We included SAVs supported by at least two of the above criteria as putative disease-causing mutations. We mapped these putative disease-causing mutations to the interfaces of predicted protein complexes. These mutations were also mapped to the disordered regions and disordered regions involving predicted PPIs of our dataset.
