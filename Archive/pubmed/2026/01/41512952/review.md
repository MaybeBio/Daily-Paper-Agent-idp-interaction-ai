## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (Abstract, Introduction, Results and discussion, Conclusion, Materials and methods, Supplementary Material description, Data and code availability, Acknowledgements)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological soundness, interpretation of results, and alignment with Nature-style criteria. No assessment of editorial fit or publication decision.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present a large-scale computational survey of predicted protein-protein interactions (PPIs) involving intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in the human proteome, using AlphaFold2 and enriched multiple sequence alignments. They report that IDRs predominantly interact with ordered domains, that interacting IDRs show higher sequence conservation, that specific posttranslational modifications are enriched at predicted interfaces, and that disease-associated mutations are significantly enriched in these interfaces.
- **Visible evidence base** Full text including four results subsections, one figure reference per subsection (Figures 1A–1B, 2A–2E, 3A–3E, 4, 5A–5D, 6A–6E), and a Materials and methods section. No figures, tables, or supplementary data were provided for direct inspection.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures, tables, and the supplementary file listing predicted interactions were not provided. The prior publication [26] describing the PPI prediction pipeline is referenced but not available. This limits independent verification of quantitative claims, statistical analyses, and the quality of structural models.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and timely topic: the role of intrinsically disordered regions in protein-protein interactions, leveraging recent advances in deep learning-based structure prediction. The authors present a systematic analysis of predicted human PPIs involving IDRs, with several biologically plausible and potentially significant findings, including the predominance of IDR-ordered domain interactions, enrichment of specific domain families, and enrichment of disease mutations at predicted interfaces. However, the manuscript relies entirely on computational predictions without experimental validation, and the evidence base provided is insufficient to fully assess the robustness of the statistical claims. The writing is generally clear, but the lack of access to figures and underlying data prevents a complete evaluation of the technical soundness. The study is likely to interest a broad readership in structural biology, bioinformatics, and molecular medicine, but the strength of the conclusions depends on the quality of the prediction pipeline and the rigor of the statistical analyses, which cannot be fully verified from the supplied material.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Structural biologists studying protein-protein interactions and intrinsically disordered proteins; bioinformaticians developing and applying deep learning methods for interactome mapping; cell biologists interested in signaling, transcriptional regulation, and nuclear processes; and researchers in human genetics and disease biology, given the reported enrichment of disease-associated mutations at predicted IDR-mediated interfaces.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a significant gap in understanding IDR-mediated interactions at proteome scale. The use of enriched multiple sequence alignments to improve prediction of IDR-involved PPIs is a methodological advance. The integration of multiple data types (domain composition, conservation, PTMs, disease mutations) provides a multi-dimensional view. The identification of specific examples (e.g., keratin head domain, KRAB domain, CDH1-β-catenin) grounds the computational predictions in known biology. The authors acknowledge limitations of computational approaches and the potential bias toward ordered interactions.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "disordered regions predominantly interact with ordered domains, whereas predicted disordered–disordered interactions are relatively rare" and that "approximately 99% (172043 out of 173863) of these residues were classified as ordered."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, subsection "Prediction of PPIs involving disordered regions in the human proteome"; Figure 1A; Materials and methods, subsection "Prediction of PPIs for disordered regions in the human proteome"
  - **Concern** The classification of residues as ordered or disordered relies on a consensus of three prediction methods (AlphaFold-RSA, AlphaFold-pLDDT, Metapredict). The manuscript does not provide details on the agreement between these methods, the thresholds used, or the validation of this consensus approach. More critically, the prediction of PPIs itself depends on AlphaFold2 and RoseTTAFold2-PPI, which are known to have lower confidence for IDR-involved interactions. The authors report that only 2.0% of interface residues reach pLDDT ≥ 90 in complex models, and 17.7% reach pLDDT ≥ 70, indicating that the vast majority of predicted IDR-involved interfaces are modeled with low confidence. The claim that IDRs predominantly interact with ordered domains may therefore reflect a systematic bias of the prediction methods rather than a biological reality.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the manuscript rests on the reliability of the PPI predictions involving IDRs. If the prediction pipeline systematically underperforms for disordered-disordered interactions, the observed dominance of IDR-ordered interactions could be an artifact. Without a rigorous assessment of prediction confidence stratified by interaction type, and without benchmarking against known IDR-mediated interactions, the primary claim is not established.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a detailed analysis of prediction confidence (e.g., pLDDT, interface pTM, predicted aligned error) stratified by interaction type (disordered-ordered vs. disordered-disordered). Include a benchmarking analysis against experimentally validated IDR-mediated interactions (e.g., from databases such as ELM or DIBS) to demonstrate that the pipeline can recover known cases. If possible, show that the low-confidence predictions do not drive the observed trends.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Statistical rigor
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs exhibit higher sequence conservation than non-interacting disordered regions" and that "certain posttranslational modifications (e.g., phosphorylation and acetylation) in disordered regions are enriched within predicted interaction interfaces."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, subsections "Sequence conservation of disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs" and "PTMs in disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs"; Figures 4 and 5A
  - **Concern** The manuscript reports differences in conservation and PTM enrichment but does not provide statistical details. No effect sizes, confidence intervals, or p-values are given. It is unclear whether the comparisons are appropriately controlled for confounding factors such as protein length, expression level, or subcellular localization. For PTM enrichment, the analysis relies on UniProt MOD_RES annotations, which are known to be incomplete and biased toward well-studied proteins. The authors acknowledge this limitation qualitatively but do not assess its impact on the reported enrichment.
  - **Why it matters** The claims of conservation and PTM enrichment are used to support the functional significance of predicted IDR-mediated interactions. Without proper statistical testing and control for confounders, these claims are not quantitatively supported. The PTM analysis, in particular, may be heavily influenced by annotation bias, which could undermine the conclusion that PTMs are enriched at IDR-mediated interfaces.
  - **Resolution test** Provide statistical tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U or permutation tests) for conservation and PTM enrichment comparisons, with appropriate multiple testing correction. Include effect sizes and confidence intervals. Perform sensitivity analyses, such as restricting the PTM analysis to proteins with high annotation coverage or using a background model that accounts for residue composition and accessibility.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "we identified a significant enrichment of disease-associated mutations in predicted PPI interfaces involving disordered regions, underscoring their functional and pathological relevance."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, subsection "Putative disease-causing mutations mapped to disordered regions involved in predicted PPI interfaces"; Figure 6A
  - **Concern** The analysis of disease-associated mutations relies on a composite definition of pathogenicity (AlphaMissense prediction plus UniProt annotations). The manuscript does not describe how the background expectation for mutation enrichment was calculated. It is unclear whether the enrichment is relative to all residues in disordered regions, all residues in the proteome, or some other baseline. The authors also do not address potential biases, such as the higher density of disease-associated mutations in well-studied genes, which may correlate with the presence of predicted PPIs.
  - **Why it matters** The claim of disease relevance is a key translational aspect of the manuscript. If the enrichment analysis is not properly controlled, the conclusion that IDR-mediated PPI interfaces are hotspots for disease mutations may be overstated. This could mislead readers about the pathogenic potential of variants in these regions.
  - **Resolution test** Clearly define the background model for mutation enrichment (e.g., expected number of mutations given gene length, mutation rate, and residue accessibility). Provide statistical significance with appropriate controls, such as matching for gene curation level or using a permutation-based approach that shuffles mutation labels across the proteome. Show that the enrichment is robust to the choice of pathogenicity criteria.

  - **Axis** Reproducibility
  - **Affected element** Materials and methods, subsection "Prediction of PPIs for disordered regions in the human proteome"
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and methods, subsection "Prediction of PPIs for disordered regions in the human proteome"
  - **Issue** The description of the PPI prediction pipeline refers to the prior publication [26] for details, but the current manuscript does not specify the exact criteria used to define a "predicted PPI" (e.g., confidence thresholds, interface residue definitions).
  - **Required correction** Provide a concise summary of the key parameters and thresholds used in the prediction pipeline, or include a supplementary table listing these parameters, to enable reproducibility.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Interpretation
  - **Affected element** Results and discussion, subsection "Domain contents of predicted PPIs involving disordered regions"
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, subsection "Domain contents of predicted PPIs involving disordered regions"; Figure 3
  - **Issue** The authors state that domains enriched in interactions with disordered regions "tend to feature α-helical repeats" but do not discuss whether this enrichment is statistically significant or whether it reflects the overall abundance of these domains in the proteome.
  - **Required correction** Add a statistical test for domain enrichment (e.g., hypergeometric test) and discuss whether the observed enrichment is specific to IDR-mediated interactions or general to all PPIs.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Completeness
  - **Affected element** Results and discussion, subsection "PTMs in disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs"
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, subsection "PTMs in disordered regions involved in predicted PPIs"; Figure 5
  - **Issue** The authors mention methylation as a modification of interest but do not provide a detailed analysis of methylation types beyond noting their presence. The example of SNRPN-SNRNP40 is described as "novel" but no experimental validation is offered.
  - **Required correction** Either expand the methylation analysis to include quantitative enrichment data or clearly state that the SNRPN-SNRNP40 interaction is a prediction requiring experimental confirmation.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (prediction confidence and potential bias), R1-M2 (lack of statistical rigor), R1-M3 (inadequate background model for mutation enrichment). These are blocking because the central claims of the manuscript depend on the reliability of the predictions and the validity of the statistical comparisons.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: The manuscript addresses a novel question (proteome-wide analysis of IDR-mediated PPIs) using a state-of-the-art computational approach. The focus on disordered regions in the context of deep learning-based interactome mapping is timely and underexplored. However, the conceptual framework (IDRs as interaction hubs) is well established, and the novelty lies primarily in the scale and methodology rather than in a new biological principle.
  - Scientific importance: If the findings are robust, they could have significant implications for understanding the role of IDRs in cellular signaling, transcriptional regulation, and disease. The reported enrichment of disease mutations at predicted IDR-mediated interfaces is potentially important for variant interpretation. However, the lack of experimental validation and the reliance on predictions with low confidence for IDR-involved interfaces temper the immediate impact.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: The topic bridges structural biology, bioinformatics, and molecular medicine, and the findings are likely to appeal to researchers in these fields. The use of AlphaFold2 and related methods is of broad interest to the computational biology community.
  - Technical soundness: The methodological approach is reasonable, but the manuscript lacks sufficient detail to assess the robustness of the predictions and the statistical analyses. The low pLDDT scores for most IDR-involved interfaces raise concerns about the reliability of the structural models. The statistical comparisons for conservation, PTM enrichment, and mutation enrichment are not adequately described.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well written and accessible, with clear explanations of the biological context and the significance of the findings. However, some sections (e.g., the description of the prediction pipeline) assume familiarity with deep learning-based structure prediction methods.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript addresses a significant and timely question, and the findings are potentially important. However, the central claims are not currently established from the provided evidence due to concerns about prediction confidence, statistical rigor, and the adequacy of the background models. The authors should address these issues with additional analyses and clarifications before the manuscript can be considered for publication.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "disordered regions predominantly interact with ordered domains" is not fully supported because the prediction pipeline may be biased against disordered-disordered interactions, and the confidence of IDR-involved predictions is low.
- The claim of "significant enrichment of disease-associated mutations in predicted PPI interfaces" is not supported without a clearly defined and statistically valid background model.
- The claim that "certain posttranslational modifications are enriched within predicted interaction interfaces" is not supported without statistical testing and control for annotation bias.
- The specific example of the BRCA1-ANAPC5 interaction is described as "not previously reported," but no experimental evidence is provided to validate this prediction.
- The claim that "a subset of disordered residues become more structured in the complex state" is based on pLDDT changes that are small (average increase from 44.9 to 48.3) and may not be biologically meaningful without additional validation.
