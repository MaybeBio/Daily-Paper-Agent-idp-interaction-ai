# Two-in-one protein tag enables the soluble expression and calcium-dependent purification of single-chain antibody fragments


## Abstract
  Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) antibodies are used in diagnostic and therapeutic biopharmaceuticals. However, its production is limited due to insoluble expression and lack of a universal purification ligand. Here, we report a novel two-in-one protein tag, termed CSQ-tag, utilizing a calsequestrin (CSQ) protein to enable the soluble expression and calcium-dependent purification for scFvs. The CSQ-tag enhanced the solubility of four different therapeutic scFvs with an average solubility of 83.8 ± 4.6% in the BL21(DE3) strain, despite its reducing cytoplasmic environment. Compared to other tags, CSQ-tag showed superior solubility, with 1.8-fold higher solubility than MBP-tag across four scFvs. The solubility enhancement of the CSQ-tag is attributed to two main characteristics: highly acidic sequences with a low isoelectric point of 3.95, and intrinsically disordered protein properties. Furthermore, CSQ-tag can cost-effectively purify therapeutic scFvs with high purity over 95% using the calcium-dependent phase transition properties. Importantly, the anti-VEGF-scFv produced with the CSQ-tag showed a binding affinity of 25.8 pM, similar to that of the existing therapeutic brolucizumab. Therefore, CSQ-tag can significantly improve productivity and simultaneously maintain scFvs’ functional integrity, thereby serving as a promising tool for universal expression and purification of therapeutic scFvs.


## Introduction
  Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have established themselves as leading biopharmaceuticals used in various medical fields, including cancer, asthma, central nervous system disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and infectious diseases, owing to their high homogeneity, specificity, and strong affinity for antigens1–3. However, mAbs have several limitations, including limited availability and accessibility, risk of side effects, and high cost4. The large size and high polarity of antibodies limit their extravasation and distribution, resulting in small volumes of distribution (Vd) ( ~ 3–9 L), which reduces their effective concentration in target tissues and diminishes their therapeutic efficacy. The low rates of clearance (8–12 mL/h) and long biological half-lives ( ~ 20–25 d) of antibodies can lead to prolonged residence times in the body when conjugated with radioisotopes or toxins, potentially causing unexpected pathogenicity and toxicity5. Additionally, the crystallizable fragment (Fc) of mAbs can interact with cell surface receptors (FcγR), sometimes leading to excessive lymphocyte activation, which may cause side effects, such as cytokine storms6,7. To overcome these issues, advancements in genetic engineering have enabled modification or removal of the Fc region, creating various antibody formats. One of the most widely used formats is a new type of short antibody called single-chain variable fragments (scFv), which can recognize target antigens but lacks the fragmented crystallizable (Fc) region8,9.

  The scFv is a single polypeptide of approximately 25 kDa consisting of variable regions of the heavy chain (VH) and light chain (VL) connected by a flexible peptide linker10. The scFvs demonstrate superior tumor penetration and faster pharmacokinetics compared to full-length antibodies owing to their small size and absence of the Fc domain11. Moreover, the high targeting ability of scFvs makes them ideal for diagnostic and therapeutic applications12. As a result, various scFv-based drugs, including Blinatumomab and Brolucizumab, have been developed and are being used in clinical settings, with many more scFv drugs currently under investigation8,13,14. However, despite these advancements, most scFvs still face several bottlenecks in the production process15. The most problematic issues are as follows: low solubility and stability of scFv form inclusion bodies in Escherichia coli16–18. In addition, the lack of a universal ligand for purification, such as protein A chromatography used for mAb purification, requires the creation of a purification protocol for each scFv19,20.

  The general production method for therapeutic scFvs in E. coli is in vitro protein refolding of inclusion bodies. However, this refolding process has several limitations. Firstly, refolding is often a time-consuming process, typically requiring several days to complete, which substantially extends production timelines21. Secondly, the refolding process necessitates high concentrations of denaturants (e.g., urea, guanidine hydrochloride) and other reagents22, and its efficiency often decreases with increasing protein concentration, limiting scalability for large-scale production23. Lastly, the efficiency of refolding can be low, with a substantial portion of the protein failing to fold correctly, generally resulting in low yields23. To address these issues, several strategies for soluble expression of scFv have been employed, including optimization of culture conditions24, co-expression with molecular chaperones, such as Skp, DsbC, and FkpA25, secretion of scFvs into the E. coli periplasmic space via N-terminal signal peptides26, use of strains capable of forming disulfide bonds in the cytoplasm (e.g., Shuffle T7 strain)16, and the use of solubility-enhancing tags (e.g., thioredoxin (Trx), small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO))27,28 and affinity tags (e.g., hexa-histidine tag, maltose binding protein (MBP), glutathione-S-transferase (GST))15,29. Among these, MBP is one of the most widely used solubility-enhancing tags and functions as a molecular chaperone, often conferring high solubility to aggregation-prone proteins. Nevertheless, MBP does not universally enhance the solubility of different scFvs. As a result, due to the persistent issues of low solubility and stability in many scFvs, a truly universal expression system for generating soluble scFvs has yet to be established.

  The purification process is a major bottleneck in scFv production30,31. Protein L, which is commonly used in the purification processes for scFv production, binds only to kappa light chains20,32. As an alternative, various affinity chromatography methods, such as Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC), are employed for scFv purification. However, the high cost of affinity resins, along with the labor-intensive processes involved in column packing and maintenance, presents significant challenges. IMAC does not inherently require high salt, low pH, or extended contact times; however, such conditions are sometimes applied to reduce impurities or nonspecific binding. Under these conditions, structurally unstable scFvs may undergo aggregation, partial unfolding, or degradation31. These limitations underscore the need for a scalable, rapid, and cost-effective purification method that maintains the high quality of scFvs.

  Calsequestrin (CSQ) is a highly acidic calcium-binding protein found in muscle cells. It plays a role in storing large amounts of calcium in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) and is involved in regulating the excitation-contraction coupling and stress response of muscle cells33. Interestingly, CSQ exists in a soluble state below a certain concentration of Ca2+, but as the concentration increases, it begins to multimerize sequentially, leading to protein polymerization34,35. We demonstrate that the structure and characteristics of CSQ enable its use as a two-in-one protein tag for soluble expression and purification. Using four therapeutic scFvs, we show that the CSQ tag markedly improves soluble expression in E. coli and enables calcium-dependent purification, thereby establishing a robust and scalable platform for scFv production. In contrast, conventional approaches, such as in vitro refolding or MBP-tag–based workflows typically depend on affinity chromatography and involve labor-intensive and costly procedures. Thus, the CSQ-tag represents a promising strategy to address a critical bottleneck in therapeutic scFv production.


## Methods

### Construction of CSQ-scFvs fusion protein
  For the CSQ-tag fusion proteins, a pBT7-N-His vector containing the CSQ sequence was synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). CSQ-linker-Tev-MCS (multiple cloning sites) vector was digested with BamHI (NEB, US) and XhoI (NEB, US). Target protein genes for anti-VEGF-scFv, anti-HER2-scFv, anti-CD3-scFv, and anti-CD19-scFv were synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). The genes encoding the four scFvs were amplified using PCR and digested with BamHI and XhoI. Each digested insert was ligated into a linearized CSQ-linker-Tev-MCS vector and identified using DNA sequencing (Bioneer, Korea).


### Construction of scFv fusion proteins with MBP, GST, SUMO, and TRX tags
  For SUMO-tagged fusion proteins, Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea) synthesized a pBT7-N-His vector containing the SUMO sequence. The MBP-tag was incorporated into a commercially available pMAL-c6T vector (New England Biolabs). The pET32 vector (Novagen) was used for the TRX tag. The GST tag was introduced using the pBT7-N-GST vector (Bioneer). The target protein genes for anti-VEGF-scFv, anti-HER2-scFv, anti-CD3-scFv, and anti-CD19-scFv were synthesized and cloned into the corresponding fusion tag vectors (MBP, SUMO, GST, or TRX) (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea).


### Protein expression and solubility analysis
  The prepared scFv plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli strains BL21(DE3) (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea) and SHuffle T7 (New England Biolabs, U.S). For seed culture, a single colony of E. coli was picked from an LB agar plate containing 50 µg/mL ampicillin and inoculated into 5 mL of Terrific Broth (TB) (Invitrogen, US). The culture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 160 RPM for 5 h. Subsequently, the seed culture was transferred to a 250 mL flask containing TB medium and further incubated at 37 °C and 160 RPM until the OD600 reached 0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; LPS Solution, Daejeon, Korea), followed by incubation at 20 °C for 18 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation for 20 min at 4000 rpm. Cell pellets were resuspended in 12.5 mL of 20 mM Tris pH 7.0 and stored at −80 °C. After pulsed sonication, the total cell lysate was obtained, and the soluble fraction was separated by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 1 h. Both the total and soluble fractions were analyzed using 10% SDS-PAGE.


### Western blotting
  Protein samples were prepared by mixing with 5× SDS-PAGE 5X sample buffer (Elpis Biotech), and then separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were electrotransferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad) in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 142 mM glycine, and 10% methanol) at 150 V for 60 min. The membrane was subsequently blocked with PBST containing 5% skim milk, and the scFv fusion tag was detected using a primary mouse monoclonal anti-hexahistidine antibody (1:5000; Proteintech, USA; Cat No. 66005-1-Ig), followed by a secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody (1:5000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA; Cat No. sc-2005). In addition, a calsequestrin 1 antibody (VIIID12) (1:5000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA; Cat No. sc-53012) was used for detection of the CSQ-tag, and a GST antibody (B-14) (1:5000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA; Cat No. sc-138) was used for detection of the GST-tag. The protein bands were visualized using a FUSION FX6 EDGE Imaging system (Vilber Lourmat, France), and band quantification was performed using the system’s built-in Evolution Capt Edge software analysis module.


### Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy
  The CD spectra were measured using a JASCO J-1500 spectropolarimeter (JASCO Corporation, Japan) equipped with a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 mm at 25 °C. CSQ-tag samples were prepared at a concentration of 0.4 mg/mL in 20 mM Tris buffer. For each spectrum, three individual scans from 260 to 190 nm were summed and averaged, and the CD signal from the solvent was subtracted. The CD intensity recorded in units of mdeg at each wavelength was normalized to the mean residue molar ellipticity ([θ]) in deg∙cm^2·dmol^−1. Secondary structure analysis was performed using the JASCO Spectra Manager Version 2 software.


### Purification of the CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv
  The CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was purified from the resulting supernatant using Ca2+-EDTA cycles. The supernatant was then treated with 20 mM Ca2+ for 30 min at 4 °C to induce CSQ multimerization. The aggregated CSQ-tagged protein was centrifuged at 5000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The obtained pellet was dissolved in 12.5 mL of buffer (20 mM Tris and 40 mM EDTA, pH 7.0). The resolved fusion protein was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The purified proteins were analyzed by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE).


### TEV protease cleavage and CSQ-tag removal
  Purified CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv (1 mg/mL) was incubated for 6 h at 30 °C with 2 μL of Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease (6 cleavage unit/μL; GenScript) in TEV cleavage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT). The cleavage results were evaluated using 10% SDS-PAGE. To further purify the protein by removing the cleaved CSQ-tag, anion exchange chromatography was employed using an ÄKTA go FPLC system (Cytiva, formerly GE Healthcare). The sample was loaded onto a Mono Q column (GE Healthcare) connected to the ÄKTA go system for this additional purification.


### HPLC SEC
  The purity of the anti-VEGF-scFv samples was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Analysis was conducted using a YL9100 Plus system (Anyang, Korea) equipped with a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (YMC, Kyoto, Japan). Each sample was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0). The column was pre-equilibrated at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min until a stable baseline and system pressure were achieved, after which 20 µL of each sample was injected and analyzed by SEC at 0.2 mL/min with a total run time of 30 min.


### Calcium-dependent turbidity
  CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was precipitated with each Ca2+ concentration for 30 min at 4 °C. The turbidity of the precipitated proteins was assessed by measuring the optical absorbance at 350 nm and was calculated as a function of Ca2+ concentration using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Nanodrop One; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).


### Hydrodynamic size distribution of CSQ-tag
  The particle size distribution of the CSQ-tags was monitored using dynamic light scattering (DLS) under different ionic conditions. CSQ-tag (1.0 mg/mL) was incubated with different KCl concentrations (0, 100, 200, and 300 mM) for 30 min at 4 °C. The size of each protein sample was measured at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano Range instrument (ELSZ-2000, Otsuka, Japan).


### Hydrodynamic size distribution of CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv
  The particle size distribution of CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv polymer was monitored using dynamic light scattering (DLS) at different calcium concentrations. CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv (1.0 mg/mL) was incubated with various Ca2+ concentrations (0–15 mM) for 30 min at 4 °C. The size of each protein sample was measured at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano Range instrument (ELSZ-2000, Otsuka, Japan).


### Inverse-transition cycle
  For the reaction, 20 mM Ca2+ was added to the CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv, and the absorbance was measured at 350 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Nanodrop One; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After adding 20 mM EDTA for reverse transition, the reaction was vortexed and the absorbance was measured at 350 nm. For the second precipitation cycle, absorbance was measured at 350 nm after the addition of 20 mM Ca2+. Subsequently, EDTA (40 mM) was added to the solution and mixed by vortexing before the absorbance was measured at 350 nm.


### Anion Exchange Chromatography
  Following TEV protease cleavage, anti-VEGF-scFv was separated from the CSQ tag using an ÄKTA start™ chromatography system (Cytiva, Sweden) equipped with a 5 mL HiTrap Q FF column (Cytiva, Sweden). The column was equilibrated with binding buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.0) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min prior to sample loading. After injection, bound proteins were eluted with a linear gradient from 0 to 100% elution buffer (20 mM Tris, 2 M NaCl, pH 7.0) over 10 column volumes (CV) at 1 mL/min. Fractions were collected at 1 mL per tube and analyzed by SDS-PAGE to confirm separation of scFv from the CSQ tag.


### Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
  Binding kinetics and affinity measurements were performed using the Biacore T200 system (Biacore Inc., Piscataway, NJ, USA). VEGF (PNP Biopharm, Seoul, Korea) was covalently immobilized onto a CM5 chip using amine coupling chemistry (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide:N-hydroxysuccinimide) to a density of approximately 14 RU. Purified anti-VEGF-scFv at concentrations ranging from 0.2 nM to 8 nM was injected over the VEGF-coated surface at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. Each concentration was injected at an association time of 2 min, followed by a dissociation time of 20 min in PBST (pH 7.4). Between cycles, the surface was regenerated by injecting 10 mM glycine (pH 3.0) for 30 s, followed by a 20 min stabilization period before the next injection.

  The kinetics and affinity of the interactions were evaluated using Biacore T200 Evaluation software. The binding curves were subjected to double referencing for normalization, and the resulting data were globally fitted using Biacore Evaluation Software version 3.2.1.


### Statistics and Reproducibility
  All experiments were performed with at least three biologically independent replicates, unless otherwise stated. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The exact number of independent replicates (n) is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Experiments not subjected to statistical analysis were independently repeated to ensure reproducibility.

## Results

### The overall process of the CSQ-tag for soluble expression and calcium-dependent purification
  The CSQ-tag functions as a two-in-one protein tag designed to facilitate both soluble expression and calcium-mediated purification (Fig. 1A). The overall process, illustrated in Fig. 1B, involves (i) soluble expression of scFvs using the CSQ-tag in E. coli, (ii) multimerization of CSQ–scFvs upon the addition of Ca²⁺, (iii) removal of impurities by centrifugation, (iv) dissolution of the purified CSQ–scFvs by adding the calcium chelator EDTA, and (v) cleavage and separation of the scFvs from the CSQ-tag by adding TEV protease. This approach enables the efficient production of scFvs by achieving both solubility enhanced expression and calcium-dependent purification.Fig. 1Scheme of CSQ-tag: A two-in-one system for soluble expression and calcium-dependent purification.A The acidic and intrinsic disorder properties of the CSQ-tag enhance the solubility of low-solubility single-chain antibody fragments. As Ca2+ concentration increases, CSQ-tag polymerizes from a monomeric molten globule state through compact monomers and dimers to a multimeric state. B Schematic representation of the CSQ-tag based two-in-one system for soluble expression and calcium-dependent purification for single-chain antibody fragments.


### CSQ-tag enhanced solubility of four different therapeutic scFv in BL21(DE3) E.coli strain
  The CSQ-tag fusion with scFv was designed with calsequestrin (CSQ) at the N-terminus, followed by a flexible linker (GSEGSEGEGGSEGSEGEG), a TEV protease cleavage site, and a C-terminal single-chain variable fragment (scFv) (Fig. 2A). Therapeutic scFvs were selected as follows: anti-VEGF-scFv, a component of brolucizumab used for age-related macular degeneration (AMD) treatment36; anti-CD3-scFv, a T-cell recognizing part of blinatumomab for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) therapy; anti-CD19-scFv, a malignant B-cell-specific part of blinatumomab37; and anti-HER2-scFv, an scFv version of trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer treatment38. Importantly, despite their clinical success, these scFvs are well known to encounter low solubility and aggregation issues in bacterial expression systems16–18.   The gene sequences of four therapeutic scFvs were cloned into the CSQ-tag vector, and each plasmid was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3). To evaluate the solubility-enhancing effect of the CSQ-tag, scFvs fused only with a poly-histidine tag were also expressed under identical conditions. Following cell lysis and fractionation, the total and soluble scFvs were analyzed by western blotting. To quantify scFv solubility, defined as the ratio of soluble to total scFv, both fractions were subjected to serial dilution prior to western blotting to avoid signal saturation. Serial dilutions ranging from 1 to 1/360 were applied, and for scFvs with low expression levels the dilution range was adjusted accordingly. As shown in Fig. 2B, the western blotting signals of CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv correlated logarithmically with dilution. However, at high dilutions (1/80 to 1/320), the signal intensities displayed an approximately linear correlation. Therefore, for quantitation, the soluble scFv was analyzed at a dilution of 1/160. As reported in previous studies, most scFvs form insoluble inclusion bodies in E. coli because of their complex structure, weak VH-VL interactions, and reduced cytoplasmic environment21. Consistent with these reports, we observed that all four His-tagged therapeutic scFvs showed extremely low solubility when expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) (Fig. 2C). The average solubility of His-tagged scFvs was approximately 0.05%, whereas the anti-CD3-scFv showed no detectable level of soluble expression (Fig. 2E). However, the solubility of the CSQ-tagged scFvs was determined to be 90.7 ± 2.2% for CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv, 60.4 ± 8.6% for CSQ-anti-CD3-scFv, 91.6 ± 4.4% for CSQ-anti-CD19-scFv, and 92.7 ± 2.2% for CSQ-anti-HER2-scFv (Fig. 2D). Notably, CSQ-anti-CD3-scFv, which exhibited the lowest solubility among the CSQ-tagged scFv, still demonstrated a solubility rate more than 60% higher than that of the His-tagged scFv. (Fig. 2E). Importantly, CSQ-tag demonstrated high solubility for four scFvs, with an average solubility of 83.8 ± 4.6% in the BL21(DE3) strain, despite its reducing cytoplasmic environment. These results indicated that the CSQ-tag significantly enhanced the solubility of all four therapeutic scFvs.

  Shuffle T7 strains with trxB and gor mutations provide a more oxidative cytoplasmic environment and overexpress the DsbC chaperone, which corrects improper disulfide bonds and aids in protein folding, making them widely used for scFv expression16,39. We also tested the expression of four therapeutic scFvs in E. coli Shuffle T7 strain (Supplementary Fig. S2). Analysis of equal volumes of soluble and insoluble fractions showed that all four scFvs tagged with the CSQ-tag exhibited high solubility in the E. coli Shuffle T7 strain. In contrast, scFvs tagged with only a His-tag remained largely insoluble, consistent with the results obtained in the BL21 strain (Supplementary Fig. S2). Interestingly, even in the reducing cytoplasmic environment of BL21(DE3) strain, the CSQ-tag promoted proper folding of scFv, allowing its universal application in general expression E. coli system.


### Comparative analysis of the CSQ-tag and conventional tags for soluble expression of scFv
  To compare the ability to enhance the solubility of anti-VEGF-scFv (brolucizumab) in E. coli BL21(DE3) strain, we applied previously reported conventional tags, such as His, SUMO, TRX, GST, and MBP15,28,40. The anti-VEGF-scFv gene was cloned into a conventional N-terminal tag and was expressed under identical culture conditions. Solubility analysis revealed that only the MBP-tag and CSQ-tag significantly enhanced the solubility of scFv. In contrast, scFvs expressed with other conventional tags were essentially insoluble, with solubilities of 0.002% for GST, 0.04% for TRX, and 0% for SUMO (Fig. 3A). The MBP-tag exhibited a solubility of 65.1 ± 4.0%, whereas the CSQ-tag demonstrated a significantly higher solubility of 90.7 ± 2.2% (Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Comparative analysis of the CSQ-tag and conventional tags for soluble expression of scFv.A Western blot analysis of anti-VEGF-scFv fused with SUMO, TRX, MBP, or GST tags expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3). Quantification was based on the determination of linear detection ranges (T, total fraction; S, soluble fraction). SUMO-, TRX-, and MBP-tagged proteins were detected using a mouse anti-hexahistidine antibody, and GST-tagged protein was detected using a GST antibody. B Solubility of anti-VEGF-scFv expressed with five different fusion tags. C Solubility of anti-HER2, anti-CD19, and anti-CD3 scFvs expressed with the MBP-tag. D Comparative solubility of four therapeutic scFvs expressed with the CSQ-tag and with the MBP-tag. Experiments were performed in triplicate. Error bars represent mean ± SD (n = 3).

  Given that MBP exhibited the greatest solubility enhancement and is recognized as a potent solubility-enhancing tag, we evaluated its performance with other therapeutic scFvs. Similarly, anti-HER2 scFv, anti-CD19 scFv, and anti-CD3 scFv exhibited solubilities of 76.6 ± 7.3%, 48.7 ± 4.8%, and 0.02 ± 0.01%, respectively, when expressed with the MBP-tag (Fig. 3C). On average, the MBP-tag resulted in a solubility of 47.6 ± 4.0% across the four therapeutic scFvs, whereas the CSQ tag demonstrated a significantly higher average solubility of 83.8 ± 4.6% (Fig. 3D). Notably, the solubility of the four therapeutic scFvs fused with the CSQ-tag was 1.8-fold higher than that of those fused with the MBP-tag.

  Protein-tags with large net negative charges have been reported to improve the solubility of target proteins41,42. These highly negatively charged protein-tags increase the electrostatic repulsion between the newly synthesized polypeptide chains, thereby reducing the rate of aggregation and allowing the protein to fold into its native structure43. According to these mechanisms, CSQ-tags showed significantly improved solubility of scFv because CSQ-tags exhibit low isoelectric point (pI) values. (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the CSQ-tag, which enhanced the solubility of scFv, had a pI of 3.95. This corresponds with previous reports on CSQ containing numerous acidic amino acid residues, particularly aspartic acid and glutamic acid33,34. These results suggest that the acidity of the CSQ-tag may significantly influence the soluble expression of scFv.


### CSQ-tag possesses significant intrinsically disordered protein characteristics for solubility enhancement
  Disordered regions located at the N-terminus are known to prevent protein aggregation and enhance solubility44–46. These disordered regions possess high conformational freedom due to their lack of secondary structure and act as entropic bristles (EB) by randomly moving and occupying the surrounding space, thereby blocking the approach of other proteins and increasing the solubility of the protein47. CSQ is known as an intrinsically disordered protein with a flexible C-tail domain that lacks well-defined secondary structure34.

  The crystal structures of CSQ have been shown to be highly structured in both Ca2+-free and Ca2+-bound states48. However, in solution, the CSQ has been demonstrated to assume an expanded structure49. We inferred that the structures observed during crystallization may have been induced by surface charge neutralization due to high ionic strength or low pH conditions. To demonstrate that the CSQ-tag is an intrinsically disordered protein in solution, we measured the size of the purified CSQ-tag (Fig. 4A) using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) at various KCl concentrations. The size of CSQ-tag gradually decreasing from 6.23 ± 0.58 nm at 0 mM KCl to 3.93 ± 0.33 nm at 500 mM KCl (Fig. 4B). This demonstrated that the CSQ-tag possesses considerable structural flexibility in response to changes in ionic strength, which is a characteristic of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs).Fig. 4CSQ-tag possesses intrinsically disordered protein characteristics for solubility enhancement.A SDS-PAGE analysis of CSQ-tag. B Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of CSQ-tag at various KCl concentrations to assess its flexible conformation in solution. C CD spectra show the mdeg and ellipticity of the CSQ-tag. D Secondary structure analysis of CSQ-tag by CD Multivariate SSE (Secondary Structure Estimation). E Schematic representation of the three domains (D1, D2, and D3) of CSQ and the design of domain-deletion variants fused to anti-VEGF-scFv.   Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) are known to exhibit characteristic CD spectra with an intense minimum near 200 nm and a low-intensity signal around 222 nm50,51. CSQ-tag displayed a similar CD spectrum (Fig. 4C). Additionally, secondary structure analysis of the CSQ-tag revealed a high proportion of random coils, with 51% of the protein structure exhibiting this characteristic (Fig. 4D). This indicated that the acidic and flexible disordered regions of the CSQ-tag, which were unstructured and lacked well-defined secondary structures, increased the entropy of the protein and reduced its intermolecular attraction. These characteristics suggested that the CSQ-tag prevented protein aggregation and enhanced protein solubility.

  To investigate the mechanistic contribution of the acidic and intrinsically disordered regions of the CSQ-tag to solubility enhancement, we generated domain-deletion variants of CSQ fused to anti-VEGF-scFv (Fig. 4E). CSQ is composed of three structurally similar domains (D1, D2, and D3). Sequence analysis confirmed domain 3 has the highest density of acidic residues and the greatest degree of predicted intrinsic disorder compared with domains 1 and 2 (Supplementary Table 2). Solubility analysis of the CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv deletion variants revealed that the full-length construct CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv exhibited ~90.7% solubility. In contrast, deletion of domain 3 (CSQ(ΔD3)-anti-VEGF-scFv) dramatically reduced solubility to ~13%, whereas deletion of domain 2 (CSQ(ΔD2)-anti-VEGF-scFv) resulted in intermediate solubility of ~76%. Deletion of domain 1 (CSQ(ΔD1)-anti-VEGF-scFv) abolished detectable expression. These differences were clearly demonstrated by SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that domain 3, with its acidic and intrinsically disordered properties, contributes to the solubility-enhancing effect of the CSQ-tag.


### Simple and easy purification of CSQ-scFv with Ca2+-dependent phase-transition properties
  Calsequestrin (CSQ) exhibits a unique phase transition behavior that depends on calcium ions. Inspired by the unique features of Ca2+ -responsive CSQ, we performed calcium-dependent purification using CSQ-scFv fusion proteins. Turbidity measurements are a well-established method for monitoring protein multimerization. Because of the multimerization of the protein with increasing Ca2+ concentration, CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv exhibited a significant increase in turbidity starting at 3 mM Ca2+, with the turbidity value rising from approximately 1.85 (OD350) at 3 mM to 3.34 (OD350) at 6 mM, reaching saturation at concentrations above 6 mM Ca2+ (Fig. 5A). The hydrodynamic size of the CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was 40.9 nm in the absence of Ca2+, increased to 1992 nm in the presence of 6 mM Ca2+, and further expanded to 2878 nm at 8 mM Ca2+, with saturation occurring at concentrations above 8 mM Ca2+ (Fig. 5B). Notably, the Ca2+-induced aggregation of CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was completely reversible upon addition of EDTA. Repeated cycles of Ca2+ and EDTA addition demonstrated the critical behavior of the reverse phase transition (Fig. 5C). ### Characterization of the purified scFv after CSQ-tag removal
  To cleave the CSQ-tag from the fusion protein, purified CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was digested with Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease. As shown in Fig. 6A, SDS-PAGE analysis showed that CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv was efficiently cleaved by the TEV protease. The CSQ-tag and anti-VEGF-scFv were subsequently separated by anion-exchange chromatography and was finally purified anti-VEGF-scFv with the high purity (Fig. 6B). Analytical-grade size-exclusion chromatography (SEC-HPLC) revealed a high level of purity ( > 98%) of anti-VEGF-scFv (Fig. 6C). In addition, the produced scFv was devoid of oligomerization or aggregation and maintained a homogeneous structure. The purification yields of anti-VEGF-scFv are summarized in Table 1. Notably, more than 90% of the scFv was expressed in a soluble fraction without the need for refolding. Approximately 95% of the fusion protein was retrieved after Ca²⁺-dependent precipitation and EDTA resolubilization. Subsequent TEV cleavage and anion-exchange polishing yielded to ~62% of the initial scFv.   Tag removal can further compromise scFv stability and promote the formation of nonfunctional inactive or misfolded aggregates, particularly when the protein is exposed to harsh buffer conditions or prolonged processing times. To confirm the functional activity of the anti-VEGF-scFv, we measured its binding kinetics based on its interaction with VEGF. Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) analysis was performed to determine the corresponding association (Kon) and dissociation (Koff) rate constants, as well as the binding affinity (Kd) (Fig. 6D). Sensogram overlays demonstrated real-time binding interactions between anti-VEGF and scFv in a concentration-dependent manner. The binding affinity of purified anti-VEGF-scFv for VEGF were determined to be 25.8 pM, which was similar to that of developed anti-VEGF scFv drug (Brolucizumab, Kd = 28.4 pM)53.

  In addition, to confirm that intramolecular disulfide bonds were correctly formed in the purified anti-VEGF-scFv (Supplementary Fig. S4), analysis of free thiols using the DTNB assay was performed. The native scFv exhibited 0.0058 mM DTT equivalents, indicating an extremely low level of free thiols, whereas the reduced scFv showed 0.127 mM DTT equivalents, indicating that multiple buried thiol groups became exposed upon reduction. Consistently, non-reducing SDS-PAGE revealed only a monomeric band ( ~ 25 kDa) with no detectable higher–molecular-weight aggregates. To provide direct evidence of intramolecular disulfide bond formation, an NEM–DTT–biotin-maleimide labeling assay was additionally performed. Free thiols were first blocked with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM)54, followed by DTT reduction and biotin-maleimide labeling. A biotin-specific signal was detected only after DTT reduction, demonstrating that buried cysteine residues became accessible upon disulfide bond cleavage, consistent with proper intramolecular disulfide bond formation in the native scFv. These results suggested that the producing scFv using the CSQ-tag did not compromise the structure of the scFv antigen-binding site and maintains functional binding affinity.

  To further establish the broad applicability of the CSQ-tag platform, we evaluated its performance with anti-CD3-scFv, which exhibited the lowest solubility among the therapeutic scFvs examined. CSQ-anti-CD3-scFv was efficiently purified through calcium-dependent precipitation, achieving >90% purity (Supplementary Fig. S5). Nonlinear regression analysis of ELIZA binding curves yielded an apparent dissociation constant (Kd) of 1.91 nM, within the same affinity range as a previously reported anti-CD3 scFv with a Kd of 1.23 nM55. These results confirm that the CSQ-tag platform enables efficient purification and preserves the functional activity of diverse therapeutic scFvs, including those with low intrinsic solubility.


## Discussion
  Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), a type of antibody fragment, are widely used in basic research and the development of diagnostic and therapeutic biopharmaceuticals. Among various expression hosts, Escherichia coli is mainly used for scFv production because it offers the advantages of rapid production and low cost. However, scFv production in E. coli typically faces two problematic issues: inclusion bodies formation due to the low solubility and stability of scFv and the absence of a universal ligand for the purification of scFv. In this study, we demonstrated that the CSQ-tag enhanced the solubility of four different insoluble therapeutic scFvs in the commonly used BL21 strain, despite its reducing cytoplasmic environment. Furthermore, the CSQ-tag enabled calcium-dependent purification, offering a novel and efficient approach for scFv production in E. coli.

  The CSQ-tag enhanced the solubility of four insoluble therapeutic scFvs: anti-VEGF-scFv, anti-CD3-scFv, anti-CD19-scFv, and anti-HER2-scFv. Although all of these were expressed as inclusion bodies when fused to a His-tag, CSQ-tagged scFv had solubilities of 90.7%, 60.4%, 92.7%, and 91.6%, respectively. Notably, the CSQ-tag demonstrated high solubility for all four scFvs, with an average solubility of 83.8 ± 4.6% in the BL21(DE3) strain, despite its reducing cytoplasmic environment, and a similar solubility in the SHuffle T7 strain, which promotes disulfide bond formation. This suggests that the CSQ-tag facilitates proper folding of scFvs even in the reducing environment of common E. coli. This implies that BL21(DE3) can be used instead of SHuffle T7, which is typically employed for disulfide bond formation and soluble expression of scFvs. Consequently, this allows for the utilization of various advantages of the BL21(DE3) strain, including high expression levels, rapid growth, high cell density, and protease deficiency56. Unlike SHuffle T7, which generally exhibits slower growth rates57, BL21(DE3) reaches higher cell densities more rapidly under standard conditions, shortening culture time and increasing volumetric productivity. In addition, its simpler culture requirements reduce operational complexity and costs, offering a more practical and cost-effective platform for scFv production. This demonstrates the potential for universal application of the CSQ-tag in various scFv production processes, leveraging the robustness and efficiency of the BL21(DE3) strain.

  Comparative solubility analysis with commonly used tags, such as His, SUMO, TRX, GST, and MBP revealed that only MBP and CSQ significantly increased the solubility of anti-VEGF-scFv. MBP-tag known as a molecular chaperone, significantly enhances the solubility of scFvs in E. coli. In a study on scFvs against human IL-17A, MBP fusion proteins demonstrated higher solubility compared to other tags, such as NusA, Trx, STII, and pelB15. Our results show that the CSQ-tag exhibited a solubility rate 1.8-fold higher than that of the MBP-tag, which is one of the most widely used solubility-enhancing tags. Notably, even for the most aggregation-prone construct, anti-CD3-scFv, the CSQ-tag significantly improved solubility to 60.4 ± 8.6%, in contrast to the negligible solubility observed with conventional tags, such as His- or MBP-tag. These results demonstrate that the CSQ-tag can universally enhance the solubility of various scFvs to a greater extent than the existing methods.

  The solubility enhancement properties of the CSQ-tag can be attributed to two main factors. First, the acidity of protein fusion partners is generally known to improve the solubility of target proteins. Proteins with a large net negative charge prevent aggregation owing to repulsive forces between negatively charged protein molecules. Negatively charged amino acids bind strongly to water molecules, forming a hydration layer around the proteins. This inhibits protein-protein interactions, thereby suppressing aggregation. The repulsive force due to negative charges prevents the newly synthesized polypeptide chains from folding too quickly, which helps reduce the formation of inappropriate intermediates and promotes correct folding. The CSQ-tag is an acidic protein with 28.5% acidic amino acid residues, such as aspartic and glutamic acids, and a low pI of 3.95. This suggests that the low acidity of the CSQ-tag influences the soluble expression of scFv.

  Second, intrinsically disordered proteins exist in a flexible ensemble state that dynamically transitions between various structural forms without a fixed three-dimensional structure. Disordered regions (Intrinsically Disordered Regions, IDRs) located at the N-terminus act as entropic bristles (EB) that enhance protein solubility and prevent aggregation. The random motion of EBs forms a large exclusion zone around the protein, physically hindering the approach of other protein molecules and thereby inhibiting aggregation and aiding protein solubility. We demonstrated that the CSQ-tag tag is an intrinsically disordered protein. The CSQ-tag showed considerable structural flexibility, decreasing from 6.23 nm at 0 mM KCl to 3.93 nm at 500 mM KCl, depending on the ionic changes. Furthermore, the CSQ-tag exhibited a characteristic CD spectrum of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) with a strong minimum intensity near 200 nm, a low-intensity signal near 222 nm, and a secondary structure with approximately 51% random coil.

  To determine whether specific acidic and intrinsically disordered regions of CSQ directly contribute to its solubility-enhancing effect, we generated domain-deletion variants of CSQ-anti-VEGF-scFv. solubility analysis revealed that deletion of domain 3 (ΔD3), which is enriched in acidic residues and intrinsically disordered, resulted in a dramatic reduction of solubility to ~13%, whereas the full-length construct exhibited ~90.7% solubility. These results indicate that domain 3 is an important determinant of the solubility-enhancing effect of the CSQ-tag and suggest that its acidic and intrinsically disordered regions contribute to improving the solubility of scFv.

  We established a calcium-dependent purification method that can be scaled up to large-batch processes by utilizing the Ca2+-dependent phase transition properties of the CSQ-tag, achieving a high purity of over 95.2%. The CSQ-based affinity precipitation method has the advantages of technical simplicity, rapid processing, and low cost. The only reagents and equipment required for protein purification were CaCl2, EDTA, and a centrifuge. In addition, the CSQ-based calcium-dependent purification method overcomes the low recovery issues of traditional precipitation methods, such as ammonium sulfate or TCA precipitation, and addresses the challenges faced by most scFv production processes that lack a universal ligand, except for affinity chromatography. The CSQ-based calcium-dependent purification method offers several significant advantages over traditional affinity chromatography techniques, such as immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) and protein L. First, the entire purification process can be completed within 1 h owing to the rapid precipitation and elution steps. Second, the method is cost-effective because it uses inexpensive reagents, avoiding the high costs associated with affinity resins used in IMAC or Protein L chromatography. Third, the precipitation process is easily scalable for large-batch production, which addresses the key limitations of column-based methods. Finally, our calcium-dependent purification method addresses the challenges faced by most scFv production processes that lack a universal ligand.

  For biochemical studies and therapeutic applications, it is crucial for proteins to maintain their stable structure and solubility even after tag removal1,58. The removal of fusion tags can interfere with the folding stability of the remaining target protein, potentially compromising its functionality. This is particularly important for scFv molecules, which exhibit lower stability and an increased risk of aggregation due to transient dissociation and inter-molecular reassociation of the two domains (VL and VH)59,60. Therefore, we confirmed that the functional structure of scFv was maintained during the CSQ-tag removal process. The binding affinity of the produced anti-VEGF-scFv was evaluated to be 25.8 pM for VEGF, which is similar to the binding affinity of 28.4 pM for the previously known anti-VEGF-scFv (brolucizumab). In addition, to validate the broader applicability of the CSQ platform, we evaluated anti-CD3-scFv, which showed the lowest solubility among the four therapeutic scFvs, and found that the CSQ-tag platform enabled efficient production and preserved its functional binding activity.

  Despite these promising results, several limitations should be noted. Although the calcium-dependent precipitation and resolubilization steps achieved high recovery ( > 93%) and purity ( > 95%), yield analysis revealed that substantial product loss occurs during the downstream TEV cleavage and ion-exchange chromatography steps, indicating that further optimization will be necessary to maximize overall yield. In addition, the relatively large size of the CSQ-tag ( ~ 45 kDa) means that a significant portion of the expressed fusion protein is removed during cleavage, thereby reducing the amount of recovered scFv. Therefore, further optimization is required to improve yield and cost-effectiveness, and additional research is necessary to establish the optimal production workflow for industrial applications. We have demonstrated that the CSQ-tag enables the successful production of therapeutic scFvs, and this strategy is likely applicable to a broad range of recombinant proteins beyond scFvs. In our ongoing follow-up studies, we are investigating more practical and scalable implementations of this method, as well as the potential therapeutic applications of CSQ-tagged proteins.

  In summary, this study introduces the CSQ-tag as a novel solution for scFv production. The CSQ-tag promotes proper folding of scFvs, even in the reducing environments of common BL21(DE3) E. coli, enabling more universal application in scFv production processes. The acidic characteristics and disordered structure of the CSQ-tag appear to contribute to the improved protein solubility, demonstrating that the CSQ-tag is more effective than conventional tags. Furthermore, the calcium-dependent purification method utilizing the Ca2+-dependent phase transition properties of the CSQ-tag provides a simple, easy, and efficient purification method suitable for large-scale production. Most importantly, the anti-VEGF-scFv antibody produced using the CSQ-tag showed similar binding affinity to the existing therapeutic brolucizumab, proving that this system can greatly improve productivity while maintaining the functional integrity of scFv. These results demonstrate that the two-in-one CSQ-tag enables soluble expression and that calcium-dependent purification provides a novel tool for the production of scFvs and other therapeutic proteins.
