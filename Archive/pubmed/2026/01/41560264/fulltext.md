# Tuning nuclear rheology through transient chromatin cross-links


## Abstract
  In eukaryotic cells, the nucleolus is a pivotal subnuclear organelle, instrumental in ribosomal RNA synthesis and nuclear organization. Although the unique viscoelastic properties of the nucleolus are associated with transient interactions between chromatin and regulatory proteins, the specific mechanistic details driving nucleolar phase separation and mechanical responses have remained largely undefined. In this study, we employ a computational approach to elucidate chromatin-protein interactions within the nucleolus of budding yeast, using a sophisticated bead-spring polymer model. This model integrates DNA and nucleolar architectures with dynamic simulations of interactions involving chromosomal structural maintenance proteins and rDNA transcriptional regulators through systematically varied cross-linking kinetics. Our findings reveal that modulations in protein-DNA interactions critically dictate the phase behavior, relaxation dynamics, and viscoelastic properties of the nucleolus, underscoring a complex but precise regulatory mechanism at play. Notably, protein-mediated bridging emerges as a critical factor enhancing nucleolar condensation and modulating stress relaxation, highlighting the transformative role of transient cross-linking in nuclear mechanics regulation. These insights not only deepen our understanding of nucleolar function but also open avenues for interventions in genetic engineering and disease therapeutics.


## INTRODUCTION
  The cell nucleus serves as a three-dimensional hub for information processing, where an organism’s genetic blueprint is precisely organized and regulated [1,2]. The mechanical stability and dynamic reorganization of the genome depend on the coordinated assembly of multiscale nuclear compartments [3–6]. Among these, the nucleolus is the most prominent membrane-less compartment, distinguished by its unique viscoelastic properties. These properties result from dynamic cross-linking interactions among the chromatin scaffold, structural maintenance proteins (e.g., SMC complexes), and transcriptional regulators such as Hmo1 [7–9]. Although the crescent-shaped topology of the nucleolus in budding yeast, anchored to the nuclear membrane and oppositely placed from the spindle pole body during G1, is well documented, the specific rheological mechanisms imparting morphological adaptability throughout the cell cycle remain elusive [3,4,10–12]. In particular, the quantitative dynamics by which transient protein-DNA cross-links modulate the nucleolus’s viscoelastic response have not been determined. Given the nucleolus’s central role in ribosomal RNA synthesis and cellular stress responses, aberrations in its mechanical behavior have been increasingly linked to diseases such as cancer and neurodegenerative disorders, underscoring the broader biological significance of understanding its dynamics [13–15].

  Traditional static cross-link models fail to capture the frequency-dependent viscoelastic responses observed during transcriptional oscillations [16–18]. Furthermore, the interplay between cross-link lifetimes and chromatin relaxation timescales has not been quantitatively addressed [19,20]. Importantly, the combined regulation of nuclear mechanics by multilevel cross-link networks, which involve both long- and short-range interactions mediated by diverse proteins, remains unexplored [21]. These gaps limit our ability to understand how the nucleolus simultaneously maintains structural stability and enables efficient remodeling of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) transcription factories. At the microscopic level, structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) complexes such as cohesin and condensin mediate an important class of chromatin–protein interactions. These complexes have been widely modeled and studied through the well-established loop extrusion mechanism [22–29]. In these models, loop-extruding factors (LEFs) dynamically associate and dissociate with chromatin (binding and unbinding kinetics), and translocate along the fiber to extrude chromatin loops, thereby organizing large-scale genomic architecture. Thus, loop extrusion is a specific realization of dynamic cross-linking that builds on stochastic binding and unbinding, enhanced by directional translocation for topological reorganization. Although loop extrusion can influence chromatin organization across a broad range of genomic scales [30], our focus here is to elucidate how stochastic binding and unbinding kinetics directly regulate local viscoelastic behavior, particularly within subnuclear compartments such as the nucleolus. In addition, loops can also arise spontaneously from the entropic behavior of confined polymers [3,31], independent of active extrusion mechanisms. Therefore, our goal in this study is to establish a more general framework for analyzing how dynamic binding and unbinding processes influence chromatin organization and mechanics, without explicitly incorporating loop extrusion in the model and simulations. We choose one of the experimentally known mechanisms for inducing dynamic structural organization in the yeast genome and show in this study that transient cross-linking within the nucleolus is, by itself, sufficient to regulate the local nucleolar viscoelasticity, including its relaxation spectrum and dynamic moduli.

  Recent breakthroughs at the intersection of soft condensed matter physics and chromatin conformation capture technologies have established new paradigms for understanding nuclear compartmentalization [32–35]. Advances in stochastic polymer modeling have revealed that both protein-mediated affinity interactions and chromatin cross-linking kinetics can drive the phase separation and compartmentalization of nuclear substructures, particularly when the timescales of cross-link dynamics are comparable to polymer relaxation timescales [4,36–38]. Meanwhile, recent single-cell studies and femtosecond laser-induced cross-linking mass spectrometry (fliX-MS) technology have achieved unprecedented resolution in resolving RNA/DNA-protein interaction dynamics [39–41]. Investigations of chromatin cross-linking proteins, notably HP1α, have revealed that their binding and exchange kinetics are highly dynamic and sensitive to chromatin condensation, suggesting that such transient bridging dynamics may play an important role in regulating the viscoelastic and mechanical behavior of the nucleus [42,43]. Moreover, stochastic simulations based on entropy-driven bead-spring polymer models capture many features of chromatin dynamics within the yeast nucleus [3,4,44–47]. These polymer simulations underscore the essential role of chromatin cross-linkers in maintaining nuclear mechanical integrity, providing critical insights into the mechanisms underlying nucleolar stability. Previous studies have explored the roles and impacts of transient cross-linking kinetics of chromosomal DNA in the nucleus of live budding yeast by SMC proteins and rDNA transcriptional regulators [4,48,49]. In these studies, simulations (using statistical polymer physics models of all 16 yeast chromosomes in the nucleus during interphase) and experimental imaging of the nucleolus in live yeast are combined to identify the minimal requirements to sequester the rDNA from the remainder of the nuclear genome [4]. Dynamic cluster detection metrics applied to the complete interphase simulation dataset revealed how the timescales of transient rDNA-chromatin cross-links tune dynamic nucleolar structure and organization (and phase separation against the nuclear wall) [48].

  Crucially, to directly probe and quantify the mechanical consequences of the fundamental binding and unbinding kinetics that underpin all forms of dynamic cross-linking, while excluding the stochastic translocation kinetics of loop extrusion in this study to isolate the effects of transient cross-linking kinetics, we employ a dynamic cross-linking framework. This approach allows us to isolate the contribution of stochastic attachment and detachment (transient cross-link binding and unbinding) dynamics to nucleolar viscoelasticity, deliberately abstracting away the directional translocation or extrusion component characteristic of loop extrusion models. Therefore, although loop extrusion models share the fundamental binding and unbinding kinetics with our transient cross-linkers, the additional directional motion and topological reorganization they describe are not the primary focus of the present investigation into nucleolar rheology. Instead, we focus specifically on the mechanical relaxation governed by the stochastic bridging interactions themselves. In this study we introduce a dynamic cross-link coarse-grained polymer model to reproduce the previous statistical physics simulations of the live yeast genome during interphase, and systematically investigate the regulatory effects of transient protein-DNA interactions on nucleolar rheology. By coarse graining across the input parameter space of cross-link formation and dissociation rates, we construct nine distinct kinetic scenarios to model nucleolar chromatin dynamics [Fig. 2(c) below] [20,50,51]. In the following section, we provide a summary of this model setup to contextualize the detailed analyses that follow.


## SIMULATION MODEL AND COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS
  In our chromosome system, we adopt the LJ system of units, where the length unit is b, the energy unit is ϵ0, and the mass unit is m. The time unit is defined as τ0=bm/ϵ0, which corresponds to the vibrational timescale of a single bead in the LJ potential. Temperature is normalized using ϵ0 and Boltzmann constant kB=1. A total of 16 chromosomes (32 arms) are modeled as coarse-grained bead-spring polymer chains without explicit twist or bending potential, i.e., bonds are freely rotating and freely jointed, constrained only by excluded-volume interactions [3,4]. Each bead, with a diameter b, represents a spherical Kuhn monomer that interacts via a shifted Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential [52]  (1) ULJ(r)=4ϵ0br12-br6-brc12+brc6,  where the cutoff distance is fixed at rc=2.5b for all simulations. Connectivity between monomers, which includes both covalent nearest-neighbor bonds and transient non-nearestneighbor cross-links, is defined using a finitely extensible nonlinear elastic (FENE) potential [51,53]:  (2) UFENE(r)=-0.5κR02ln1-rR02+4ϵ0dr12-dr6+ϵ0,r<R0,  where κ=30ϵ0/b2 is the spring constant, R0=1.5b stands for the maximum extension of the bond, and d set to 1.2b for covalent bonds and b for transient bonds.

  We have adopted NVE ensemble and Langevin thermostat in LAMMPS. The equation of motion for each bead, under an implicit solvent that neglects hydrodynamic interactions, is described by Langevin dynamics [20,54]:  (3) md2ridt2=-∇iU-ζdridt+Wit,  where m=m0 is the monomer mass, ri is the position of the ith bead, and ζ is the friction coefficient. Here U denotes the total conservative potential (including both FENE and LJ contributions) acting on bead i, and Wi(t) represents a stochastic white noise force with zero drift and variance proportional to the temperature and ζ. While viscous drag is included on each bead, hydrodynamic interactions between beads and chains are neglected (free draining approximation). Simulations are performed using the LAMMPS molecular dynamics package at a constant temperature T=ϵ0/kB. Initial configurations for simulation are based on the pre-equilibrated 3D genome structure of budding yeast from Refs. [3,4], which capture key spatial constraints such as centromere clustering and nucleolar anchoring. Simulations are conducted in a cubic box of size lbox=30b with periodic boundary conditions, where the box length lbox=30b is chosen to be significantly larger than the maximum extent of the chromosome chains to eliminate any boundary effects. After 1.5×106τ0 of equilibration, we run 2.1×106τ0 for stress relaxation analysis and an additional 1.5×106τ0 for Rouse mode analysis.

  The ends of each coarse-grained polymer chain (corresponding to a chromosome arm) are tethered via strong attractive interactions. One end attaches to a centromere-spindle pole body tether, while the other connects to a telomere-nuclear membrane tether, as listed in Table I. Table I also presents the number of beads assigned per chromosome arm and explicitly maps each Chain ID (1–32) to a specific chromosome and its arm 1 or arm 2. For simplicity, we label each chromosome arm as “arm 1” and “arm 2” according to their chain indices in the simulation model. This labeling does not necessarily correspond to the biological p and q arms. In the simulation, telomeres and centromeres were fixed at fixed positions in space, with tethering positions based on previous in vivo observations of yeast chromosomes (see Table II). In our model, the nucleolus is represented by a 361-bead corresponding to the arm 2 of chromosome XII. Each bead represents approximately 5 kilobase pairs of genomic DNA, thus covering ~1.8Mb in total. This coarse graining is consistent with previous polymer models of the yeast genome [3,4]. This information is visualized in a schematic diagram (Fig. 1) to enhance clarity. All beads not assigned to the nucleolus are designated as nonnucleolar. Importantly, the pair interactions among all beads are identical and defined by the LJ potential above [3,4]; the only distinction is that nucleolar beads are selectively assigned dynamic, transient cross-linking interactions, reflecting a modeling assumption motivated by prior studies suggesting SMC protein enrichment at the rDNA locus [55,56]. However, direct evidence for preferential and dynamic SMC binding to the nucleolus remains limited, and this selective implementation serves as a working hypothesis to explore potential rheological consequences.

  In the simulations, each nucleolus bead is capable of forming transient bonds [20,50,51]. Transient bond formation: At every time interval τB, which serves as a proxy for both the concentration of cross-linkers and their accessibility to binding sites on the nucleolus chain, a search for new transient cross-links is performed. If, at any given τB, the separation rij between any two free nucleolus beads i and j is less than the predefined critical distance rB=1.0b (fixed throughout this study), a transient bond is formed between the beads with probability PB. This probability reflects the binding affinity between the nucleolus bead pair. Consequently, the kinetics of transient bond formation is governed by the two tuning parameters, τB and PB. For simplicity, the bond formation kinetics are classified into three categories: fast binding (FB), characterized by the shortest τB and highest PB; medium binding (MB), with an intermediate τB and lower PB; and slow binding (SB), associated with the longest τB and lowest PB. It should be noted that, in this study, nucleolus beads are restricted to forming divalent transient bonds. Transient bond breakage: At every interval τU, each transient bond formed during the bond formation process has a probability PU to break. Analogously, the bond breakage kinetics are categorized into three groups: fast unbinding (FU), corresponding to the shortest τU and highest PU; medium unbinding (MU), with a longer τU and lower PU; and slow unbinding (SU), associated with the longest τU and lowest PU. In total, a matrix of nine binding-unbinding kinetic cases is explored. The specific values of τB,PB,τU, and PU for each case are provided in Table III. In practice, the combination of τB,PB,τU, and PU determines the cross-link density cXlink and the bond lifetime τlife. The lifetime is defined as the time Δτ at which the survival probability of a bond PXlink(Δτ)=1-PUΔτ/τU falls to 1/2. For each case, after the number of cross-links stabilizes, we can directly count the number of transient cross-links at any time, and then compute cXlink by averaging the number of cross-links formed between nucleolar beads over 1 × 105 states, and normalize by the number of unique bead pairs i.e., 180. Table IV summarizes cXlink and τlife values for all kinetic cases (e.g., see Supplemental Material Ref. [57] for additional details).

  In our model, the binding and unbinding kinetics are defined as input parameters. Binding kinetics is related to the ability or probability of nucleolus chain segments to form transient cross-links under the help of condensins in a biological system. A faster binding kinetics simulates essentially a biological environment that, for example, contains more condensins and/or condensins have a higher probability to meet and (attractively) interact with nucleolus chain segments, binding nucleolus chain segments to form transient cross-links more easily. The unbinding kinetics is modeled as representing the lifetime of existing transient cross-links, i.e., the time duration of condensins binding together nucleolus chain segments. A slower unbinding kinetics corresponds to, for example, a biological environment, in which condensins (attractively) interact more strongly with nucleolus chain segments, which increases the resistance against thermal fluctuations and thus enhances the average lifetime of the transient bonds. The unbinding rate solely determines τlife. For a fixed unbinding rate (and thus a constant τlife), the bulk density of cross-links, cXlink, increases with the binding rate. On the other hand, at a fixed binding kinetics, both τlife and cXlink increase with a reduction of the unbinding kinetics. Therefore, while τlife is controlled exclusively by the unbinding kinetics, cXlink is jointly determined by both the binding and unbinding kinetics.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
  We simulate 32 chromosome arms as coarse-grained bead-spring polymer chains tethered at their telomeres and centromere, with the nucleolus modeled as a designated 361-bead subchain on the 24th arm. The equilibrium conformation of chromosomal strands in nucleus that have not undergone transient cross-linking between nucleolus beads is shown in Fig. 2(a). Transient cross-linking, mediated by SMC proteins, is introduced exclusively among the nucleolus beads via tunable binding (τB,PB) and unbinding (τU,PU) kinetics. This framework enables us to systematically explore how variable transient cross-link dynamics influence chromatin conformations, viscoelastic properties, and relaxation processes. Figure 2(c) displays the equilibrium conformations for nine different transient cross-link cases. The highest cross-link density, cXlink, is observed when the bond formation kinetics exhibit fast binding and slow unbinding (FBSU), whereas the lowest cXlink occurs when the kinetics involve slow binding and fast unbinding (SBFU). Across the range of cXlink values, the nucleolus condensed state varies markedly: lower cXlink values correspond to a more dispersed nucleolus distribution, while higher cXlink values yield a more concentrated nucleolus. These observations are consistent with the experimental image replotted from the Ref. [4] and shown in Fig. 2(b).

  The MD simulation-based stress relaxation modulus G(t) is computed to quantify the time-dependent structural evolution of the nucleolus subchain and the nonnucleolus chromatin chains. There exists a standard protocol that relates G(t) to the stress autocorrelation function (SACF) of the off-diagonal components of the system stress tensor via the Green-Kubo relation [58–60]:  (4) G(t)=VkBTSACF(t),  where V is the system volume and the temperature is maintained at kBT=ϵ0, with ϵ0 being the energy unit in the simulations. In the simulations, the stress tensor is computed as a six-component vector with components σxx,σyy,σzz,σxy,σyz, and σxz. For instance, the σxx component is calculated as  (5) σxx=1V∑k=1Nmmvkx2+∑k=1Nrkxfkx,  where rkx and fkx denote the x components of the position and force vectors for the kth bead. The stress autocorrelation function is computed according to  (6) SACF(t)=13σxy(t)σxy(0)+σyz(t)σyz(0)+σxz(t)σxz(0),  where σxy,σyz, and σxz are the off-diagonal components of the stress tensor. When calculating the stress relaxation modulus for the nucleolus, only the nucleolus beads are considered; similarly, for the nonnucleolus, only the corresponding beads are selected. Prior to initiating the cross-linking kinetics for the nucleolus, the stress relaxation functions for the nucleolus and nonnucleolus were computed separately.

  As shown in Fig. 3(a), the stress relaxation function for the nucleolus eventually decays, whereas that for the nonnucleolus does not. Consequently, the simulation results can be fitted using Eq. (7) for partially entangled polymer chains and the Eq. (8) for permanently cross-linked chains, respectively [51,61]:  (7) Gt=kBTnmtτmonγ+Gente-tτcha,   (8) G(t)=kBTnmtτmonγ+Gent.  We attribute the lack of stress relaxation in the nonnucleolus to the absence of conformational relaxation of the nonnucleolar strands, as a result of the tethering of the chromatin strand ends and that the presence of long strand tangles preserve the overall chromatin structure. In contrast, the nucleolus, located in the middle of the 24th arm, has relatively free ends, allowing its conformation to relax completely over long timescales. To further reveal the viscoelastic properties of the nucleolus and nonnucleolus, Fourier transforms of the respective G(t) functions were computed in obtaining the storage (elastic) modulus G′(ω) and the loss (viscous) modulus G″(ω):  (9a) G′ω=Gter+ω∫0∞Gt-Gtersinωtdt,   (9b) G″(ω)=ω∫0∞G(t)-Gtercos(ωt)dt.  The meaning of Gter is the modulus of viscoelastic material at equilibrium or over a long timescale. In practice, the functions G(t) used in these integrals were not raw simulation outputs, but analytical expressions obtained by fitting the simulation data using Eqs. (7) and (8). This approach reduces the impact of statistical noise and artifacts associated with limited time windows. The Fourier integrals were then evaluated numerically using these fitted functions to obtain G′(ω) and G″(ω). As shown in Fig. 3(b), the nonnucleolus chromatin chains behave more like elastic gel with the elastic modulus becoming increasingly larger than the loss modulus at decreasing shearing frequencies. In contrast, the nucleolus subchain displays viscoelastic behavior, similar to that of partially entangled polymer chains [4,37,45,62]. While our simulations provide a theoretical framework for interpreting the viscoelastic behavior of the nucleolus, we note that experimental evidence also supports the presence of time-dependent mechanical responses in nucleolar structures. Although detailed rheological characterization of the nucleolus remains limited, recent studies have captured signatures of nucleolar fluidity and viscoelasticity using live-cell imaging and microrheology techniques. For example, Feric et al. demonstrated that the nucleolus consists of coexisting liquid phases with distinct surface tensions and viscosities, supporting a model in which internal dynamics are governed by viscoelastic phase behavior [63]. Similarly, Brangwynne et al. observed coalescence and deformation dynamics in Xenopus nucleoli, consistent with a fluid-like yet mechanically constrained material [64]. These findings support the plausibility of modeling the nucleolus as a viscoelastic polymeric domain, dynamically regulated by transient internal interactions such as cross-linking.

  Since the nonnucleolus chromatin chains do not relax, while the nucleolus subchain relaxes, Rouse-mode analysis was performed to quantify the relaxation spectra of the nonnucleolus subchain across all lengthscales. The averaged autocorrelation function of the pth Rouse mode, which describes the relaxation dynamics of a polymer subchain containing N-1p beads, is defined as [65,66]  (10) Xp(t)=2N1/2∑i=1NricospπNi-12.  The obtained autocorrelation functions can be fitted at all length scales by a stretched exponential function commonly used to describe the structural relaxation of unentangled subchains:  (11) Xp(t)⋅Xp(0)=Xp2exp-tτpβp,  where τp is the relaxation time of the pth mode and βp is the corresponding stretching factor. Here βp=1 corresponds to a simple exponential decay, whereas βp<1 indicates a broader distribution of relaxation times. This stretched exponential form is not merely a convenient fitting tool, but a well-established functional form for capturing relaxation in polymer systems with dynamic heterogeneity. Prior studies have shown that disordered, cross-linked, or glassy polymers often exhibit stretched exponential behavior due to spatially varying constraints and segmental friction [65,66]. In our simulations, the chromatin polymer experiences heterogeneous local environments due to both centromere-telomere tethering and transient intranucleolar cross-links. These features naturally introduce a spectrum of relaxation times across different segments, which is compactly described by the stretching exponent βp. Notably, the stretching factor βp, as shown in Fig. 4(c), increases with increasing p, which indicates that shorter segments approach single-exponential relaxation (i.e., βp→1). This trend suggests a more homogeneous dynamic environment for localized motions, whereas longer segments (lower p) experience greater heterogeneity in their relaxation pathways, likely due to complex environmental constraints and intermolecular interactions. The effective monomeric relaxation rate, as shown in Fig. 4(b), is computed as  (12) Weff=14τpeffsin2pπ2N.  The effective monomeric relaxation rate Weff shows a marked increase with increasing p. This observation highlights the growing influence of local friction on shorter segments (higher p), while longer chain segments (lower p) are more affected by collective friction and spatial constraints, which significantly impede their relaxation. These findings underscore the critical role of length-scale-dependent dynamics in governing the relaxation processes within the chromosome ensemble [67,68]. The quantities analyzed here—such as the stress relaxation modulus and Rouse mode spectra—provide indirect but informative proxies for the viscoelastic and dynamic behavior of the nucleolus. Such metrics have been experimentally linked to nucleolar fluidity and morphology in yeast and higher eukaryotes, including shape fluctuations, internal flows, and fusion behavior [63,64]. Our modeling framework aims to connect these observables with underlying cross-linking kinetics.

  After introducing transient cross-linking between nucleolus beads, as shown in Fig. 5, we first computed the radial distribution functions ϕAArbb and ϕABrbb to quantify the local distribution of nucleolus subchain and the nonnucleolus chromatin chains. As the cross-link density cXlink increases, the density of nucleolus beads in close proximity to each other (at short distances, rbb≈0) also increases, leading to progressively larger deviations from the baseline system without transient cross-linking. At low cross-link densities (e.g., cXlink=0.013), the radial distributions of nucleolus and nonnucleolus beads around nucleolus beads remain close to those of the baseline system. In contrast, at high cross-link densities (e.g., cXlink=0.91), the radial distributions deviate substantially from the baseline, reflecting a highly condensed nucleolus morphology driven by the high density of transient cross-links. This morphological condensation is consistent with experimental observations showing that nucleolar structure can dynamically reorganize in response to molecular crowding, transcriptional activity, and changes in cross-linking components [4,69].

  To reveal quantitatively the influence of transient cross-links on the structural relaxation of the nucleolus subchain and the nonnucleolus chromatin chains, as shown in Fig. 6, we calculated the stress relaxation modulus for both nucleolus and nonnucleolus chains, at different cXlink of transient cross-linking between nucleolus beads. Compared to the benchmark system without transient cross-linking, the stress relaxation modulus of the nonnucleolus chains is essentially unaffected by the transient cross-linking kinetics of the nucleolus. In contrast, the stress relaxation modulus of the nucleolus chains is sensitively influenced by transient cross-links. In particular, when the cross-link density is in an intermediate range, the stress relaxation modulus is markedly elevated relative to the no-cross-linking reference system, and its structure does not relax within the simulated timescale. This result suggests that the dynamic mechanical properties of the nucleolus are not maximized at the highest degree of structural condensation (i.e., maximum cross-link density), but rather at moderate cross-link densities where the strongest mechanical response is achieved. This nonmonotonic behavior may arise from the interplay between transient cross-linking and dynamic structural organization, which together influence the network’s ability to retain stress. At weak cross-linking, the relaxation modulus G(t) remains low because the nucleolar chain is effectively in a loose, un-cross-linked state, which relaxes rapidly due to the absence of cross-link-locked and -strengthened entanglement of nucleolar chain segments with surrounding nonnucleolar chains. In this case, the nucleolar chain segments are sparsely distributed within the matrix of nonnucleolar chains. At high cross-link densities, the nucleolar chains form a compact cluster (as shown in Fig. 2), inside which there is no interpenetration of nonnucleolar chain segments, as is indicated by the radial distribution functions shown in Fig. 5. This clustering thus limits the ability of the nucleolar beads to entangle effectively with neighboring nonnucleolar chains, leading again to a relatively fast relaxation and thus to a low G(t). In contrast, at intermediate cross-linking, the nucleolar chains and nonnucleolar chains intermix more effectively under the assistance of cross-links, which promotes chain entanglements and consequently delays the relaxation of nucleolar chains.

  The segments of the nucleolus chain were analyzed using Rouse mode analysis under different cXlink of transient cross-linking between nucleolus beads. When comparing the effective monomeric relaxation rates, Weff, of the nucleolus chain with those of the no-cross-linking reference system, we observe that higher cross-link densities result in a greater deviation from the reference rates, particularly for larger mode numbers p, as shown in Fig. 7. This suggests that the relaxation of short chain segments is more significantly influenced by the cross-linking kinetics when the cross-link density is high. In other words, transient cross-linking effectively slows down the monomeric relaxation of short chain segments. When comparing the stretching factor βp of the nucleolus chain to that of the no-cross-linking reference system, we observe that higher cross-link densities cause βp to deviate further from the reference values, especially for larger mode numbers p. In some cases, the correlation appears to be reversed. This suggests that the relaxation behavior of the chain segments is substantially altered at high cross-link densities: the relaxation of long chain segments becomes more homogeneous, while that of short chain segments becomes significantly more heterogeneous due to dynamic transient cross-linking.


## CONCLUSIONS
  In this study, computational modeling of chromatin-protein interactions within the nucleus of budding yeast highlights the critical impact of protein-mediated bridging on nucleolar rheology. Through comparative analyses across various models, differing in transient cross-linking kinetics, we have identified a nonlinear relationship between cross-link density and the dynamic mechanical property of the nucleolus subchain, where optimal cross-linking balances complex dynamics with mechanical stability. The radial distribution relationship between the beads confirms the correlation between cross-link density and the nucleolus’ condensed properties. A larger cross-link density corresponds to a more condensed state of nucleolus. By contrast, we find through statistic computation of the stress relaxation modulus the nonmonotonic correlation between transient cross-links and viscoelastic response by changing the density and lifetime of transient cross-links. In addition, the application of Rouse mode analysis quantifies the nonmonotonic effect of different cross-link densities and lifetimes of cross-links on the multiscale relaxation processes. By quantifying the role of transient cross-linking on regulating the dynamic structure and mechanical property of the nucleolus, these findings establish a mechanical framework for understanding nucleolar structural remodeling during transcription and lay the groundwork for elucidating rheological anomalies in pathological nuclear compartments. Viable methodologies for the strategic manipulation of chromatin-protein interactions may pave the way for a more profound comprehension and manipulation of nuclear mechanics in both health and pathological states through innovative genetic and therapeutic interventions [70–72]. We defer to a future study how the results herein are perturbed by superimposing loop extrusion kinetics into the modeling baseline presented.
