# Droplet growth, Ostwald’s rule, and emergence of order in Fused in Sarcoma


## Significance
  The unstructured part of an RNA-binding protein (FUS-LC) that undergoes phase separation has three regions that form mutually exclusive, nonpolymorphic cross-β cores. The C-terminal core (core-3) forms first, while the fibril forming core-2 does not influence phase separation. The most stable N-terminal core-1 assembles last. The molecular basis for the observed anomalous behavior is unknown. Simulations show that the kinetics and relative stabilities of fibrils, determined by rarely populated high free energy excited structured states encoded in the monomer, are inversely correlated. Core-3 structure, predicted using AlphaFold, and simulations show that β-strand emerges in this region early during the droplet formation, and drives the assembly of FUS-LC. Implications for fibril formation in related low-complexity sequences are pointed out.


## Results

### Ostwald’s Rule of Stages.
  To establish the relationship between the kinetics of formation of the three cores and their stabilities with respect to the random coil (RC), which is the ground state, we used the N∗ theory (summarized in SI Appendix) posits that within the native basin of attraction of the monomer high free-energy conformations are populated with low probability. In particular, the N∗ states resemble structural motifs found in amyloid fibrils (28–30).

  The conformations belonging to the N∗ state are determined using long equilibrium simulations. We used the structural overlap function, χ (SI Appendix, Eq. S3), describing the simi-larity between the conformation of the monomer and the structure in the fibril to characterize the excited states. For core-1 (core-2), we used fibril structure determined using solid-state NMR (20) (PDB ID: 5W3N; Fig. 5A) [cryo-EM (23) (PDB ID: 6XFM; SI Appendix, Fig. S15)]. We used AlphaFold2 (31) and AlphaFold3 (32) to generate structures for the prediction core-3 fibril (SI Appendix, Results). We define the N∗ states as fibril-like conformations with structural overlap parameters χfib.i≥χc.i, where i=1,2,3 denotes the core. The procedure for selecting the values of χc.i is described in SI Appendix, Methods. The N∗-states are populated in the conformational ensemble of monomers at extremely low levels (usually less than 0.1%). It was shown (20, 24) that core-1 is only modestly more stable than core-2, which makes the absence of core-2 fibrils in FUS-LC (23) puzzling. Moreover, neither estimates of the time scales of formation of the fibril-like structures of the three cores nor the fate of core-3 has been investigated in experiments.

  To establish a relation between the kinetics of formation of the three cores to their stabilities, we calculated the distribution of first passage times, P(τFPT)=1M∑k=1Mδ(τFPT−τ1k), where τFPT is the first time at which the transition from the random coil (RC) ground state to the corresponding N∗ state occurs in the kth trajectory. We associated τFPT with the time at which a structure with χfib.i≥χc.i is reached for the first time in various trajectories. Using 200 independent Brownian dynamics simulations (Methods), each lasting 280 ms, we observed 42 transitions to core-1, 87 transitions to core-2, 116 transitions to the U-bend conformation of core-3, and 124 transitions to the S-bend conformation of core-3. The smallest number of transitions to core-1 suggests that it is kinetically least accessible.

  Because the first passage time (FPT) is not reached in all the trajectories, only truncated distributions of FPTs were sampled (Fig. 1B–D) in the simulations. Consequently, only the lower bounds for the true mean FPTs can be estimated. The error bars represent the SE of the mean. We could conclude, based on the calculated MFPTs, that the time for forming core-3 is the shortest, while core-1 has the longest formation time. Because only a small fraction of the trajectories reach the N∗ states, there is uncertainty in reaching this conclusion, which we resolve using a method described below.

  To assess the statistical significance of the differences in the mean first-passage times (MFPTs) to the various N∗ states, we conducted pairwise comparisons between the MFPT values using Welch’s unequal variances t test (33). The MFPT for core-1 (129.17±11.73 ms) is significantly greater than for core-2 (97.47±8.70 ms), the U-bend conformation of core-3 (94.17±6.59 ms), and the S-bend conformation of core-3 (99.41±6.61 ms), with P-values between 0.02 and 0.04. However, the differences between core-2, core-3 U-bend, and core-3 S-bend are not statistically significant (P>0.6), nor is the difference between the two core-3 conformations. The lack of statistical significance is due to the finite duration of the simulations, which limits the sampling of rare transitions, resulting in underestimation of the MFPTs. To resolve this issue, we apply a correction described in Eq. 1 below.

  Although P(τFPT), (Fig. 1B–D) is broad, the RC →N∗ transitions occur by two-state kinetics. The logarithm of the FPT distributions, ln[τFPT], which could reveal kinetic traps as distinct modes (34), shows that the distributions are predominantly unimodal (Fig. 1E–G). Moreover, the fraction of trajectories which have not transited from RC →N∗ transitions as a function of time, computed as PRC(t)=1−∫0tP(τFPT)dτFPT also exhibit approximately exponential behavior (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Thus, we model the transitions using two-state kinetics, which allows us to approximate P(τFPT), by an exponential distribution, P(τFPT)=λie−λiτFPT, where λi=1/⟨τc.i⟩^ (Fig. 1H). We estimated the true MFPTs, ⟨τc.i⟩^ using Eq. 1. The precisely calculated MFPT values are ⟨τc.1⟩^= 1,554 ± 16 ms, ⟨τc.2⟩^= 685 ± 10 ms, ⟨τc.3U⟩^= 426 ± 7 ms, and ⟨τc.3S⟩^= 404.9 ± 7.0 ms (Fig. 1I). These results unambiguously show that the order of formation of fibril-like structures is, core-3 followed by core-2 and finally core-1, ⟨τc.1⟩^>⟨τc.2⟩^>⟨τc.3⟩^. We should emphasize that MFPT estimates are only proxies for the times needed to form the fibrils, which occur on timescales of days in FUS-LC (20, 21, 23, 35).

  We then assessed whether the N∗ states identified in the isolated monomer are structurally distinct from the rest of the conformational ensemble by comparing the residue-wise propensity pβc.i for β-strand formation in fibril-like conformations-defined by χfib.i≥χc for each core i=1,2,3, to that in the remainder of the ensemble pβRC, which predominantly consists of random-coil-like structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). For each core i, the ratio pβc.i/pβRC exceeds unity primarily within the corresponding core region of FUS-LC. This shows that the N∗ states, although sampled by the isolated monomer, capture key structural features of fibril-like conformations. Additionally, we computed the ensemble-averaged intrachain contact maps corresponding to the fibril-like N∗ states. The contact map for each core shows contacts localized within its corresponding region (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D).

  Having shown that the excited states in the monomer ensemble are fibril-like structures, we calculated the relative stabilities of the three N∗ states (SI Appendix, Methods) sampled in the equilibrium Langevin simulations of FUS-LC. The free energy difference between the core-1 and core-2 is ΔF1,2=−0.93±0.13 kcal/mol, which is reasonably close to the value obtained from solubility measurements (23), −0.35 ± 0.19 kcal/mol. The relative stability of core-2 with respect to the U-bend topology of core-3 is ΔF2,3U=−7.37±0.15 kcal/mol. Among the core-3 variants, the one with the S-bend topology is more stable than the U-bend conformation (ΔF3S,3U=−5.59±0.12 kcal/mol), while both are less stable than core-2 (ΔF2,3S=−1.78±0.11 kcal/mol). The stability hierarchy is reflected in the energy differences ΔF1,3U=−8.29±0.22 kcal/mol and ΔF1,3S=−2.70±0.10 kcal/mol. Strikingly, the rates of formation of the three fibril-like structures are inversely correlated with free energy differences between different cores (Fig. 1I), which is in accord with the Ostwald’s rule of stages, found previously in dipeptide supramolecular polymers (36).


### Sequence-Dependent Modulation of Fibril Formation Kinetics.
  To relate the FUS-LC sequence to the observed order of events, we evaluated the reconfiguration times, τrec (SI Appendix, Methods), of individual cores from BD simulations of the FUS-LC monomer (residues 1-214). The resulting τrec values (Fig. 1J) quantify how rapidly each core segment loses memory of the initial conformation. Our results reveal a strong correlation between τrec and ⟨τFPT⟩, for N∗-state formation (Fig. 1J). These τrec values were obtained for the designated cores within simulations of the full FUS-LC sequence, rather than from isolated fragments. Core-3 has the shortest τrec, implying greater conformational flexibility and more efficient sampling of configuration space compared to core-1. In contrast, the longer τrec of core-1 reflects slower structural reorganization, consistent with its larger ⟨τFPT⟩. The core lengths (Nres=56,38, and 35 for cores 1-3, respectively) partially explain these differences: For instance, for a Rouse chain, τrecRouse∼Nres2 (37). However, the substantial disparity between core-2 and core-3 indicates that sequence-specific interactions play a dominant role. Core-1 and core-2 are enriched in tyrosine and glutamine residues, which can promote stabilizing interactions (Inset in Fig. 1J). Together, these results suggest a mechanistic link summarized as sequence → flexibility → accessibility, where sequence composition modulates local flexibility and thereby influences the kinetic accessibility of aggregation-prone states in FUS-LC.


### Absence of Core-3 Accelerates the Formation of Core-1 and Core-2.
  We repeated the calculations described above for FUS-LC-N (residues 1-163) to illustrate the effect of core-3 on the kinetics of core-1 and core-2 formation. In 200 Brownian trajectories, each lasting 60 ms, there are 16 transitions to core-1 and 100 transitions to core-2. The values of the MFPTs, estimated from the completed transitions, are ⟨τc.1⟩=31.03±4.6ms, ⟨τc.2⟩=27.41±1.7ms (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The unimodal distributions of the FPTs imply P(τFPT)=λie−λiτFPT, allows us to calculate the true MFPTs, ⟨τc.i⟩^, using Eq. 1. We find that ⟨τc.1⟩^=694.97±0.01 ms, ⟨τc.2⟩^=85.14±0.002 ms (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Equilibrium simulations of FUS-LC-N yielded a free energy difference between core-1 and core-2 of ΔF1,2=−2.24±0.06 kcal/mol. As is the case for FUS-LC, the formation rates of fibril-like structures and the free energy differences between the competing core structures in FUS-LC-N are inversely correlated (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Interestingly, core-1 and core-2 form threefold and 11-fold slower in FUS-LC than in FUS-LC-N, which lacks core-3 except for 9 residues (full core-3 consists of 36 residues). This finding is sufficient to conclude that core-3 region of the FUS-LC sequence must play a key role in the assembly of FUS-LC (however, see below). These findings further show that the N∗ states of core-3 are mutually exclusive with those of core-1 and core-2.


### Core-1 and Core-3 N∗ States Do Not Coexist.
  From the analysis of the marginal probabilities P(core1) and P(core3) obtained from the unconstrained simulations, we find no evidence for the simultaneous formation of core-1 and core-3 N∗ fibril-like states. The joint probability vanishes, P(core1,core3)=0, indicating that across our extensive dataset (48 s of cumulative Brownian dynamics time and 8×1011 integration steps in Langevin dynamics), the two motifs never coexist spontaneously. To elucidate the structural or physical basis for this mutual exclusivity, we performed constrained Langevin dynamics simulations of the FUS-LC monomer to compute the conditional probabilities of reaching one N∗ state given that the other is formed. In the first set of simulations, harmonic constraints were applied to enforce the key contacts stabilizing core-1, and in the second, those stabilizing core-3, based on the corresponding fibril structures. Under constrained conditions, we evaluated the conditional probabilities P(core1|core3) and P(core3|core1). If the two events were independent, one would expect P(core1|core3)=P(core1) and P(core3|core1)=P(core3). However, we observe strong suppression of one core when the other is enforced: P(core1|core3)=P(core1)/82 and P(core3|core1)=P(core3)/3,930. Thus, the two N∗ states are mutually suppressive, implying that the being in the core-1 N∗ state reduces the likelihood of forming core-3, and vice versa.


### Droplet Formation and Ostwald Ripening.
  We initiated five multichain simulations from distinct initial conformations in a homogeneous solution containing 200 randomly distributed, noninteracting FUS-LC chains (refer to Methods; see also a Top panel in Fig. 2A; also SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The box size corresponded to an overall protein concentration of 600 μM, which is larger than the typical range [100 to 250 μM (38, 39)] used in in vitro experiments but is sufficiently low to observe both the condensed and dilute phases in equilibrium (Fig. 2A). The unbiased simulations show that oligomers of different sizes form spontaneously (Movie S1). We divided the oligomers into three groups: small oligomers (2-9 proteins), large oligomers (10-31 proteins), small droplets (32-70 proteins), and large droplets (>70 proteins). At the start of the simulations (t<0.1×109τ, where τ is a simulation time step defined in Methods), the FUS-LC monomers quickly coalesce into small oligomers, which rapidly disintegrate into monomers and reassemble repeatedly. At intermediate times, some of the small oligomers act as nucleation sites, leading to the formation of large oligomers. The initial clustering is in accord with the expectations of the classical nucleation theory (CNT) for the formation of the subcritical nuclei (40). Eventually, one of the oligomers serves as a nucleation site, leading to the spontaneous formation of a small droplet at t>0.5×109τ. The transition from a subcritical to a supercritical nucleus is a hallmark of CNT, signifying the instant when the nucleus exceeds the critical radius and initiates the stable growth of a large FUS droplet (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The onset time for stable droplet growth varies significantly across different simulation runs (Fig. 2), suggesting that the droplet assembles by multiple pathways, which is also consistent with CNT predictions.

  Typically, monomers disintegrate from the oligomers or smaller droplets, thus exposing a higher fraction of sites to the solvent (modeled implicitly here). These are subsequently reincorporated into larger droplets, which is reminiscent of Ostwald ripening (41, 42), a phenomenon in which less stable smaller droplets or oligomers disintegrate into monomers (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A and Movie S2) due to their higher surface energy and chemical potential (Fig. 3C). Such monomers subsequently diffuse and are reincorporated into larger droplets or oligomers, which are free energetically more favorable because of their lower surface-to-volume ratio and reduced interfacial energy. In our simulations, the higher fraction of solvent-exposed sites in smaller droplets (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) accelerates their dissolution, effectively supplying material for the growth of larger droplets, as anticipated by Ostwald ripening (42, 43). This process results in the coarsening of the system over time, with larger droplets becoming more prominent while smaller ones diminish. The observed dynamics highlight the interplay between thermodynamic stability and molecular exchange, which governs the temporal evolution of the phase-separated FUS-LC droplets.


### Monomer Conformations in the Dilute and Dense Phase Are Similar but Not Identical.
  We compared the Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) profiles (I(q) with q being the scattering wave vector) calculated for FUS-LC chains within the droplet to I(q) in the dispersed phase (Fig. 3A). The profiles were virtually indistinguishable from each other, which is consistent with several experimental studies on FUS-LC of varying sequence length. 1) In a recent work (44), NMR signals for FUS NTD (residues 1-267) were indistinguishable between the dilute and condensed phases. Similar conclusions were reached using solid-state NMR studies of FUS-LC (45). 2) Solution-state NMR studies of FUS-LC-N (residues 1-163) (46) showed no significant conformational changes upon phase separation on the timescale of the experiment. 3) Double electron–electron resonance (DEER) spectroscopy studies of FUS NTD, consisting of residues (1-267) (47) also showed that the conformational similarity of FUS in dilute and condensed phases. The measured distance distributions between selected pairs of residues within FUS NTD monomers inside and outside the condensed phase were similar (47). We calculated these distributions between the same set of residues in FUS-LC and found that they are similar both the phases (Fig. 4). The averages and SDs of these distances show reasonable agreement with those reported for FUS NTD (SI Appendix, Table S4 and Fig. 4). 4) The inferred distances between the N-terminal residue and three selected residues in FUS-LC-N monomers both inside and outside the droplet using experiments smFRET (48) showed only a modest increase in the condensed phase. We computed the same distances for FUS-LC and similarly observed a minor increase for monomers inside droplets (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). This increase in distance between the N-terminal and other residues suggests a modest degree of monomer unwinding within the condensed phase, which is also reflected in the distributions of the radius of gyration (Rg) and end-to-end distance (Ree) for FUS-LC proteins inside and outside the droplet (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).


### Inherent Structures (IS).
  Although the conformations of monomeric FUS-LCs remain largely similar in homogeneous and phase-separated environments, subtle structural differences emerge in the latter. To further investigate this, we calculated the residue-dependent β-strand propensity of FUS-LC monomers in both the environments (Fig. 3D). The overall β-strand propensity remains low, suggesting that monomeric FUS-LCs inside droplets are also disordered. However, the monomers within droplets have nearly threefold higher propensity for β-strand formation compared to free monomers (Fig. 3D). To ascertain that the strand content is enhanced in the droplet, we determined its inherent structure (IS) (49) using a method that was introduced to probe packing in dense liquids. The IS, which locates local minima in the energy landscape, was determined by quenching the conformation of the droplet to a low temperature. We find that the β-strand (Fig. 3E) content is typically greater in the cores than elsewhere. In particular, the greater β-strand content in core-3 shows that there is a higher tendency for structural ordering in this region even before core-1 forms, which is consistent with experimental observations (21) and Ostwald’s rule of stages (Fig. 1). Because fibrils form only on times scales on the order of days (20, 21, 35) it implies that the droplets in our simulations and experiments (46) are metastable.


### Chemical Potentials in the Two Phases Are Similar.
  For t>3.5×109τ, a stable droplet forms from which only the monomers disassociate on long time scales (Fig. 2). However, polymer chains exhibit continuous dynamic exchange, moving in and out of the droplet (Fig. 2B; also Movie S3), as required for phase equilibrium. To ensure that we characterize the equilibrium state of the condensate, we discarded the first 3.5×109τ time steps in the trajectory. To assess that equilibrium is established, we calculated the chemical potentials in the condensed (μc) and dispersed (μd) phases using the approach described in SI Appendix. We assumed that the polymers that are not in the condensed phase constitute the dispersed phase. In other words, the variations in the oligomer sizes found in the dispersed phase are not treated as separate species. The relative difference, Δμ=(⟨μc⟩−⟨μd⟩)/⟨μc⟩, is 0.003 ± 0.03. The large error bars that exceed the mean value are due to the finite system size (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

  The similarity between the chemical potential distributions of the two phases, Pd(μ) and Pc(μ) (Fig. 3B), was assessed using the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) (SI Appendix, Methods). The calculated divergence, DJS(Pd(μ)‖Pc(μ))=0.01. The small value of the JSD shows that the two distributions are similar. Because their mean values are comparable, we surmised that the observed differences arise from the larger variance in μ for the condensed phase (Fig. 3B). Given the limited system size accessible to reliable simulations (up to 200 chains), concluding that the chemical potentials of the coexisting phases are similar is reasonable.


### Concentrations of the Coexisting Phases.
  The establishment of the equality of the chemical potentials in the two phases allows us to calculate the coexisting protein concentrations (SI Appendix, Methods). The average FUS-LC concentration of the condensed phase is ρc=(16.24±8×10−4) mM (Figs. 2D and 3H; also SI Appendix, Fig. S12B), which is in excellent agreement with a few experiments. Raman imaging experiments were used to estimate the dense phase concentration to be 15.0 mM under standard conditions (pH 7.6 and 150 mM NaCl) (38). An independent study using quantitative phase imaging reported that the FUS-LC concentration in the condensed phase to be 337.3 mg/mL (15.7 mM, assuming a molecular weight of 21.5 kDa for FUS-LC) (50), which is comparable to the 20 mM concentration reported for FUS NTD (residues 1-267) (47). The average FUS-LC concentration in the dispersed phase in the simulations is ρd=(0.28±2×10−4) mM (Fig. 3H), which is in reasonable agreement with the experimentally reported concentration of ≈100 μM (47, 51). In our simulations, we observed that on average there are 114.80±0.05 FUS-LC chains in the condensed phase, while 85.2±0.05 are in the dilute phase (Figs. 2C and 3G), which agrees with experimental estimates (47). The elevated overall protein concentration used in the simulations likely results in the larger value ρd of the low-density phase. The density of the droplet calculated using simulations is about ≈58 higher than in the dispersed phase. The corresponding experimental ratio is ≈157. The good agreement between the simulations and several experiments further validates our model.


### Mechanism of Droplet Formation.
  Given that the chains in the droplet are disordered, it is natural to wonder if specific interactions between the FUS-LC polymers initiate phase separation. To address this issue, we analyzed interchain interactions. The results, which were qualitatively consistent across all the simulation runs, are illustrated in SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9. At short times, interchain interactions are rare because most proteins exist as free monomers or small oligomers. However, the majority of interchain interactions that form during the early times are mediated by the core-3 region, with frequent interactions between residues 164-176 of one protein and the corresponding residues of another. Subsequently, other nonpreferential interactions, which are intermittent and disrupted over time, are observed. The contacts between residues 164-176 consistently form the most populated interchain interactions. Interactions mediated by core-1 are discernible only at later stages of droplet formation, with preferential contacts involving core-1 residues 61-77. Interestingly, amino acid residues in core-2 (residues 112-150) do not engage in preferential interactions at either the beginning of the simulation (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9) or not at later stages (Fig. 3I), except when mediated by core-1 or core-3. At equilibrium, interactions between core-2 regions of different chains are the least probable (Fig. 3I), a finding that supports the observation that core-2 is not required to bind to hydrogels (21).

  We performed additional five independent Brownian dynamics simulations for a system of 64 FUS proteins with viscosity set to 10−3 Pa·s (Methods). In the first 150 μs of each simulation, transient oligomers containing 4-8 chains form and dissociate (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), in accord with the results from low-friction simulations (Fig. 2). Analysis of the interchain interactions revealed that residues 164-176 primarily mediate contacts between the chains in the oligomers (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). From the temporal progression of the contact maps (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C), we find that condensate formation is initiated from oligomers that act as nucleation sites, predominantly involving core-3. This suggests that core-3 plays a central role in mediating droplet formation (see below). This is further supported by the interaction patterns observed in dimers and small oligomers (fewer than 10 chains; SI Appendix, Fig. S9D): Although the core-1 region also participates in interchain interactions, their relative contribution decreases in larger oligomers, where core-3-mediated interactions become dominant.


### Core 3 Residues Drive FUS-LC Condensation.
  It is surprising that the most frequent and the strongest interchain interactions are mediated by the C-terminal tail of FUC-LC, particularly residues 164SSGGGGGGGGGGN176 (Fig. 3 F and I). Strikingly, these residues have the highest propensity to form β-strands at the monomer level (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). The relevance of core-3 in FUS-LC assembly was demonstrated (21) by systematically truncating residues from C terminus, and probing their ability to bind to hydrogel. Truncation beyond residue 180 resulted in a complete loss of the ability of GFP-tagged test proteins to bind to mCherry hydrogels formed by the intact FUS-LC (figure 2A in ref. 21), thus suggesting that residues that are critical for FUS-LC condensate formation are within the 165-180 range. This finding is supported by our simulations which show that the most probable interactions are mediated by residues 164-176. In addition, our simulations show that residues 71TPQGYGSTGGYGSS84 (Fig. 3 F and I) also form several interchain interactions within the droplet. Kato and McKnight also systematically created FUS-LC variants by truncating chunks of residues from the N terminus (figure 2B in ref. 21). The results of these experiments show that the crucial residues for FUS-LC hydrogel binding activity are within residues 61-77, which also aligns with our simulation results. The early formation of structure in residues (164-176), containing mostly Glycines, the absence of discernible order in core-2, and emergence of order late in core-1 show that core-3 that initiates the assembly of FUS-LC.


### Structural Model for Core-3.
  In light of the importance of core-3 in FUS-LC assembly, we used AlphaFold2 [AF2; (31)] and AlphaFold3 [AF3; (32)] to predict the unknown structure of core-3. To assess the reliability of AF2 and AF3, we first determined the structures of core-1 and core-2 and compared them to the experimentally resolved counterparts (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S15 for AF3). AlphaFold3 generates structurally diverse models of the fibrils based on different random seeds (52). Although the predicted and experimentally derived structures deviate to some extent, the majority of residues that form β-sheet secondary structures in the experimental fibrils are also found in β-sheets in the AF3-predicted models. Moreover, the predictions are sufficiently accurate to validate Ostwald’s rule of stages. This conclusion is based on a comparison of mean first-passage times (MFPTs) computed using both AF3-predicted and experimental fibril structures (see SI Appendix, Results).

  To identify a reliable fibril model for our simulations, we evaluated all conformations produced by AF3 for FUS141−214, which forms core-3 fibrils experimentally (21), using the quality metrics reported by AlphaFold (32). Among these, the four-subunit assembly (Fig. 5C) consistently showed the highest reliability. We therefore adopted this conformation for all subsequent analyses (SI Appendix contains details).

  AF3 predictions show that the two outer extended segments of core-3 correspond to residues 155-163 and 182-190, while the inner segment consists of residues 168-177 (Fig. 5C). Within this architecture, residues 156-163 and 172-190 form β-strands in a serpentine-like β-sheet arrangement, whereas residues 164-166 adopt a β-turn conformation that facilitates chain reversal. Residues 155 and 167-172 remain in a random coil conformation. SI Appendix, Fig. S16 A and B display per-atom pLDDT scores for the FUS155−190 and FUS141−214 fibrils, respectively. The monomeric unit in the FUS155−190 fibril adopts a U-bend topology with a “kinked” region spanning residues 176-184 (SI Appendix, Fig. S15D). In contrast, the core-3 region (residues 155-190) of the FUS141−214 monomer adopts an S-bend topology (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, the “kinked” region (residues 176-184) is conserved across both models.

  Given that core-3 forms cross-β polymers (21), we used the predicted structures to determine the residue-dependent secondary structural propensity (53). Both the U-bend and S-bend topologies exhibited a β-sheet secondary structure throughout most residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 C and D). Additionally, residues 164-176, which showed strong interchain interactions in our simulations (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), also displayed prominent intra- and interchain contacts in the predicted core-3 fibrils (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 E and F). This result further highlights the importance of core-3 in mediating interactions critical for fibril assembly.


## Discussion
  Using an in-depth study of phase separation in the low-complexity domain of FUS and its variants, we have resolved several long-standing questions regarding its assembly. Although the weak interactions between polymer chains might suggest an absence of preferred interchain contacts within droplets, our simulations reveal that interactions involving residues 164-176 (the center of core-3) become structured early and initiate droplet formation. This implies that ordering in core-3 emerges first and subsequently propagates toward core-1. The sparse interchain contacts associated with core-2 (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9) further supports experimental evidence that core-2 formation is not required for hydrogel binding (21). In addition to these results, several other findings with broader implications are discussed below.

  An unusual feature of FUS-LC is that the identities and structures of the ordered cores and the resulting fibril morphology depend on the sequence length. Full-length FUS-LC forms a nonpolymorphic fibril with only core-1 (residues 39-95) ordered, whereas a truncated construct (residues 110-214) reveal a fibril stabilized by core-2 (residues 112-150). FUS-LC harbors core-3 (residues 155-190), which was suspected to initiate fibrillization (21). Our coarse-grained simulations reveal a hierarchy of the fibril-forming regions: Ordering begins in core-3 and then propagates to core-1, despite core-1 being thermodynamically more stable. This ordering sequence aligns with Ostwald’s Rule of Stages (ORS), in which metastable structures form prior to the most stable state. In contrast, the subsequent growth and coarsening of droplets follow Ostwald ripening (OR), where larger condensates grow at the expense of smaller ones. We also find that early β-strand formation in core-3 provides a nucleation site that links condensate formation to the onset of fibril assembly, with interchain contacts in this region driving the earliest oligomerization events. These initial steps are reminiscent of Classical Nucleation Theory (CNT), which envisions multiple transient intermediates (here, oligomers of different sizes and compositions) forming in parallel before the system selects the dominant pathway. ORS then describes how these intermediates evolve through a sequence of metastable states toward the most stable fibril structure.


### Nucleation and Ostwald Ripening.
  Droplet formation and growth occur in two stages. First, monomers collide to form transient oligomers that repeatedly assemble and dissociate until one reaches a critical size (Movie S1). Once formed, this nucleus grows into a stable droplet. In the second stage, excess monomers and small oligomers are “devoured” by the largest droplet (42), driving coarsening and producing a pronounced “winner-take-all” behavior as smaller droplets shrink (54). These simulations clearly depict the coupled roles of thermodynamic stability and molecular exchange in shaping the evolving droplet landscape.

  Our results provide a structural view of nucleation and growth, linking the early formation of a critical oligomer, consistent with Classical Nucleation Theory, with the long-time coarsening dynamics governed by Ostwald ripening. Because monomers and oligomers explore a heterogeneous ensemble of conformations, only a subset appears competent for nucleation, consistent with prior theoretical and experimental studies (55, 56). Despite this complexity, the size dependence of droplet stability observed in our simulations aligns with both frameworks: CNT identifies the critical nucleus needed for sustained growth, while Ostwald ripening explains the preferential enlargement of larger droplets at the expense of smaller ones. Together, these mechanisms govern the temporal evolution of the phase-separated FUS-LC condensates. Although one would expect that the growth of droplets should scale with time as ∼t1/3 according to the Lifshitz-Slyozov theory, our results reveal (not shown) a clear deviation from this classical scaling, indicating that additional physical mechanisms influence the coarsening dynamics in our system. The deviations from that theory have been noted (57, 58).


### Core-3 Structure Prediction.
  To determine the kinetics of core-1 and core-2 formation from the monomer conformational spectrum, we used the available experimental fibril structures as references (20, 23). For core-3, the reference structures were taken from AF3 predictions, which produce both U-bend and S-bend topologies with the same residues forming the kink. Using the structural overlap parameter, we computed the corresponding mean first-passage times and found that the kinetics for the two topologies are nearly identical. This demonstrates that our conclusions regarding Ostwald’s rule of stages are robust to variations among the AF3-predicted conformations (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Surprisingly, the S-bend structure predicted by AF3 for FUS141−214 shows reasonable agreement with an unpublished Cryo-EM structure of FUS138−201 (M. Kato and S. McKnight, private communication). We were pleasantly surprised by this result, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that AF3 is broadly reliable for predicting amyloid architectures. The performance of AlphaFold for intrinsically disordered regions and fold-switching proteins has been questioned (52, 59–61). Nevertheless, for FUS-LC, the AF3-derived core-3 models yield kinetic trends that are in excellent agreement with experimental observations (21), suggesting that these predicted structures are reasonable working models. We also performed all-atom multichain simulations of FUS141−214 (up to five chains, Amber14/TIP3P (62), 38 ns; results not shown). No cross-β fibrils formed, and the residues remained largely disordered, inconsistent with the expected β-rich fibrillar morphology. Therefore, these structures were not used as references for MFPT calculations. Although enhanced sampling might access more ordered states, this has not been demonstrated for condensate-forming IDPs, and fibril formation occurs experimentally on timescales of days-well beyond all-atom simulations.


### Ostwald’s Rule of Stages.
  The presence of excited N∗ fibril-like structures within the monomer ensemble (24) provides insight into how fibrillar assemblies emerge in IDPs and other aggregation-prone proteins (22, 30, 63). The inverse correlation between the fibril-core stability and the kinetics of their formation-derived from the properties of the N states-is consistent with the Ostwald’s rule of stages, previously demonstrated for dipeptide supramolecular polymers (64) and Aβ peptides (65). Our simulations suggest that experimental identification of these N∗ states, for example by relaxation-dispersion NMR (63, 66) or time-resolved solid-state NMR (67), would be valuable for elucidating the mechanisms of condensate formation and their subsequent maturation into fibrils. Although FUS-LC fibrils assemble only on timescales of days (20, 21, 35), we expect that Ostwald’s rule will continue to govern the transition from droplets to solid-like aggregates.

  Although the N∗ conformations are sampled only by isolated monomers, they exhibit an increased β-strand propensity in the corresponding fibril-forming regions of FUS-LC (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This supports the relevance of N∗ states for characterizing fibril formation, as these monomer-accessible conformations mirror key structural features of the fibrils. We surmise that such low-population N∗ states are embedded in the monomer’s free-energy landscape and act as precursors to aggregation-prone structures (30).


### Role of Core-3.
  The mutual exclusion in the formation of core-1 and core-3 structures was established using ALS-associated mutations (21). Our simulations provide indirect evidence for this key finding. It is based on a comparison of the MFPTs for fibril-like conformations in FUS-LC and FUS-LC-N (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The absence of the core-3 facilitates the formation of core-1 and core-2, implying that core-3 either energetically or through steric interactions hinders these transitions. The increased β-strand propensity in the core-1 region of FUS-LC-N compared to FUS-LC further shows that the presence of core-3 suppresses the cores with greater stability (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Importantly, the relative stability between core-1 and core-2, ΔF1,2, increases from −0.93±0.13 kcal/mol in FUS-LC to −2.24±0.06 kcal/mol in FUS-LC-N. The enhanced stability suggests that core-3 exerts a greater destabilizing effect on core-1 than on core-2, further supporting its role in modulating fibril formation pathways.


### Implication for FUS-LC Assembly.
  Our results confirm that the thermodynamically more stable core-1 compared to core-3 (ΔF1,3S=−2.70±0.10 kcal/mol) forms significantly later than core-3 in FUS-LC (Fig. 1I). Kinetic studies revealed that core-3 forms within 50 h of incubation (figure in ref. 21). In contrast, core-1 forms only after an incubation period of 5 to 7 d (20). The combination of kinetic and thermodynamic findings suggests that core-3 must transition into core-1 over time. Although core-1 and core-3 do not coexist in FUS-LC, our kinetic studies of FUS1-163 shows that the absence of the core-3 region facilitates the formation of core-1. The time to form fibril-like core-1 structure decreases by a factor of ≈4 in FUS-LC-N compared to FUS-LC (compare Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S1J). This implies that, at the early stages of FUS-LC assembly, core-3 likely hinders core-1 formation. As previously discussed, of core-1 and core-3 was previously shown in experiments (21) using ALS-associated mutations.

  The relatively large number of interchain contacts mediated by core-1 residues in dimers indicates that dimerization could occur through cooperative, overlapping interactions involving both core-1 and core-3. However, this does not imply that fibril formation originates from core-1 at the dimer level because two chains are too small to exceed the critical nucleus size. Prior studies of Aβ peptides (68, 69) and α-synuclein (70) have estimated the size of the critical to be ≥6 monomers. Moreover, the relative contribution of core-1-mediated contacts diminishes in assemblies that are larger than dimers (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D), while β-strand propensity emerges predominantly in core-3 (Fig. 3). This suggests that fibril formation is initiated by core-3, consistent with the experimental observations (21).

  Our simulations predict that in FUS-LC core-2 fibril-like structures form after core-3 but before core-1. Experiments demonstrated that core-2 is not required for hydrogel binding (21), which accords well with our simulations that show that there are a limited number of interchain contacts between the core-2 regions in FUS-LC monomers within the condensates (Fig. 3). The scarcity of favorable interactions likely impedes the formation of stable core-2 fibrils. The destabilization of core-2 could also arise from entropic repulsion between the “fuzzy coats” surrounding the ordered regions of the nonpolymorphic FUS-LS fibrils (20, 23).

  Finally, there are similarities between FUS-LS and the low-complexity domain of the TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43). In the latter, core-1 (“aggregation” core) and core-2 (about 10 residues outside of core-1) transition from liquid to solid morphology (71). The ordered cores do not typically coexist (as in FUS-LC) and form on different time scales (72). It appears that Ostwald’s rule of stages should be manifested in TDP-43 as well. The generality of the results found here (Ostwald’s rule, nucleation and growth of condensates, maturation of droplets into fibrils extremely long time scales) for other low-complexity sequences remains to be established.


## Methods

### SOP-IDP Model.
  FUS protein simulations used the transferable SOP-IDP model (26, 27) whose energy function is given by, USOP−IDP=UFENE+UELE+UEXV+UBB+UBS+USC. Here, UFENE models chain connectivity, UELE electrostatics, and UEXV excluded volume. The LJ terms UBB, UBS, and USS describe backbone–backbone, backbone–side-chain, and side-chain–side-chain interactions. Importantly, USS incorporates chemically specific, residue-dependent interactions with strengths derived from knowledge-based statistical potentials (73), and side-chain sizes chosen from van der Waals radii to match the molar volumes of the respective side chains (SI Appendix, Table S1), thereby capturing chemically specific effects. Further details can be found elsewhere (27) and SI Appendix, Methods. The SOP-IDP force field was used in monomer simulations and multichain simulations without alterations.


### Transition to Fibril-Like Monomer N∗ States.
  Previously, we showed that the monomer ensemble contains sparsely populated, high-free energy conformations resembling those found in the fibrils (24, 65). These N∗ conformations can be identified from long equilibrium simulations using the structural overlap parameter (SI Appendix, Eq. S3). To compute the transition rates into the N∗ states, we performed Nsim=200 independent Brownian dynamics trajectories in the high-friction regime, using a solvent viscosity of 10−3 Pa·s, comparable to that of water (SI Appendix for details). Each trajectory was 240 ms in duration, which is often insufficient to observe rare transitions into the N∗ ensemble. We therefore applied the correction procedure described below to obtain accurate estimates of the transition times.


### True Mean FPT.
  Because the duration of the simulation trajectories is finite the transition from the disordered ground state to the N∗ state cannot be observed in all the trajectories. As a result, the mean first passage time is underestimated. To correct for the underestimate, we used the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) to calculate the true mean FPT, by assuming that the first passage times FPTs are exponentially distributed. The true mean first passage time, denoted as ⟨τ⟩, is given by,[1]⟨τ⟩=∑k=1NOτk+NE·TNO.

  The equation above (Eq. 1), derived elsewhere (74) is similar to the approach in ref. 75, accounts for the contributions from events that occur within the duration (T) of the simulation trajectory as well those that do not on T. In Eq. 1, τk is the FPT that is less than T, NE denotes the number of simulations where the FPT exceeds T (indicating cases where the first passage did not occur within T), NO is the total number of observed FPTs that are less than T, and NE+NO = Nsim. The robustness of Eq. 1 was tested across different values of NS and T. The validity of Eq. 1 was established by explicit comparisons to precisely solvable models (74).
