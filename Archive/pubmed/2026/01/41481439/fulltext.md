# Sticky interactions govern sequence-dependent dynamics in biomolecular condensates


## Significance
  Cells use biomolecular condensates enriched with intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) to spatiotemporally organize and regulate diverse biochemical processes. The material properties of these condensates, such as their viscosity and internal motion, are critical to their function, yet the molecular origins of these properties are not well understood. In this work, we uncover how the amino acid sequence of IDPs governs their dynamics across multiple length scales, ultimately determining the physical properties of the condensates they form. By linking molecular interactions to macroscopic material properties through polymer physics, particularly a sticky Rouse-like framework, we establish a predictive framework for sequence–dynamics–rheology relationships. This integrative approach, combining polymer theory and molecular simulations, paves the way for rational design of synthetic condensates with programmable properties.


## Results
  The IDP condensates can be described through a multiscale framework that connects sequence features, microscopic dynamics, and macroscopic material properties (Fig. 1). We investigated how two key sequence parameters, chain length N and degree of charge segregation influence the dynamics of individual chains across multiple length scales, which in turn influence the macroscopic material properties of the dense phase. To enable comparison across different IDP lengths and charge patterning, we normalized the previously developed sequence charge decoration SCD parameter (34, 35) (nSCD) to range between 0 and 1 (with the lower and upper bounds corresponding to perfectly alternating and diblock sequences, respectively) and performed all simulations at a fixed temperature of 300 K. By combining analyses at the monomer, chain, and condensate level, we sought to build a predictive understanding of how molecular-level features shape the macroscopic dynamics and rheology of IDP condensates.


### IDPs Adopt Near-Ideal-Chain Conformations in the Dense Phase Regardless of Charge Patterning.
  The conformations of IDP chains play an important role in determining condensate dynamics and function (36–38). To characterize chain dimensions in the dense phase, we analyzed the end-to-end distance Re and the radius of gyration Rg. The end-to-end distance was computed as Re=Re2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The radius of gyration was computed as Rg=Rg2=tr G (Fig. 2, Inset), where G is the gyration tensor defined as G=1M∑i=1NmiΔriΔri. Here mi is the mass of the i-th residue, Δri is the displacement vector from the chain’s center of mass, and M is the total mass of the chain.

  Re and Rg are closely correlated in the dense phase (Fig. 2). The best fit across all chains is Re=6.18 Rg, slightly above the theoretical value of Re=6 Rg expected for an ideal Gaussian chain (31, 32), but far below the value of Re=6.45 Rg expected for an excluded volume chain (39). This near-ideal but modest deviation suggests that chains adopt approximately Gaussian conformations in the dense phase but may exhibit slight expansion beyond ideal behavior. We further found that Rg showed minimal dependence on nSCD (Fig. 2, Inset), consistent with previous findings at fixed chain length (36). This insensitivity to sequence charge segregation contrasts with the dilute phase, where increasing nSCD promotes chain compaction due to stronger intrachain electrostatic interactions (25, 36, 40–42). Additionally, Rg increased with chain length according to the scaling law Rg ~ Nν, with ν ranging between 0.529 and 0.572 across all nSCD values. These values lie between the θ-solvent limit (ν = 0.5) and the good-solvent regime (ν ≈ 0.588) (31, 32), suggesting that chains behave close to ideal but not perfectly so, consistent with weak excluded-volume effects. However, given that our N range spans less than two decades and includes short chains close to the Kuhn length, these numerical power-law exponents should be interpreted cautiously.

  To further validate this result without relying solely on N dependence, we used two complementary approaches. First, we computed the intrachain distance Rij=Rij2 as a function of sequence separation |i-j| (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and extracted local scaling exponents after excluding short separations below the Kuhn length, estimated from Gaussian fits to the end-to-end distance distribution P(Re). The resulting exponents were close to 0.5 for all nSCD values and chain lengths, with slightly higher values for the shortest chains (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Second, we fitted P(Re) to the empirical Mazur form (43), P(Re)=CRe2exp(−αRet), where t = 2 corresponds to the Gaussian limit and t ≈ 3.2 to the excluded-volume limit (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The parameters C and α are functions of t. Most sequences exhibited t-values near 2, again consistent with Gaussian statistics (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Deviations from the ideal value were primarily observed for shorter chains, where finite-size effects are expected to be most pronounced.

  Together, these analyses indicate that E-K sequences adopt near-ideal conformations in the dense phase, consistent with previous findings for biomolecular condensates (36, 44–46). The small deviations from ideal behavior observed here likely arise from finite-size effects and the heterogeneous nature of polyampholyte sequences. Overall, the results support a picture in which dense-phase IDPs behave as nearly Gaussian chains, with conformations governed primarily by chain length rather than charge patterning.


### Chain Length and Charge Patterning Modulate the Dynamics and Rheology of IDP Condensates.
  Having established how sequence features influence chain conformations in the dense phase, we next examined their impact on material properties. We focused on translational diffusivity D, a key measure of molecular mobility within the dense phase. To quantify chain motion, we computed the mean square displacement (MSD) of residues across dense phases formed by E-K sequences with varying N and nSCD (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). MSD was defined as MSDt=rit-ri02, where rit is the position vector of residue i at time t. Initially, we found that all sequences exhibited ballistic motion, with MSD scaling as t2. As interchain interactions became more pronounced, MSD transitioned to a subdiffusive regime (47) with MSD ~ t0.5. At longer times, the motion became diffusive with MSD growing linearly (MSD ∝t1). These transitions occurred more slowly in sequences with longer N and higher nSCD, indicating that these sequence features modulate chain dynamics. Comparable three-regime MSD behavior was also observed in Martini simulations of N = 50 E-K sequences that include explicit solvent, ions, and angle/dihedral potentials, confirming that these dynamical features are not model-specific (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Polymer theories predict a decrease in diffusivity D with increasing chain length N. In the Rouse model for unentangled polymers, D∼N-1. In contrast, entangled polymer systems exhibit D∼N-2.3 in experiments, approximately consistent with the theoretical expectation for reptation dynamics (D∼N-2) (31, 32). To test this scaling behavior in the E-K condensates, we extracted D values from the diffusive regime using the relation MSD =6Dt (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). For a given N, increasing nSCD monotonically reduced D, due to stronger interchain electrostatic interactions arising from charge segregation (25, 42). Across different nSCD values, D decreased with increasing N, following a power law with exponents ranging from −1.70 to −2.24 (Fig. 3A). These exponents suggest that the system is mostly in a crossover regime between Rouse and reptation behavior. Additionally, only at high nSCD and longer N, normalized MSD values (MSD/t0.5; SI Appendix, Fig. S9) showed a gradual decrease in slope values from 0 (Rouse-like) toward −0.25 (reptation-like) in the intermediate regime, before reaching diffusive motion with a slope of 0.5 (48). This indicated that systems are close to the onset of reptation-like behavior only at higher nSCD and longer N.

  We next computed the viscosity η of dense phases formed by E-K sequences with varying N and nSCD, using the Green–Kubo relation (Methods). Polymer theories predict η∼N1 in the Rouse model (31, 32), while experimental results in the reptation regime show η∼N3.4. In our simulations, η increased with N following a power law, with exponents between 1.91 and 2.14 across different nSCD values (Fig. 3B). These values are steeper than predicted by the Rouse model but remain well below those associated with fully reptating polymers. These results suggest that our simulated condensates lie in a crossover regime, where increasing chain length and interchain interactions begin to influence rheology without yet producing fully entangled dynamics. Although viscosity estimates at longer N show larger uncertainties due to statistical noise inherent in the Green–Kubo method, which has been widely used to characterize condensate viscosities (49–51), we believe that the scaling trends remain robust. For a given N, increasing nSCD also led to higher η values, reflecting enhanced interchain electrostatic interactions (Fig. 3B). Moreover, the data collapsed onto a single master curve following D∼η-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), confirming the validity of the Stokes–Einstein relation in the dense phase. Together, these results highlight the critical role of sequence features such as chain length and charge patterning in shaping the material properties of the dense phase. The scaling exponents further support that the condensates are in a crossover regime between Rouse-like and reptation-dominated dynamics.


### Normal Mode Analysis Reveals Multiscale Relaxation in IDP Dense Phases.
  To investigate the molecular origins of condensate material properties, we performed a normal mode analysis. This approach characterizes relaxation dynamics across different segmental lengths, from monomers to entire chains, by projecting the dynamics onto the Rouse modes (40, 52–55). We computed the autocorrelation function cp of the normal modes Xp within the dense phase (Methods), where higher mode number p corresponds to smaller chain segments (Fig. 4A). We found that cp for different N and nSCD decayed to zero (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), indicating complete relaxation. We extracted relaxation times τp by fitting cp to a stretched exponential form (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). As expected, low-p modes, which represent chain-level dynamics, relaxed more slowly, whereas high-p modes corresponding to smaller segments relaxed more rapidly.

  Segments at the monomer scale (p≈N-1) relaxed quickly and showed no significant variation with N (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Segments spanning up to approximately three monomer units exhibited minimal changes in relaxation across different nSCD values (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S13). For longer segments, relaxation times τp increased with both N and nSCD, mirroring the slowdown observed in diffusivity and viscosity. When plotted against normalized mode number N/p, segmental relaxation times for different N collapsed onto a single curve at each nSCD value. The data followed a power-law scaling τp∼N/pν, with ν ranging from approximately 2.2 to 2.9 (Fig. 4C). Again, these exponents indicate that segmental dynamics fall within the crossover regime between Rouse (2) and reptation (3.4) scaling (31, 32).

  Overall, the normal mode analysis revealed that increasing N and nSCD slowed the relaxation of several intermediate-sized segments, mirroring the dynamic slowdown observed in the condensate material properties. Because our simulation results place the E-K condensates in the crossover region between Rouse and reptation regimes, we next examined whether polymer dynamics theories, starting with the Rouse model, could quantitatively describe the observed trends.


### Rouse Theory Fails to Capture Medium Friction in IDP Dense Phases.
  The Rouse model describes a polymer chain as a series of N beads connected by harmonic springs with rms length b. Each bead undergoes Brownian motion and experiences a friction coefficient ζ (Fig. 5A) (24, 31, 32, 56). No other interactions between the beads are included. Chain reconfiguration dynamics can be characterized by the relaxation time τR of the end-to-end vector Re, which for a Rouse chain is given by[1]τR=ζN2b23π2kBT=ζNRe23π2kBT,

  where kB is the Boltzmann constant and T is the temperature. The expression for τR uses the ideal chain relation Re2=Nb2, which is supported by our earlier findings that all sequences in the dense phase follow near-ideal chain statistics (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S5). The Rouse model predicts translational diffusivity D as[2]D=kBTNζ.

  The Rouse model also yields the bulk viscosity η of an unentangled polymer solution as[3]η=ηs+cζNb236=ηs+cζRe236,

  where ηs is the solvent viscosity and c is the concentration of chain segments (monomers per unit volume) in the dense phase.

  We first asked whether the Rouse model could predict chain reconfiguration dynamics in condensates using Eq. 1. To test this, we computed end-to-end vector relaxation times τRe in E-K condensates from the autocorrelation function ce of the end-to-end vector Re, using the same procedure as for obtaining τp from the normal modes. The autocorrelation ce decayed to zero for all sequences (SI Appendix, Fig. S14), highlighting complete chain relaxation. The sequence-dependent changes in τRe (Fig. 5B) mirrored the dynamic slowdown observed in viscosity and diffusivity. At fixed N, increasing nSCD slowed chain reconfiguration consistent with enhanced electrostatic interactions. Relaxation times τRe scaled with N according to a power law, with exponents ranging from 2.44 to 2.77 across different nSCD values. As with macroscopic dynamics, these exponents fall within the crossover regime between Rouse and reptation-like dynamics (31, 32, 48). We next tested Eq. 1 using simulation-derived quantities to predict τRe: the measured Re2 and the Langevin friction coefficient ζ applied in the simulations. The Rouse model systematically underestimated τRe, with errors increasing at higher nSCD values (Fig. 5C). Predictions for diffusivity and viscosity using Eqs. 2 and 3 showed similarly poor agreement with simulation data (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). These discrepancies suggest that the effective friction experienced by monomers in the dense phase exceeds the nominal Langevin friction ζ specified in the simulations.


### Rouse Framework Links Chain Relaxation Dynamics to Condensate Material Properties.
  Although the Rouse model does not quantitatively capture sequence-specific effects on chain relaxation times, viscosity, or diffusivity, we asked whether it could still serve as a useful framework by connecting chain-level relaxation to macroscopic properties. A recent study demonstrated that this is possible by using measured relaxation times in place of model parameters such as the monomeric friction coefficient ζ (24). In this approach, the diffusivity D can be expressed directly in terms of the chain relaxation time τR:[4]D=Nb23π2τR=Re23π2τR.

  Similarly, viscosity η can be written as a function of either D or τR as[5]ηD=ηs+kBTcpRe236D,[6]ητR=ηs+π2kBTcpτR12,

  where cp =c/N is the protein concentration in the dense phase.

  We applied these equations using simulation-derived values of Re2 and τRe (used in place of τR) and found that the Rouse model accurately predicted both η and D across all sequences (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Notably, this same Rouse relationship holds even when solvent effects are explicitly represented using the Martini model (SI Appendix, Fig. S16), which provides a higher-resolution description and yields lower dense-phase concentrations than the HPS model (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). These lower concentrations place the systems further away from the entangled regime, consistent with the assumption that topological constraints are lower. In contrast, incorporating entanglement corrections into the Rouse model did not improve agreement with our simulation data (SI Appendix, Fig. S18; Methods). These results indicate that the Rouse model, even without entanglement corrections, provides a quantitative link between chain relaxation and condensate material properties in the crossover regime. This is because both chain relaxation times and material properties measured from simulations implicitly account for the influence of sequence features such as charge patterning and interaction strength, even though these features are not explicitly included in the Rouse framework. This finding leads to a question: What governs the internal chain relaxation times, such as τRe, that underlie macroscopic properties like diffusivity and viscosity?

  As a preliminary step, we tested whether replacing ζ in Eq. 1 with monomer relaxation times τ0≈ζb2kBT measured directly from normal model analysis of the dense phase could improve the Rouse model’s predictions. Specifically, we utilized the following relation:[7]τR=τ0N23π2.

  Using τ0 led to slightly better agreement between Rouse predictions and simulation results (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), compared to using ζ. This improvement likely stems from the fact that τ0 implicitly accounts for crowding effects present in the dense phase. However, deviations persisted across different nSCD values, as τ0 does not capture sequence-specific effects (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S13). We also tested whether incorporating entanglement corrections into the Rouse model (Methods) could improve the prediction of τRe, using both ζ and τ0 as inputs. In both cases, the Rouse model with entanglement failed to reproduce the observed τRe values across different sequences (SI Appendix, Fig. S20).

  Together, these results demonstrate that the Rouse framework captures the relationship between chain relaxation dynamics and condensate material properties when relaxation times are known. However, it does not offer a direct connection between sequence-level features, such as charge patterning, and the resulting chain relaxation or material properties, which are beyond the scope of the model. To address this gap, we next investigated whether an alternative theoretical framework could explain the sequence-dependent dynamic slowdown observed in our simulated condensates across multiple length scales.


### Sticky Rouse-Like Framework for Sequence-Dependent Condensate Dynamics.
  In charge-rich IDP condensates, oppositely charged residues form transient interchain contacts, which can slow dynamics, especially in sequences with greater charge blockiness. The sticky Rouse model (57–62) extends the classical Rouse framework by incorporating these intermittent binding and unbinding events (Fig. 6A). To quantify these contact dynamics in our simulations, we measured τEK for each sequence, which reflects how long electrostatic E-K contacts persist, including intermittent breaking and reformation (25, 28, 29) (Methods). We found that τEK increased with chain length N and eventually plateaued (Fig. 6B). For sequences with nSCD ≤ 0.20, the plateau occurred beyond N = 100, while for nSCD > 0.20, it occurred beyond N = 150, even though macroscopic properties continued to show a dynamic slowdown. At fixed N, increasing nSCD led to longer-lived contacts, consistent with stronger electrostatic interactions between charged residues. To better understand how sequence features influence τEK, we developed a phenomenological model based on the sticky Rouse framework (Methods). The model incorporates the effects of charge patterning and viscosity, and it allows τEK to be estimated directly from sequence features such as N and nSCD. The resulting expression accurately captures the simulation trends across all sequences (solid lines in Fig. 6B) and provides a direct link between sequence-level features and local interaction timescales. Importantly, these contact lifetimes (4 to 83 ps) are much longer than the relaxation times of the shortest segments observed in the normal mode analysis (<0.7 ps for segments spanning up to approximately three monomer units; Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13), explaining why these segments show nearly identical relaxation across sequences, as their local motions are too fast to be affected by E-K contact dynamics.

  We then asked whether the sticky Rouse model could quantitatively predict the chain relaxation times τRe observed in simulations. In the classical formulation, the lifetimes of sticky bonds are typically comparable to or longer than the overall chain relaxation times. However, in our system, the measured contact lifetimes (Fig. 6B) are much shorter than chain relaxation times (Fig. 5B), and therefore the traditional assumptions of the sticky Rouse model do not strictly apply. Therefore, we use a sticky Rouse-like framework that adapts the conceptual foundation of the classical model to describe systems with transient, sequence-dependent interactions. Specifically, we use the average contact lifetime extracted from the contact autocorrelation function as an effective monomer reconfiguration timescale within a Rouse-like description. In this framework, the chain relaxation time τRs is given by[8]τRs=τEKN23π2,

  where τEK replaces τ0 in Eq. 7 as the relevant monomer relaxation time.

  Indeed, the predicted τRs values matched simulation-derived τRe across all sequences, with the data collapsing onto the identity line (Fig. 6C). Because earlier analysis established that τRe is directly coupled to macroscopic observables, this accurate prediction of τRe implies that diffusivity and viscosity can also be recovered via τEK. Indeed, using τRs in the corresponding Rouse expressions, we found that D and η can be quantitatively predicted across the sequences investigated (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). To test whether entanglement effects might further refine these predictions, we also applied a sticky Rouse model with reptation-like corrections. While the extension preserved the nSCD-dependent trend, seen in the collapse of data across sequences, it consistently overestimated τRe (SI Appendix, Fig. S22; Methods). This suggests that entanglement dynamics are not the dominant contributor in these condensates. To test the robustness of our theoretical predictions, we applied the sticky Rouse model to Martini simulations. First, we computed the average intermittent E-K contact lifetimes for N = 50 sequences across different nSCD values and found that they increase with nSCD (SI Appendix, Fig. S23), consistent with the trends observed in the HPS simulations. Next, using these lifetimes as input to the sticky Rouse model, we found that the predicted end-to-end relaxation times, diffusivities, and viscosities agree closely with the corresponding simulation results (Fig. 6 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S21), confirming that our theoretical framework remains valid across models and concentration regimes. Together, these findings show that incorporating the lifetimes as an additional timescale into the Rouse framework, when paired with a sequence-informed model of contact lifetimes, provides a robust and quantitative connection from monomer-level interactions to chain relaxation and ultimately to the macroscopic material properties of IDP condensates.


## Discussion
  Our findings offer a generalizable framework for predicting how sequence-level features of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) govern the material properties of biomolecular condensates. By anchoring the analysis in chain relaxation times, we establish a mechanistic link between residue-level interactions and emergent material properties. Through systematic variation of chain length N and charge patterning nSCD, we demonstrate that longer and more charge-segregated sequences give rise to slower dynamics in the dense phase. This slowdown is better captured by chain-level relaxation times than by monomer-level friction, highlighting the importance of cooperative effects in IDP-rich condensates. The observed scaling of relaxation times and material properties with N places our systems in a crossover regime between Rouse and reptation dynamics, suggesting that entanglements are emerging but not yet dominant. Notably, the chain lengths examined here are comparable to those found in biologically relevant IDPs (33), implying that the crossover behavior we observe may be broadly applicable to natural systems.

  An important question is whether the observed influence of nSCD on dynamics arises solely from direct sequence effects or from differences in dense-phase concentration. To disentangle these contributions, we performed control simulations in which all electrostatic interactions were removed from sequences with intermediate charge segregation (nSCD = 0.46). In these neutral systems, every bead was assigned a hydrophobicity value of λ = 0.5 and dense-phase concentration was kept fixed. Despite identical densities, the neutral systems exhibited weaker scaling of diffusivity, viscosity, and relaxation time with chain length–closer to Rouse-like behavior–compared to the charged systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S24 and Table S2). These results indicate that the slower dynamics observed with increasing nSCD cannot be explained by changes in dense-phase concentration alone; instead, they arise primarily from sequence-dependent intermolecular interactions mediated here by electrostatic complementarity that reinforce interchain associations and shift the system deeper into the semidilute-to-entangled crossover regime.

  While the classical Rouse theory models a polymer as a series of beads connected by springs with no specific interactions (24, 31, 32), this simplification limits its predictive power for complex biological systems where residue-level interactions, particularly electrostatics (63, 64), are key. In our simulations, although the chains exhibit near-ideal conformational statistics, the dynamic behavior is dominated by sequence-specific electrostatic interactions. These interactions lead to a systematic breakdown of the Rouse predictions when material properties are computed using the nominal monomeric friction. By contrast, a sticky Rouse-like framework incorporates the effect of transient interresidue interactions and allows us to replace monomer relaxation times with contact lifetimes τEK, enabling accurate prediction of both chain and condensate-level dynamics. Importantly, our phenomenological model relates τEK directly to sequence features such as nSCD, allowing the full sequence-dynamics-material property link to be quantitatively established.

  Our study focuses on charge-neutral E-K sequences, which allow controlled tuning of charge patterning while avoiding confounding effects from net charge or hydrophobicity. These findings are directly relevant to many natural IDPs that exhibit varied charge distributions (65, 66), and they provide a baseline for future studies incorporating additional sequence complexity. For example, the influence of hydrophobic or aromatic residues, which can contribute (45, 67–69) to short-range and directional interactions, can be explored in future extensions of the sticky Rouse framework. Overall, this work lays the foundation for predictive sequence-to-property design principles for biomolecular condensates with tailored material characteristics.


## Methods

### Sequence Generation.
  To investigate the effects of chain length N and charge patterning on the material properties of dense phases, we utilized charge-neutral polyampholytes composed of equal numbers of glutamic acid (denoted as “E,” net charge qE = −e, diameter σE = 5.92 Å, molar mass mE = 129.1 g/mol) and lysine (denoted as “K,” net charge qK = +e, diameter σK = 6.36 Å, molar mass mK = 128.2 g/mol). These sequences served as model intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) with N values ranging from 20 to 250. For each N, we systematically varied the charge patterning, creating sequences that ranged from perfectly alternating arrangements of E and K to fully segregated blocks of E and K. To quantify the distribution of charges along the chain, we used the sequence charge decoration (SCD) parameter (34, 35):[9]SCD=1N∑m=2N∑n=1m-1qmqnm-n.

  In this representation, sequences with alternating E and K residues exhibit the maximum SCD value (SCDmax), while sequences with a diblock distribution have the minimum SCD value (SCDmin). To compare sequences of different charge patterning and lengths, we normalized the SCD values using the minimum and maximum SCD limits for each N, resulting in the normalized SCD (nSCD) defined as[10]nSCD=SCD-SCDmaxSCDmin-SCDmax,

  With this normalization, an nSCD of 0 corresponds to a perfectly alternating sequence, while an nSCD of 1 corresponds to a diblock sequence, regardless of the length of the sequence (40, 70). We selected sequences with nSCD values of 0, 0.02, 0.20, 0.46, and 1 for each N, allowing a systematic investigation of the impact of charge patterning on the dense phase material properties. E-K sequences generated are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1.

  The standard Lennard-Jones potential ULJr is expressed as[13]ULJr=4ϵσr12-σr6,

  Here, σ corresponds to the average diameter of the interacting residues and ϵ = 0.2 kcal/mol reflects the interaction strength. ### Simulation Details.
  To study the dense phase of E-K sequences with varying N and nSCD, we conducted NPT simulations of the sequences in a cubic simulation box at a constant pressure of 0 atm for a total duration of 0.5 μs. During this time, the sequences reached their preferred dense phase concentration ρ (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). Following this, we transitioned to Langevin dynamics (LD) simulations in the NVT ensemble, adjusting the simulation duration based on N and nSCD. Sequences with longer chain lengths and higher charge segregation, particularly those with N = 250 and nSCD = 0.02, 0.20, 0.46, and 1, as well as N = 200 with nSCD = 0.20, 0.46, and 1, required longer simulation runs of 2.5 μs to capture the diffusive motion of chains. For the remaining E-K sequences, NVT simulations were performed for 1 μs. All dense-phase NVT simulations included a 40-ns equilibration period, and only the data after this equilibration period were used for analysis. Notably, E-K sequences of N = 20 and 50 with nSCD = 0, as well as N = 20 with nSCD = 0.02, did not form a stable dense phase during the P = 0 atm simulation. This suggests that their perfectly alternating charge arrangement led to weaker intermolecular attractions (77). The N = 50 trajectories from our recently published work (25) were used for all the analyses involving N = 50 in this work.

  Single-chain simulations of E-K sequences of varying N were conducted in a cubic box with an edge length of 400 Å, ensuring that the box size was large enough to avoid artificial self-interactions between the chains and their periodic images. Each simulation was run for a total duration of 1 μs.

  All simulations were performed using HOOMD-blue (version 2.9.3) (78) with features extended using azplugins (version 0.10.1) (79). Dense phase simulations were conducted for 1500, 600, 300, 200, 150, and 120 chains of E-K sequences for N = 20, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250, respectively. Simulations were conducted at 300 K with periodic boundary conditions applied in all three Cartesian directions with a timestep of 10 fs and a Langevin damping factor of tdamp = 1000 ps was applied to set the friction coefficient ζi of a residue of mass mi in the chain to ζi=mitdamp. Trajectories were saved at intervals ranging from 0.1 ps to 500 ps to compute segmental dynamics, contact dynamics, and the condensate material properties.

### Normal Mode Analysis.
  We computed the normal modes Xp of E-K sequences inside the dense phase as (40, 52–55).[20]Xp=2N∑iricospπNi-12.

  Here, ri is the position vector of the i-th monomer and mode number p corresponds to a subchain of N-1p segments for a chain of length N. Therefore, modes 1≤p≤N-1 describe the internal relaxation of a chain. Specifically, p = 1 corresponds to the slowest chain-level dynamics. The autocorrelation function cp of Xp (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), which is well described by a stretched exponential function cpt=e-t/τβ, is given by[21]cpt=Xpt.Xp0Xp2,

  where τ is the characteristic time scale, and β is the stretching exponent.

  The characteristic relaxation time τp of mode p (SI Appendix, Fig. S12) is calculated as follows:[22]τp=∫0∞cpt dt=τβΓ1β.

  Note that Γ is the gamma function.


### Calculation of Monomer–Monomer Lifetimes.
  To estimate contact lifetimes of oppositely charged residues in the dense phase, we computed the intermittent contact time autocorrelation function (25, 28, 29) c(t) as (SI Appendix, Fig. S28):[23]ct=h0hth20,

  where h(t) is a step function that equals 1 if a pair of oppositely charged residues i and j are in contact at time t, and 0 otherwise. A contact was defined as occurring when the distance between residues was below a cutoff of 1.5 σ, where σ is the average diameter of E and K residues. The autocorrelation function captures how long contacts persist, including repeated breaking and reforming of interactions. The characteristic contact lifetime τEK was determined by integrating c(t) up to the time at which it decayed to 0.05 (25, 26).


### Phenomenological Model for Sequence-Dependent Contact Lifetimes.
  To quantitatively model the effect of sequence on the contact lifetime τEK, we built on the sticky Rouse framework (57, 62), where the sticker lifetime is given by[24]τEK=τbeε/kBT.

  Here, τb is the baseline contact time in the absence of specific bonding interactions (primarily due to random collisions), and ε is an effective interaction energy between oppositely charged residues. To account for sequence effects, we decomposed ε into[25]ε=ε0+ΔεnSCD,

  where ε0 is contact energy for a perfectly alternating sequence, and Δε captures the influence of charge clustering. We estimated Δε≈αnc, where nc is the average number of like-charge nearest neighbors, and found empirically that nc=1.76nSCD0.24 (SI Appendix, Fig. S29).

  We also accounted for viscosity effects on τb, using[26]τb∼η1+kη,

  where η = aNγ and k is a constant. Combining these expressions, we obtained the following empirical equation[27]τEKnSCD,N=aNγ1+bNγec nSCD0.24,

  where the fitted parameters were γ=1.44, a=0.022, b=0.0066, and c=3.2. These values were selected to best fit the trends in simulation data across all sequence variants. The scaling exponent γ closely matched the chain length dependence of viscosity, supporting the idea that longer chains hinder contact dynamics.


### Rouse Theory with Entanglement Corrections.
  To evaluate whether chain entanglements contribute to the dynamics of IDPs in our simulated dense phases, we tested an extended version of the Rouse model that includes entanglement corrections. In this framework (24, 32), Rouse chain motion is constrained within an effective tube of diameter a (also known as entanglement spacing), representing the spatial confinement due to surrounding chains. The chain diffusion coefficient is given by[28]D=kBT3Nζa2Re2.

  On the other hand, the dense phase viscosity of the polymer solution is given by[29]η=ηs+cζNb236Re2a2.

  The chain relaxation time that governs the reptation dynamics is the disentanglement time τd (also known as reptation time) (24, 88), which is the time it takes for a chain to disentangle from a tube within which it was confined and is given by[30]τd=ζN3b4π2kBTa2=3τRRe2a2,

  where τR is the Rouse time and b is computed using the mean squared end-to-end distance Re2 of chains within the dense phase using the relation Re2=Nb2. τd can also be written in terms of the monomer relaxation times τ0≈ζb2kBT as[31]τd=τ0N3b2π2a2.

  On the other hand, we can also express the dense phase properties in terms of the chain disentanglement time. By combining Eqs. 28 and 30, D can be written in terms of τd as[32]D=Re23π2τd.

  This expression matches the form seen in the original Rouse model (Eq. 4), indicating that reptation theory does not introduce additional correction terms for predicting the diffusion coefficient from chain relaxation dynamics. Therefore, we did not separately compute D using the simulation-derived τRe in the entanglement-corrected Rouse equation, as it would yield the same result as the standard Rouse expression D=Re23π2τR.

  Similarly, combining Eqs. 29 and 30, η can be expressed in terms of τd as[33]ητR=ηs+π2kBTcpτd12Re2a2,

  where cp (=c/N) is the protein concentration in the dense phase. First, we estimated the entanglement spacing a for E-K sequences in our simulated condensates through topological analysis using the Z1+ algorithm developed by Kröger’s group (89, 90). This method constructs primitive paths by minimizing the chain’s contour lengths under the constraint of fixed ends. From the resulting primitive path configuration, the mean number of entanglements (also referred to as kinks) per chain Z was estimated (SI Appendix, Fig. S30A). The entanglement length NeM-kink, defined as the number of monomers between successive kinks, was then estimated (SI Appendix, Fig. S30B) as NeM-kink=dNdZ, where N is the chain length. The corresponding entanglement spacing a (SI Appendix, Fig. S30C) was computed using the relation (88):[34]a=45NeM-kinkb2.

  We used simulation-derived quantities Re2, τ0, and τRe (as τd), along with a from topology analysis and the mean monomer friction (ζ), in Eqs. 30, 31, and 33 to predict η and τRe (SI Appendix, Figs. S18 and S20).

  We also evaluated an extended framework by incorporating sticky interactions into the entanglement-corrected Rouse theory. Here, we substituted τ0 in Eq. 31 with τEK. In this case, the chain relaxation time becomes[35]τds=τEKN3b2π2a2=τEKN2Re2π2a2.

  We used simulation-derived τEK along with a from topology analysis in the above equation to predict τRe (SI Appendix, Fig. S22).

  While theoretical predictions rely on end-to-end vector relaxation times, we also computed end-to-end distance relaxation times and found them to be about three times shorter than vector relaxation times (SI Appendix, Fig. S31). This provides a quantitative reference for interpreting experimental measurements, which typically has access to only distance-based relaxation in Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) experiments. The shorter distance relaxation times observed here could contribute to the apparent necessity of including entanglement corrections in experimental analyses (24), as the measurements are based on distance relaxation rather than vector relaxation.


### Martini Simulations.
  The Martini data shown in Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Figs. S16, S21, and S23 are from trajectories generated in our recently published work (25) and for the sake of completeness, we briefly summarize the simulation details. We simulated E-K sequences (N = 50) using the Martini 3 coarse-grained force field with explicit solvent and ions (91). The protein chains were coarse-grained using martinize2 Python script, and nonbonded interactions were modeled using the Verlet cutoff scheme with an 11 Å cutoff for van der Waals and electrostatic interactions. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the reaction-field method with a dielectric constant of 15 (92).

  To generate the dense phase, we initially placed 80 chains in a slab geometry (100 Å × 100 Å × 740 Å) and solvated them with ~50,000 water particles using the insane python script (93). After energy minimization with the steepest descent algorithm, we equilibrated the system in the NPT ensemble (T = 300 K, P = 1 bar along the z-direction) for 20 ns using a 20 fs timestep. The production run was conducted for 18 μs under the same conditions, allowing the slab to reach equilibrium. The dense phase was then extracted and simulated in a cuboid box at 100 mM salt concentration to match HPS model conditions.

  To compute the translational diffusivity D, we performed NVT simulations at 300 K for 10 μs and extracted D values from the long-time regime where MSD scaled linearly with time. To compute the zero-shear viscosity η using the Green–Kubo relation (50, 80–85), we conducted 10 replica simulations, each lasting 10 μs, and averaged η over all replicas. All simulations were performed using GROMACS (version 2023.1) (94) with a velocity-rescaling thermostat (95) (time constant of 1 ps) and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat (96) (time constant of 12 ps). Intermittent contact lifetimes, mean end-to-end distances and end-to-end vector relaxation times were calculated using the same procedure as described for the HPS model, using only the backbone beads.
