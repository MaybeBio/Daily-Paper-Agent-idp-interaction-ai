# Targeting the intrinsically disordered AR-NTD through a machine learning-based enhanced sampling workflow


## Abstract
  Targeting the intrinsically disordered N-terminal domain of the androgen receptor (AR-NTD) represents a promising strategy to overcome resistance in prostate cancer. However, its inherent lack of a stable tertiary structure and highly dynamic conformational ensemble pose formidable challenges for rational drug design. This study introduces an integrated computational workflow that combines enhanced sampling techniques and machine learning collective variables to identify druggable conformations of the AR-NTD and elucidate the binding mechanism of its modulator, EPI-002. We characterize nine metastable states of the Tau-5 region and reveal that ligand recognition is driven by π–π stacking and structured water-mediated hydrogen bonds. Leveraging these insights, we perform structure-based virtual screening based on the identified druggable conformations and identify K53, a rationally designed AR-NTD antagonist, which exhibits potent anti-proliferative activity in enzalutamide-resistant prostate cancer cells. K53 directly binds the AR-NTD, suppresses AR transcriptional activity, and demonstrates high selectivity for cancer cells. This work provides a rational design paradigm for targeting intrinsically disordered proteins and offers a therapeutic candidate for resistant prostate cancer.


## Introduction
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) play diverse biological roles and are closely related to a wide range of major human diseases1–3. Unlike folded proteins, IDPs lack a stable three-dimensional (3D) structure under physiological conditions due to their relatively flat free energy landscapes, resulting in highly heterogeneous conformational ensembles. Consequently, describing IDPs using a single or limited number of representative structures is insufficient to capture their structural complexity. Instead, dynamic conformational ensembles, representing a distribution of interconverting states, could better capture their structural heterogeneity and flexibility. This strategy has been applied across multiple systems, including α-synuclein4, tau5, c-Myc6, and p537, demonstrating its utility in IDP-targeted drug discovery. However, these studies have not yet provided a comprehensive exploration of IDP conformation space, nor have they systematically investigated IDP-ligand interactions to guide rational drug design, highlighting the need for advanced computational and experimental workflows to systematically map conformational landscapes and decode ligand-binding mechanisms, thereby accelerating the development of IDP-targeted therapeutics.

  Enhanced sampling techniques in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations represent powerful tools to characterize IDP ensembles. These approaches enable extensive exploration of conformational space and reconstruction of free energy landscapes, yielding both representative conformations and their statistical weights. Enhanced sampling techniques can be broadly classified into two types: collective variable (CV)-based methods and CV-free methods8, the distinction primarily reflects whether explicit low-dimensional CVs are introduced. CV-based techniques apply bias potential or biasing force along predefined CVs to facilitate barrier crossing9, with prominent examples including umbrella sampling10, metadynamics11,12, adaptive biasing force13, and on-the-fly probability enhanced sampling (OPES)14–16. In contrast, CV-free methods without relying on predefined CVs, typically by modifying the effective sampling distribution or potential energy surface. Representative examples include replica exchange molecular dynamics (REMD)17,18, replica exchange with solute tempering (REST)19,20, and accelerated molecular dynamics (aMD)21 enhance sampling by constructing a series of overlapping probability distributions without relying on predefined CVs.

  The conformational dynamics of IDPs are often high-dimensional, diverse, and mutually orthogonal, posing significant challenges in defining low-dimensional CVs that accurately capture their slow dynamical modes. As a result, CV-free approaches like REST have been adopted in IDP studies due to their simplicity and generality22–24. While REST provides a global, unbiased enhancement of sampling, it still lacks the ability to selectively probe how ligands and proteins actually associate. In contrast, the emergence of machine learning-based collective variables (MLCVs)25 has introduced new opportunities, as they not only retain sampling efficiency but also enable a more directed characterization of ligand–protein binding pathways. MLCVs can be categorized into three primary categories: supervised, unsupervised, and time-informed learning. Supervised methods, such as Deep Linear Discriminant Analysis (Deep-LDA)26 and Deep Targeted Discriminant Analysis (Deep-TDA)27, aim to distinguish known metastable states. Unsupervised learning approaches, such as AutoEncoder-based CVs (AE CVs)28,29, focus on dimensionality reduction. Time-informed learning methods, exemplified by Time-lagged Independent Component Analysis (Deep-TICA)30, are designed to extract the system’s slow dynamical modes. A series of studies has demonstrated the considerable potential of MLCVs in biomolecular simulations31–34, providing a data-driven framework to capture complex conformational dynamics and enhance sampling efficiency in disordered systems.

  The N-terminal domain of the androgen receptor (AR-NTD), a prototypical IDP that functions as the AR’s primary transactivation domain, plays an essential role in prostate cancer (PCa)35. Currently, all androgen receptor (AR) antagonists approved for PCa treatment, such as bicalutamide and enzalutamide (Enz), share a primary mechanism of action: targeting the ligand-binding pocket of the AR ligand-binding domain (AR-LBD). However, the majority of patients develop acquired resistance to these therapies. Clinical resistance mechanisms commonly include AR-LBD mutations, AR gene amplification, the expression of AR splice variants (ARVs), and de novo intratumoral synthesis of androgens36. It is particularly noteworthy that ARVs, which lack the ligand-binding pocket or entire LBD, are inherently resistant to both marketed AR-LBD-targeted antagonists and those in development. ARVs are expressed in over 20% of early-stage PCa patients and nearly 40% of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) patients37, posing significant therapeutic challenges. Given that the resistance mechanisms of mutations, amplification, and splice variants all exert their effects through the AR signaling pathway while preserving the integrity of the NTD, the development of AR antagonists targeting the AR-NTD represents a promising strategy to overcome clinical resistance, particularly against ARVs that evade current LBD-directed therapies.

  The primary compounds targeting the AR-NTD are EPI-001 and its analogs, which were discovered through high-throughput screening and are derivatives of bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BADGE) originally isolated from marine sponges, and they represent promising candidate therapeutics for CRPC38. However, EPI analogs suffer from several limitations: modest binding affinity, limited specificity toward the AR-NTD, and a covalent binding mechanism that may lead to off-target reactivity and toxicity concerns. Importantly, medicinal chemistry optimization of the EPI scaffold yielded EPI-738639, a nonvalent analog lacking the epoxide warhead with approximately 20-fold higher potency than EPI-001 that progressed to clinical evaluation. However, masfaniten ultimately failed in phase I/II trials, highlighting the ongoing challenges of achieving robust efficacy and selectivity when targeting the intrinsically disordered AR-NTD. Therefore, elucidating how EPI analogs recognize the binding site of the AR-NTD is critical, not only for understanding their mode of action but also for enabling the rational, structure-guided design of next-generation AR-NTD antagonists with improved efficacy and safety. It has been reported that EPI analogs covalently bind to Cys404 at the Tau-5 region of the AR-NTD40,41. Previous enhanced sampling studies have primarily examined the interaction of EPI-002, the most potent stereoisomer of EPI-001, with the R2–R3 subregions of Tau-5. These studies observed that the ligand engages transient helical surfaces, induces partially folded rotational states, and that aromatic residues predominantly drive this process16. However, the covalent nature of EPI-002 remains poorly characterized. Recently, MD studies of covalent EPI-002 adducts have shown that covalent attachment can markedly alter the conformational ensemble of the AR-NTD42. Nevertheless, it remains unclear how small molecules initially engage the AR-NTD and progress toward covalent adduct formation, knowledge that would be essential for guiding rational drug design.

  In this work, we established a generalized workflow integrating CV-free enhanced sampling, CV-based enhanced sampling, and MLCVs to identify druggable conformations of IDPs and elucidate their ligand interaction mechanisms, thereby providing guidance for rational drug design. As a case study, this workflow is applied to investigate the AR-NTD. The analysis is expanded to the full Tau-5 ensemble, explicitly characterizing how transient binding pockets guide EPI-002 recognition and facilitate its covalent stabilization at Cys404. Our findings reveal that the hydrogen-bond networks between EPI-002 and the protein/water environment essentially reinforce its π–π stacking with aromatic residues, thereby underpinning ligand recognition and covalent locking. Furthermore, virtual screening based on its workflow identified an antagonist, K53, the first rationally designed antagonist against the AR-NTD. Its discovery highlights not only the conformational plasticity of the AR-NTD that underpins its druggability but also the practical feasibility of our strategy for elucidating ligand recognition in IDPs and discovering lead compounds targeting IDPs.

### An ML-based enhanced sampling workflow for deciphering ligand binding mechanisms to IDPs
  Our workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. Given the high-dimensional complexity and orthogonal dynamics of IDPs, we first employ the REST algorithm to explore the conformational space. From a probabilistic perspective, ligand binding is expected to occur preferentially within metastable conformational states. Due to the temporal discontinuity inherent in replica exchange trajectories (resulting from frequent swapping between replicas), conventional time-informed methods cannot be directly applied. AEs, however, offer a robust alternative. Enhanced sampling was carried out using replica exchange with REST3, employing eight replicas distributed over 300–450 K, with all protein atoms selected for solute tempering. Replica exchange attempts were performed every 2 ps, resulting in an average exchange acceptance rate of approximately 6.5%, which is sufficient to ensure replica mixing and enable exploration of the conformational space. Full simulation parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Each replica was simulated for 4.1 μs, yielding a total aggregated simulation time of 32.8 μs.

In addition, the time evolution of the radius of gyration (Rg) across replicas was monitored and showed stable fluctuations without systematic drift (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating a well-equilibrated and converged ensemble.   To further refine the ensemble, we applied a maximum entropy reweighting algorithm64,65 using Cα chemical shifts from the 300 K trajectory as constraints (“Methods”).   The FES projected onto Rg and Sα (Fig. 2b) revealed that the dominant free energy minima reside at Rg < 2.0 nm, suggesting that metastable conformations of Tau-5 favor compact states. This observation is consistent with our hypothesis that compact IDP conformations are more likely to form transient binding pockets. In particular, the region defined by 3 < Sα < 6 and 1.4 < Rg < 2.0 nm corresponds to the “molten globule” region previously reported22. While Rg and Sα are physically interpretable, they provide limited resolution for distinguishing metastable states. To overcome this limitation, we introduced AE CVs.

### Unveiling the binding pockets in metastable conformations using SWISH
  In our SWISH simulations, benzene was used as a solvent probe at a concentration of 1 M. For each metastable conformation identified from the AE CV, four independent replicas were prepared with scaling factors λ set to 1, 1.05, 1, and 1.15. Increasing λ enhances the solvation of apolar surface regions, thereby modulating hydrophobic interactions on the protein surface and effectively mimicking the presence of small-molecule ligands.

  Each metastable conformation was subjected to 50 ns of SWISH simulation. Analyses focused on the λ = 1.0 replicas to identify conformations harboring druggable binding pockets. As illustrated in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4, SWISH successfully revealed binding sites across all sampled metastable states, demonstrating its effectiveness in uncovering cryptic ligand-binding pockets in disordered systems.Fig. 4SWISH simulations uncover druggable pockets in metastable Tau-5 conformations.Representative initial and final structures from SWISH simulations show the opening of cryptic ligand-binding pockets in the metastable conformations identified from the AE CVs.

  Subsequently, molecular docking of EPI-002 into the identified pockets was performed using the Glide algorithm66,67. The docking results consistently placed the chlorine atoms of EPI-002 in close proximity to the sulfur atom of Cys404 in Tau-5, strongly suggesting a favorable geometry for covalent bond formation. In addition, numerous aromatic residues were found surrounding the ligand within the ligand-binding site, implicating them in π–π stacking and other stabilizing non-covalent interactions during ligand recognition (Supplementary Fig. 5).

  To further confirm the feasibility of covalent binding, we performed covalent docking68,69, which confirmed that Cys404 within the identified binding pockets can indeed form covalent bonds with EPI-002. Remarkably, the covalently docked poses closely resemble those obtained from the non-covalent docking (Supplementary Fig. 6), lending further support to the hypothesized binding mode of EPI-002 with the AR-NTD.


### Coupling OPES with MLCVs for precise binding free energy calculations
  Water plays a pivotal role in ligand binding70,71 and the conformational dynamics of IDPs72. Inspired by recent advances in the integration of water-mediated interactions into CVs32,33, we designed an MLCV to capture water behavior relevant to both ligand solvation and binding site hydration. To characterize ligand solvation, we calculated the water coordination number around the chloride atom of EPI-002, denoted as {G}. For hydration at the binding site, we calculated the water coordination number around the sulfur atom of Cys404, denoted as {H}. In addition, high-density water regions within the binding site were identified as hydration spots {Vi}, with their geometric centers used to define supplementary descriptors through local water coordination analysis.

  These descriptors {G}, {H}, {Vi} were then incorporated into a multitask learning framework46 to construct a prior CV for enhanced sampling. The multitask CV was trained using two datasets: (i) a labeled dataset derived from short unbiased MD simulations capturing bound and unbound states, and (ii) an unlabeled dataset obtained from OPES-explore16 trajectories that sample rare events and transition pathways. This formulation allows the CV to encode both structural differences between metastable states (via labeled data) and solvent-mediated transition mechanisms (via unlabeled data), thereby providing a practical CV for estimating ligand–protein binding free energies in disordered systems.

  We next performed OPES-explore16 simulations to compute the binding free energy (∆G) of EPI-002 to the nine metastable conformations of Tau-5 (Supplementary Fig. 7). For these simulations, we employed two CVs: (i) the distance z from the binding site, defined as the projection of the ligand’s center of mass along the binding axis, and (ii) the multitask water CV described earlier. To ensure efficient sampling of binding/unbinding events, we applied a funnel-shaped restraint potential73,74, which confines the accessible volume of the unbound ligand and prevents it from drifting into bulk solvent. The entropic correction associated with the funnel restraint was computed analytically and incorporated into the final binding free energy. To minimize artifacts from protein conformational fluctuations, positional restraints were imposed to all Cα atoms throughout the simulations.

  Each trajectory exhibited multiple binding and unbinding events, ensuring adequate sampling of both bound and unbound states (Supplementary Figs. 9–17). Convergence of the binding free energy was rigorously verified across independent simulations (Supplementary Figs. 18–26). The resulting corrected ∆G, reported in Table 2, ranges from – 3 to – 5 kcal/mol across the nine metastable conformations, with conformation 6 showing the most favorable binding energy (– 4.82 kcal/mol).   The observed weak binding affinity in the millimolar range contrasts with the low micromolar affinities reported experimentally75,76.   Finally, we projected the two-dimensional FES onto the z coordinate and the multitask water CV (Supplementary Figs. 27–35). The resulting FES lacked well-defined basins, reflecting the limited accuracy of the multitask water CV. This outcome is expected, as a simple multitask framework does not guarantee the extraction of slow modes. Consequently, more rigorous MLCV algorithms are therefore required to capture the essential dynamical degrees of freedom governing IDP–ligand interactions.


### Hydrogen bond network formation among EPI-002 and Tau-5 revealed by Deep-TICA
  To elucidate the binding/unbinding mechanisms of EPI-002 with Tau-5, we retrained a set of Deep-TICA CVs using the trajectories from the OPES-explore simulations. To capture the evolution of ligand-protein interactions, we considered the distances between the aromatic moieties of the ligand and the aromatic side chains of Tau-5, for example, {dd_Tyr406_lig1}, defined as the distance between the aromatic center of Tyr406 and the ligand’s distal ring (relative to the chlorine atom), and {dd_Tyr406_lig2}, defined as the distance to the proximal ring. Additional descriptors include the center-of-mass distance between the ligand and the binding site ({com}), as well as the distance between the sulfur atom of Cys404 and the chlorine atom of EPI-002 ({dd_Cys_lig}). These descriptors were designed to capture π–π stacking, noncovalent contacts, and potential reactivity at the chlorohydrin moiety. Using these inputs, we trained two Deep-TICA models: one to construct a Distance CV and the other trained using water coordination descriptors to yield the Water CV.

  Projecting the two-dimensional FES onto the Deep-TICA Water and Distance CVs enabled us to resolve metastable binding states and assess descriptor contributions. Here, we focus on metastable conformations 2 and 6, which exhibited distinct binding behaviors and well-converged binding free energies (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 6). Complete analyses for all nine conformations are provided in Supplementary Figs. 36–49.

  As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, the FES of conformation 2 revealed three different binding poses (B, B1, B2) sharing similar overall geometries. Among them, pose B corresponds to the global free energy minimum, while B1 and B2 are higher in free energy by 0.369 kcal/mol and 2.359 kcal/mol, respectively. In pose B, EPI-002 forms hydrogen bonds with His382, Ala396, and Tyr445. In B1 and B2, subtle ligand rearrangements disrupt hydrogen bonding with Tyr445 and His382, respectively, destabilizing these states. Hydration analysis revealed that water regions {V3}, {V8}, {V16} (containing 4, 3, and 5 molecules, respectively) localize between EPI-002 and His382. During ligand dissociation, an intermediate state (I) emerges where protein–ligand hydrogen bonds are lost, and the pocket becomes fully hydrated, containing five water molecules. Three of these waters, residing in {V3} and {V16}, form bridging hydrogen bonds between the chlorohydrin group of EPI-002 and His382. In the fully unbound state (U), the ligand is completely solvated, and the pocket returns to its apo form. Descriptor analysis identified {V3}, {V8}, {V16} as the strongest contributors to the Water CV, while the Distance CV was dominated by {dd_His382_lig1}, reflecting the central role of His382 in both direct and water-mediated interactions.

### Discovery of AR-NTD antagonists based on identified druggable conformations
  Building on our workflow, which identified druggable conformations of the AR-NTD and their interactions with the known modulator EPI-002, we applied this approach to discover antagonists through virtual screening (see Methods). The compound libraries from Specs ( ~ 200,000 compounds, downloaded in November 2023) and ChemDiv (ChemDiv 2021 release, ~200,000 compounds) were used. For each conformation, the top 1% of compounds from Specs and the top 0.1% from Chemdiv were retained based on their ranking. Compounds that were consistently enriched in at least three of the nine conformations were prioritized. Following drug-likeness filtering and clustering, 58 candidates were selected for biological evaluation, and their SMILES representations are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

  Initially, these compounds were tested for antiproliferative activity in PCa cell lines with high AR expression, including LNCaP (expressing full-length AR) and 22Rv1 (expressing AR-V7). Six compounds exhibited notable inhibitory effects, each suppressing cell growth by over 35% in both cell lines (Fig. 6a). Among them, compound K53 demonstrated the most potent inhibitory effect (Fig. 6b). It not only outperformed the second-generation AR antagonist Enz in LNCaP cells but also effectively inhibited the proliferation of enzalutamide-resistant 22Rv1 and LNCaP-EnzR cells (Fig. 6c). Crucially, K53 displayed negligible cytotoxicity in the non-tumorigenic human prostate stromal cell line WPMY−1, comparable to Enz, and similarly exhibited minimal antiproliferative effects in non-tumorigenic fibroblast cells (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 51), indicating its high selectivity for cancer cells.   Mechanistic studies further confirmed the activity of K53. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays using the AR-NTD-AF1 fragment revealed its direct binding with a dissociation constant (KD) of 2.41 × 10⁻⁵ M (Fig. 6e). To assess the specificity of the observed interaction, reference compounds with well-characterized AR binding modes were incorporated into the SPR analysis. The AF1-targeting inhibitor EPI-002 exhibited a clear, concentration-dependent interaction with AF1, yielding a KD of 6.28 × 10⁻⁵ M. In contrast, the LBD-directed antagonist enzalutamide failed to produce a dose-dependent SPR response, indicating that the assay selectively reports AF1 engagement (Supplementary Fig. 52A). A fluorescence polarization competition assay further indicated that K53 does not act through the canonical ligand-binding pocket. Whereas enzalutamide efficiently displaced a fluorescent ligand from the recombinant AR-LBD, K53 exhibited only weak and non-dose-dependent competition, even at high concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 52B). As an orthogonal validation in solution, ligand-observed 1H NMR spectroscopy was performed. While K53 alone exhibited sharp, well-resolved resonances characteristic of a freely tumbling ligand, pronounced line broadening and signal attenuation were observed when K53 was combined with the AF1 fragment, indicating that K53 has affinity for AF1 (Fig. 6f). This interaction was further supported by WaterLOGSY experiments, which showed a characteristic signal sign inversion for K53 selectively in the presence of AF1, but not the AR DNA-binding domain (DBD) (Supplementary Fig. 53). Finally, target engagement was evaluated using a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) in LNCaP cells. Treatment with K53 induced a reproducible rightward shift in the AR melting curve relative to the DMSO control, indicative of increased thermal stability and consistent with direct target engagement in cells (Fig. 6g). Collectively, these complementary biochemical, biophysical, and cellular assays support a specific interaction of K53 with the AR-NTD-AF1 region.

  Given that AR functions as a nuclear hormone receptor whose oncogenic potential is mediated through transcriptional regulation, we further assessed the impact of K53 on AR-mediated transcriptional activity. Utilizing the LNCaP-ARR2PB-eGFP cell line79, K53 was found to dose-dependently inhibit dihydrotestosterone (DHT)-induced AR activity, with an IC₅₀ of 2.331 μM (Fig. 6h). Given the sequence similarity between the AR and other steroid receptors, we next examined the nuclear receptor selectivity of K53. Reporter assays showed that K53 did not antagonize the activity of progesterone receptor (PR), glucocorticoid receptor (GR), or mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) induced by exogenous hormones, respectively, nor did it activate PR or MR in the absence of hormones. These results indicate that K53 is largely specific to AR, without apparent cross-reactivity toward other steroid receptors (Supplementary Fig. 54). Consistently, quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses showed that K53 significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of canonical AR target genes including KLK3 (PSA), FKBP5, and TMPRSS2 in a concentration-dependent manner in LNCaP cells, whereas treatment with EPI-002 at 5 μM exhibited no notable inhibitory effect (Fig. 6i). These findings were further corroborated at the protein level, indicating that K53 effectively disrupts AR signaling at both the transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. 6j).

  Structural modeling provided further insights. As shown in Fig. 6k, l, K53 binds within the hydrophobic pocket of metastable conformation 6 of Tau-5. The phenyl moiety anchors into the pocket through hydrophobic interactions, while the terminal triazole ring forms stable hydrogen bonds via its polar substituents. Importantly, the three-dimensional superposition reveals a pronounced structural resemblance between K53 and EPI-002: the conserved hydroxyl–ether–aromatic motif aligns closely in space, and the orientation of the hydrophobic aromatic rings shows a clear overlap. This strong structural similarity highlights a conserved structure–binding relationship, indicating that K53 remains within the EPI chemical scaffold while introducing modifications that may enhance activity and drug-like properties. Together, these interactions allow K53 to achieve a stable binding mode within Tau-5. This conserved structure-binding relationship suggests that K53 may retain functional features of EPI-like molecules while offering improved activity and drug-like properties.

  Consistent with this structural analysis, OPES-based free energy calculations estimate a binding free energy of − 8.33 kcal/mol for K53 in metastable conformation 6 (Supplementary Fig. 58), corresponding to a predicted KD of approximately 8 × 10−7 M. This value is in reasonable agreement with the experimentally measured affinity of 2.41 × 10−5 M, supporting the validity of the identified binding mode. Together, these computational and experimental results indicate that K53 achieves a stable binding configuration within Tau-5. Furthermore, rational modification of K53, for example, incorporating a nucleophilic group to enable covalent interaction, represents a promising avenue to further enhance its potency.


### Assessing the transferability of the workflow to c-Myc
  To validate the general applicability of our workflow beyond AR-NTD, we applied it to the c-Myc system using the intrinsically disordered C-terminal fragment (N353-S437), demonstrating its transferability across distinct IDPs.

  Using the same setup as for Tau-5, the system was modeled with a99SB-disp protein force field and the corresponding a99SB-disp water model. REST3 enhanced sampling was carried out over the 300–450 K temperature range for 1 μs, using parameters identical to those employed for Tau-5. It is worth noting that, in this case, our objective was solely to sample metastable conformations, rather than to perform a comprehensive equilibrium characterization of the conformational ensemble as conducted for Tau-5. Analysis of the 300 K replica using an AE for dimensionality reduction revealed seven distinct metastable minima (Fig. 7a). Among these, metastable conformation 1 was selected for further investigation, as it provides a suitable structural basis for pocket opening and subsequent ligand docking (Fig. 7c). ## Discussion
  In this work, we developed a broadly applicable enhanced sampling workflow that integrates MLCVs with generalized ensemble and importance sampling techniques to investigate druggable conformations of IDPs and their ligand-binding mechanisms. Using the AR-NTD and its modulator EPI-002 as a model system, we identified nine druggable conformations and revealed that ligand recognition is mediated not only by π–π stacking but also by key aromatic residues. These residues participate in extensive hydrogen-bond networks involving both trapped water molecules and the ligand, highlighting the cooperative role of structured waters in IDP–ligand interactions. Moreover, structure-based virtual screening against the identified conformations led to the discovery of the AR-NTD antagonist K53, whose activity was experimentally validated. This marks the first breakthrough in rational drug design targeting the intrinsically disordered AR-NTD.

  Despite these advances, our workflow warrants further refinement. Future enhancements include incorporating machine learning committor function82,83 and replacing conventional feedforward architectures with graph neural networks84. In addition, while EPI-002 is known to act via covalent binding, our study focused only on its noncovalent interactions. As a result, the chemical steps underlying covalent bond formation are not explicitly described here. Incorporating QM/MM calculations77 or hybrid quantum-classical sampling schemes into the workflow would enable more accurate characterization of covalent reaction pathways and provide a more complete mechanism description of AR-NTD–ligand interactions.

  More broadly, the workflow presented here is not limited to the androgen receptor but is readily extensible to other IDPs and challenging drug-discovery targets, such as c-Myc, tau, and related disordered regulatory proteins. Overall, our findings highlight the power of enhanced sampling as a lens to capture the dynamic interplay between ligands and disordered proteins. The growing body of research on complex biological systems continues to emphasize the importance of atomistic simulations and statistical approaches in advancing biophysics. We believe that combining machine learning with enhanced sampling provides a natural and effective paradigm for exploring the conformational dynamics of IDPs and guiding the rational design of next-generation therapeutics.


## Methods
  All MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022.685, integrated with the PLUMED 2.9.086 plugin and PyTorch library 2.2.187. The initial conformation of Tau-5 (residues 341–446) in the AR-NTD was predicted using AlphaFold255, with ACE and NME capping the termini. The a99SB-disp protein force field56 was used for the Tau-5 protein, the a99SB-disp water model56 was used for water molecules, and the GAFF force field88 with RESP charges89 was applied to characterize EPI-002. The steepest descent minimization algorithm was used for energy minimization. The time step was set to 2 fs. Bond lengths and angles for protein and ligand were constrained using the LINCS90 algorithm, while water constraints were applied using the SETTLE91 algorithm. The temperature was maintained at 300 K under the NVT ensemble using the velocity rescale thermostat92 with a time constant of 2000 ps. The pressure was set to 1 bar under the NPT ensemble using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat93, and the velocity rescale thermostat92 was used to control the temperature at 300 K with a time constant of 2000 ps. During the equilibration process, position restraints were applied to all protein-heavy atoms. The PME algorithm was used for electrostatics with a grid spacing of 1.2 nm. A cutoff length of 1.2 nm was used to calculate van der Waals interactions. The box for the REST3 simulation of Tau-5 is a cube with a side length of 12.5 nm, while the boxes for the other simulations are cubes with a side length of 10 nm. The boxes were solvated with water molecules, and sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the charge. All ML-CV training and analysis were performed using the mlcolvar46 library.


### Simulations of the Tau-5 protein

#### REST3 simulations
  The REST3 algorithm43 was implemented for microsecond-scale simulations, with replica exchanges occurring every 2 ps. All protein atoms were selected as the solute region. The solute temperature was scaled using 8 replicas within the 300–450 K range. Each replica was simulated for 4.1 µs, yielding a cumulative simulation time of 32.2 µs. Further details of the parameter settings for the REST3 implementation are provided in Supplementary Table 1.


#### Blocking error analysis
  Statistical errors for the REST3 simulations were analyzed using blocking analysis, with optimal block size determined as described in the previous study58. The analysis was performed using the pyplock Python package. In this process, the MD trajectory is divided into a given number of equally sized “blocks”. For each block, time-averaged values of observables are computed. The standard error of these block-averaged values across all blocks is then calculated and adopted as the statistical error estimate.


#### Maximum-entropy ensemble reweighting
  The NMR chemical shifts of the 300 K replica from the REST3 simulations of Tau-5 were calculated using SPARTA +62. A maximum entropy reweighting algorithm64,65 was applied with Cα NMR chemical shifts as constraints. The Cα NMR chemical shift predictions were performed using a Gaussian error model with a standard deviation of θ = 1.73 ppm.

#### Design of AE distance CV for Tau-5
  To analyze the FES of the Tau-5 protein trajectory, AE CVs28,29 were employed to capture its complex dynamic transitions. From the 300 K REST3 trajectory, the Cα–Cα distance between R1 and R2, R2 and R3, and R1 and R3 within Tau-5 were selected as descriptors, generating 1899 Cα–Cα distance pairs that served as the input features for training the MLCVs. The neural network architecture comprised seven layers with 1899, 64, 64, 2, 64, 64, and 1899 nodes, with the shifted softplus as an activation function. The training was performed using the ADAM optimizer94 with a learning rate of 1.0 × 10−4 and a mean squared error (MSE) loss function. Following the training process, the bottleneck layer of the AE was extracted and used as the CV.


#### SWISH Simulations
  For each metastable conformation, we performed SWISH45 simulations following a standardized protocol. Each system comprised four parallel replicas with a scaling factor (λ) uniformly distributed between 1.00 and 1.15, simulating 50 ns for each replica. Benzene at a concentration of 1 M was used as a cosolvent, with both the benzene molecules and the protein system thermally coupled to the same temperature group. By visually inspecting the trajectory at λ = 1.0, we identified a conformation for each metastable state where the binding pocket was opened.


#### Molecular docking
  The Protein Preparation Workflow module in Schrödinger was utilized to optimize any unreasonable regions in the protein structure. The ligand EPI-002 was prepared using the LigPrep module. Glide66,67 was employed to dock EPI-002 into the binding pockets of each metastable conformation. Then, the Covalent Docking module68,69 in Schrödinger’s was used to perform covalent docking of EPI-002 into each metastable conformation. The reaction type was specified as a nucleophilic substitution, with Cys404 designated as the reactive residue.


### Simulations of the Tau-5 protein complexed with the EPI-002 ligand

#### Design of multitask water CV
  Our neural network architecture comprises a four-layer topology with 64, 16, 4, and 1 nodes. All hidden layers employ the shiftplus activation function. The descriptors consist of a set of water coordination numbers ({G}, {H}, {Vi}). We performed 30 ns unbiased simulations for both the bound and unbound states to generate the labeled data, and used OPES explore16 to sample the binding and unbinding times once as the unlabeled data. In the auxiliary TDA loss function27 of the multitask CV46, the target means and width of distributions corresponding to the two states (metastable A, B) were selected as μA = − 10.00, μB = 10.0, and σA = 1.0, σB = 1.0. The weight factor of the auxiliary TDA loss was set as 1.0. The training was performed with the ADAM optimizer94 with a learning rate of 1.0 × 10−5.

The funnel geometry confines the ligand to a cylindrical volume above the binding site in the dissociated state. As the ligand approaches the binding site, the funnel constraint gradually broadens to avoid obstructing binding events. The parameters defining the shape of the funnel constraint are provided in the PLUMED file.

#### OPES Explore simulations
  For each EPI-002/Tau-5 metastable conformation, three independent OPES explore16 simulations were carried out: 300 ns for conformations 2 and 6, 160 ns for conformation 9, and 200 ns for the rest. #### Design of Deep-TICA water CV and Deep-TICA distance CV
  The Deep-TICA training strategy follows the protocol proposed by Bonati et al.30, implementing a neural network architecture with four sequential layers utilizing the ReLU activation function. For the Water CV, the descriptors include a set of water coordination numbers ({G}, {H}, {Vi}), as described in the Results section. The Distance CV employs a set of ligand-protein interatomic distances, also detailed in the Results section. The training trajectories were derived from the OPES explore simulations. We employed the ADAM optimizer94 with a learning rate of 1.0 × 10⁻⁴. The loss function incorporated two TICA eigenvalues. The Deep-TICA model was trained with a lag time of 1, and the resulting CVs were used to analyze the FES of the OPES to explore biased trajectories.


### Virtual screening and activity validation

#### Virtual screening
  The nine metastable conformations of Tau-5 were used to generate a receptor network using the receptor network generation module in Schrödinger. Virtual screening against the Specs database was performed using the Glide66,67 docking program. Initially, ligands were prepared using the ligPrep module in Schrödinger, generating all possible protonation states at pH = 7.0 ± 2.0 for two compound libraries: Specs and Chemdiv. For the Specs database, the ligand dataset was screened using the Glide SP scoring, and the top 20,000 compounds were redocked using the Glide XP scoring for higher accuracy. These compounds were then ranked based on their docking scores. The top 2000 compounds (top 1%) were retained. If a compound ranked in the top 1% for more than four conformations, it was kept, resulting in 215 compounds. These compounds underwent drug-likeness filtering using the ADMET95–97 module of Drugflow98 and were clustered into 10 groups using the linkage clustering algorithm. A representative subset of compounds from each group was selected, resulting in a final set of 40 compounds. For the ChemDiv database, the top 200,000 compounds were initially selected using HTVS, followed by Glide SP screening to retain the top 20,000 compounds, and then redocked with Glide XP for higher precision. If a compound was ranked in the top 1‰ for more than three conformations, it was kept, resulting in a total of 161 compounds. These were filtered for drug-likeness using the ADMET95–97 module of Drugflow98, and clustered into 10 groups using the linkage clustering algorithm. Representative compounds were selected from the clustering results, yielding a final set of 21 candidates. In total, 61 compounds were identified from both libraries, of which 58 were purchased, excluding those unavailable from suppliers.


#### Cell culture and reagents
  LNCaP, 22Rv1, LNCaP-ARR2PB-eGFP, LNCaP-EnzR, NIH-3T3 and WPMY-1 cell lines were used in this study. The LNCaP-EnzR cell line was previously established and characterized by our group99. The LNCaP-ARR2PB-eGFP reporter cell line, used to assess AR transcriptional activity, was constructed and validated as previously described by stably introducing an androgen-responsive ARR2PB promoter driving the expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP)79. WPMY-1 and NIH-3T3 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All prostate cancer cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) was purchased from Solabio, and Enz from MedChemExpress; both were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare stock solutions. A library of 58 structurally diverse candidate compounds for screening was purchased from TargetMol and prepared in DMSO at a final stock concentration of 20 mM for subsequent in vitro assays.


#### MTT Assay for cell proliferation
  Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT colorimetric assay. LNCaP, 22Rv1, LNCaP-EnzR, NIH-3T3, and WPMY-1 cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 3000 to 5000 cells per well and incubated overnight to allow for cell attachment. The following day, cells were exposed to gradient concentrations of K53 or Enz for 72 hours, with LNCaP cells subjected to an extended 120 h treatment. At the end of the incubation period, 10 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to allow for formazan formation. After incubation, the medium was aspirated, and 100 μL of a lysis solution containing 10% SDS and 0.1% HCl in PBS was added to solubilize the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek).


#### AR Transcriptional activity assay
  The AR transcriptional activity was evaluated using the LNCaP-ARR2PB-eGFP reporter cell line. Prior to compound treatment, cells were cultured in phenol red-free RPMI-1640 medium containing 5% char-coal-stripped serum (CSS) for five days to deplete endogenous androgens. Cells were then seeded into black-wall 96-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Subsequently, cells were treated with various concentrations of test compounds. 5 nM DHT was co-administered to activate AR signaling. After 72 h of treatment, eGFP fluorescence was measured using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 nm and 535 nm, respectively. Relative fluorescence intensity was used as a readout of AR transcriptional activity.


#### SPR Binding assay
  Experiments were performed on a Biacore 8K system (GE Healthcare) using a CM5 sensor chip (#29104998; Cytiva). Recombinant AR-NTD-AF1 protein (Suzhou PLD Technology Co., Ltd.) was immobilized on the CM5 chip via standard amine coupling chemistry in PBST buffer (10 mM PBS containing 0.01% (v/v) P20 surfactant, pH 7.4). All measurements were performed at 25 °C. The analytes (K53, enzalutamide, and EPI-002) were injected at a flow rate of 30 μL/min with contact and dissociation times of 120 s and 60 s, respectively. Each analyte was tested at a series of concentrations (3.125–50 μM, two-fold serial dilution). A reference flow cell without immobilized protein was used, and data were processed using double referencing (reference surface subtraction and blank injection subtraction). Binding responses were recorded in multi-cycle kinetics mode. Data were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation software (v5.0.18). Sensorgrams were fitted using a steady-state affinity model to derive KD.


#### Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
  LNCaP cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes and cultured in complete medium for 24 h, followed by treatment with the indicated compound at 30 µM or DMSO as a control for 1 h. Cells were then detached, resuspended in PBS, and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and a 25 °C water bath. Proteins were collected by high-speed centrifugation. The resulting protein samples were heated at the indicated temperatures for 3 min in a PCR thermocycler, followed by cooling to room temperature for 3 min, and centrifuged again to isolate the soluble fraction for subsequent Western blot analysis. Band intensities were quantified by Western blotting (relative to β-actin) and normalized to the intensity of the 43 °C sample. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM of duplicate samples.


#### Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
  ¹H NMR spectra of small molecules, either alone or in the presence of purified protein, were recorded on a Bruker 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a CP2.1 QCI 600S3 probe. Standard ¹H experiments were acquired with 32 scans in a total volume of 500 µL containing 500 µM small molecule, either alone or in combination with 5 µM protein, in 20 mM phosphate buffer prepared in 100% D₂O. WaterLOGSY experiments were performed using the ephogsygpno.2 pulse sequence with 128 scans in a total volume of 500 µL containing 500 µM small molecule, either alone or with 5 µM protein, in 20 mM phosphate buffer prepared in 90% H₂O/10% D₂O. All spectra were processed using TopSpin 4.5 with standard parameters.


#### Fluorescence polarization
  Fluorescence polarization measurements were performed using the PolarScreen Androgen Receptor Competitor Assay Kit (#A15880; Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol.


#### Nuclear receptor cross-reactivity analysis
  To evaluate potential cross-reactivity with other steroid nuclear receptors, transcriptional reporter assays were performed for MR, PR, and GR. For MR and PR assays, HEK293T cells were plated into white 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in DMEM supplemented with 5% CSS. Cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding MR or PR (70 ng), an MMTV-driven firefly luciferase reporter (24 ng), and a Renilla luciferase normalization construct (7 ng). After 12 h of transfection, cells were exposed to 100 nM aldosterone (for MR) or 100 nM progesterone (for PR) in the presence of the indicated concentrations of test compounds. Dual-luciferase signals were quantified using the Dual-Lumi™ Luciferase Assay Kit (#RG088M; Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For the GR transcriptional assay, HeLa-MMTV-Luc reporter cells were starved in white 96-well plates with DMEM with 5% CSS serum for hormone deprivation. Cells were then treated with 10 nM dexamethasone together with the indicated concentrations of compounds for 24 h. Firefly luciferase activity was measured using the Firefly Luciferase Assay Kit (#RG055M; Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

### Application of the workflow to the c-Myc system
  The computational protocol employed for the c-Myc system closely followed that established for Tau-5. Accordingly, only methodological differences specific to the c-Myc setup are described below.


#### Initial structure construction
  The initial structure of c-Myc was generated using the tleap module of the Amber100 software package. The structure was first energy-minimized and then subjected to a short high-temperature unfolding simulation of 100 ps at 600 K in vacuo under the NVT ensemble. A representative structure was selected from the unfolding trajectory and used as the starting structure for subsequent simulations.


#### Design of AE distance CV for c-Myc
  From the 300 K REST3 trajectory, all Cα–Cα distances within the c-Myc protein were selected as descriptors, generating 3570 Cα–Cα distance pairs that served as the input features for training the MLCVs. The neural network architecture comprised seven layers with 3570, 64, 64, 2, 64, 64, and 3570 nodes, respectively, employing the shifted softplus activation function.
