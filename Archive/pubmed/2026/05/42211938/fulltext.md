# A unified mechanistic perspective on protein–polysaccharide interactions underlying hybrid gel formation: A mini review


## Abstract
  Hybrid protein–polysaccharide gels are an emerging class of soft materials with versatile applications in food science, pharmaceuticals, and biomedical engineering. By combining the functional attributes of proteins with the structural and hydration properties of polysaccharides, these gels exhibit tunable mechanical strength, viscoelasticity, and stimuli-responsive behaviors. Despite extensive studies on their macroscopic properties, the molecular mechanisms driving gelation remain incompletely understood. This mini review presents a unified mechanistic perspective, linking molecular interactions, mesoscale phase behavior, and macroscopic network formation. Early-stage complexation is governed by electrostatic interactions modulated by charge patchiness, counterion-release entropy, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, van der Waals interactions, and covalent crosslinking. Such associations often trigger liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), forming coacervate droplets that serve as precursors for cluster aggregation. Droplet growth and coalescence eventually yield percolated three-dimensional networks, which determine gel mechanics and rheology. Environmental and formulation factors—including pH, ionic strength, protein-to-polysaccharide ratio, temperature, and mechanical stress—strongly influence each stage of assembly, enabling precise tuning of gel microstructure and function. Advanced experimental and computational tools, such as spectroscopy, scattering methods, imaging, and multiscale simulations, provide mechanistic insight across scales. By integrating these perspectives into a predictive framework, this review highlights strategies for designing mechanically robust and stimuli-responsive gels for food and biomedical applications. Remaining challenges include dynamic characterization, LLPS kinetics, network aging, and nanoscale binding pathways, offering directions for future research in next-generation functional materials.


## Introduction
  Hybrid protein–polysaccharide gels represent a rapidly advancing class of soft matter materials that integrate the functional versatility of proteins with the structural and hydration properties of polysaccharides (Guo et al., 2024; Nath et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024). Proteins contribute bioactivity, enzymatic responsiveness, and directional non-covalent interactions, whereas polysaccharides provide mechanical stability, modulate water retention, and influence macromolecular transport within the gel network (Farahani et al., 2025a). The synergistic combination allows the design of hybrid gels with properties superior to single-component systems, including enhanced elasticity, tunable viscoelasticity, and controlled diffusion (Li et al., 2023; Yiasmin et al., 2024a).

  Over the past two decades, extensive research has explored protein–polysaccharide interactions. Early studies focused on simple complex coacervation and the formation of electrostatic assemblies (Guo et al., 2024), while later works characterized mesoscale structures, phase behavior, and rheology of hybrid gels (Ye et al., 2024; Zhu and Jiang, 2022). Despite these advances, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing hierarchical gelation remains limited (Khoshdouni et al., 2024a). In particular, the interplay between early complexation, LLPS, cluster aggregation, and network percolation, as well as the modulation of these processes by environmental factors, is not fully elucidated (Timilsena et al., 2019).

  A key intermediate in gel formation is liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), where protein–polysaccharide complexes condense into dense coacervate droplets. These droplets act as mesoscale precursors, concentrating macromolecules and promoting cluster aggregation. Subsequent coalescence and percolation of droplets form continuous three-dimensional networks, which define the gel's mechanical and functional properties (Yiasmin et al., 2024a). Understanding LLPS kinetics, droplet maturation, and the influence of environmental conditions such as pH, ionic strength, temperature, protein-to-polysaccharide ratio, and mechanical stimuli is crucial for predictive gel design (Timilsena et al., 2019).

  Despite the growing body of experimental and theoretical work (Khoshdouni et al., 2025), critical gaps remain, particularly regarding the nanoscale binding pathways, ion-specific effects, aging phenomena, and multiscale integration from molecular to macroscopic levels. Developing predictive frameworks that couple molecular simulations, mesoscale modeling, and rheological characterization is essential for rational design. Such approaches can enable programmable, stimuli-responsive gels with tailored mechanical strength, controlled porosity, and specific functional behaviors (Zhu and Jiang, 2022).

  This mini-review provides a mechanistic and multiscale perspective on protein–polysaccharide gelation. It first examines molecular interactions driving early complexation, then explores LLPS as a bridge to network formation, followed by cluster aggregation and percolation into continuous networks. Environmental modulation, predictive modeling, and future research opportunities are discussed. By integrating historical context, recent advances, and mechanistic insights, this review aims to guide the rational design of next-generation hybrid gels for applications in food technology, pharmaceuticals, and biomedicine (Guo et al., 2024; Nath et al., 2022). Unlike previous reviews, this work presents a unified hierarchical framework linking molecular interactions, LLPS, cluster aggregation, and network percolation to macroscopic gel mechanics. It explicitly emphasizes how environmental and formulation parameters modulate each stage of assembly. Furthermore, this review provides a predictive, pathway-aware perspective, integrating experimental and computational approaches to guide the rational design of functional protein–polysaccharide gels.

### Electrostatics and charge patchiness as Organizational drivers
  Electrostatic attraction is the primary initiator of complexation, but directional binding arises from protein charge anisotropy (charge patchiness). Quantitative thermodynamic studies using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) on food-relevant systems like gelatin–pectin have reported measurable thermodynamic parameters including binding constants (Ka), Gibbs free energy (ΔG), enthalpy (ΔH), and entropy (ΔS). For example, gelatin–pectin complex coacervation at pH 3.5 is spontaneous and entropy-driven with negative ΔG and positive ΔS, whereas increased ionic strength shifts the process toward greater enthalpic contributions, demonstrating how electrostatic and hydrogen bonding vary quantitatively with formulation conditions (Guo et al., 2024).

  This heterogeneity promotes localized binding, biasing assembly toward either soluble complexes or phase-separated structures. Environmental factors strongly influence this process: near the protein's isoelectric point (pI), reduced net charge facilitates aggregation, whereas high ionic strength screens electrostatic interactions, producing weaker or transient associations (Alrosan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2015).


### Counterion-release entropy and thermodynamic directionality
  Binding of oppositely charged macromolecules releases condensed counterions into the solution, generating substantial entropy gain that stabilizes the complex (Turgeon et al., 2003).This effect is particularly pronounced with multivalent ions and provides a thermodynamic driving force that explains why some systems undergo coacervation rather than forming homogeneous gels (Li et al., 2023). In addition to qualitative descriptions, quantitative calorimetric analyses of protein–polysaccharide systems (e.g., whey protein isolate with propylene glycol alginate) confirm that both positive enthalpy (ΔH) and positive entropy (ΔS) contribute significantly to coacervation, indicating that electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding make measurable thermodynamic contributions under controlled pH and concentration conditions. Interpreting these parameters in terms of energetics helps link molecular driving forces with macroscopic gelation behavior.


### Hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding as stabilizing modulators
  Partial protein unfolding exposes hydrophobic residues that associate with polysaccharide domains, promoting cluster densification (Khoshdouni et al., 2025). Concurrently, hydrogen bonds between protein and polysaccharide reinforce intra- and inter-cluster connectivity, influencing whether early complexes mature into continuous gels or remain kinetically trapped aggregates (Guo et al., 2024; Alrosan et al., 2025).


### Weak interactions and permanent network fixation
  van der Waals forces cumulatively strengthen coacervate droplets and prevent coalescence during LLPS. At later stages, covalent crosslinking—via enzymatic transglutamination or Maillard-type reactions—irreversibly stabilizes the network, enhancing mechanical robustness and long-term gel stability (Zheng et al., 2024a).


### Hierarchical integration and design implications
  Early gelation follows a hierarchical sequence: electrostatics initiate association, counterion release provides thermodynamic directionality, hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds stabilize clusters, weak forces reinforce droplets, and covalent crosslinks consolidate the network. The dominance of each interaction is context-dependent, governed by pH, ionic strength, temperature, and macromolecular architecture. Recognizing this hierarchy allows rational tuning of gelation pathways toward desired structural and functional outcomes (Gentile, 2020).


## Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) as a mechanistic bridge
  After initial complexation, protein–polysaccharide systems often undergo liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), producing polymer-rich coacervate droplets that act as dynamic precursors to percolated gel networks (Alrosan et al., 2025). LLPS links molecular interactions to macroscopic gelation (Farahani et al., 2025b), driven by electrostatic neutralization, multivalent interactions, and counterion-release entropy. These forces concentrate proteins and polysaccharides into dense mesoscale domains, enhancing local interaction frequency and facilitating droplet coalescence, network percolation, or arrested phase separation (Li et al., 2023).


### Microphase versus macrophase separation
  LLPS can occur as microphase or macrophase separation. Microphase separation forms nanoscale droplets (∼10–200 nm) that remain uniformly dispersed, favoring homogeneous networks, reproducible mechanical properties, and narrow mesh distributions, suitable for optical clarity and structural uniformity (Turgeon et al., 2003). Macrophase separation produces micron-sized domains and compositional heterogeneity, leading to spatially variable stiffness and weaker networks prone to fracture (He et al., 2021). The balance between these states depends on protein–polysaccharide ratio, pH, ionic strength, temperature, and ion valency. High charge density and flexible polymer chains favor microphase separation, emphasizing the role of molecular architecture (Guo et al., 2024; Alrosan et al., 2025). Different LLPS pathways can occur depending on molecular compatibility, including segregative, associative, and complex associative phase separations (Fig. 1). ### Droplet formation and maturation
  In this section and the following, we introduce and define key concepts relevant to protein–polysaccharide gel assembly, including LLPS droplets, aggregated clusters, percolated networks, diffusion-versus reaction-limited cluster aggregation (DLCA vs RLCA), environmental factors influencing droplet formation, and gel aging processes such as syneresis and bond reorganization. Droplet formation in food emulsions generally involves processes such as nucleation, aggregation, coalescence, and Ostwald ripening, as widely reported in the literature (Guo et al., 2024). Similar LLPS-driven aggregation has been observed in peptide systems (Yuan et al., 2023). Unlike peptide LLPS, which is primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, protein-polysaccharide coacervation is dominated by electrostatic interactions and charge complementarity. Droplet stability and maturation kinetics also differ, with polysaccharide-containing droplets exhibiting enhanced viscosity and chain flexibility effects that influence cluster formation and network percolation. Heterogeneous droplet populations emerge even under uniform conditions, affecting network connectivity and mechanical anisotropy. Droplet maturation involves internal restructuring driven by interfacial tension, electrostatic frustration, and viscoelastic relaxation. Shape deviations such as elongation or irregularity facilitate droplet interpenetration, physical bridging, and formation of a percolated pre-gel network (Khoshdouni et al., 2024b). Maturation, rather than size alone, dictates final cluster density, connectivity, and mechanical stiffness (Table 2) (Ye et al., 2024). Here, we distinguish between LLPS droplets, aggregated clusters, and percolated networks. LLPS droplets are dynamic, liquid-like condensates formed via phase separation, whereas aggregated clusters are kinetically trapped assemblies of partially desolvated macromolecules. Percolated networks arise when clusters connect to span the system, forming a continuous gel structure. ### LLPS kinetics and environmental control
  LLPS kinetics balance thermodynamic driving forces with kinetic constraints, including supersaturation, affinity, and temperature. Rapid nucleation generates many small droplets that may arrest, forming brittle networks, whereas slower growth favors fewer, larger droplets, enhancing interconnection and network robustness (Guo et al., 2024). Environmental factors—pH, ionic strength, temperature—and mechanical forces modulate droplet nucleation, size, stability, and alignment. Recent advances in confocal microscopy, DLS, and microfluidics allow real-time observation of LLPS and network evolution, highlighting the sensitivity of gel microstructure to subtle kinetic or environmental perturbations (Cai et al., 2025). Understanding these dynamics enables rational tuning of droplet size, network connectivity, mechanical properties, and functional heterogeneity for tailored gel design. The kinetic pathway of cluster aggregation—whether diffusion-limited cluster aggregation (DLCA) or reaction-limited cluster aggregation (RLCA)—strongly influences the final network structure, connectivity, and mechanical properties. Networks formed via DLCA tend to be more open and fractal-like, whereas RLCA produces denser, more homogeneous gels. Additionally, long-term aging can lead to syneresis, bond reorganization, or coarsening of the network, affecting gel stability and rheological behavior. Including these considerations provides a more complete mechanistic picture of protein-polysaccharide gel maturation. These kinetic considerations are particularly relevant for controlling texture, stability, and functional performance in food gels (Yuan et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025).


## From droplets to percolated gel networks
  Once protein–polysaccharide droplets form via LLPS, the system undergoes hierarchical mesoscale reorganizations in which droplets aggregate into clusters and progressively interconnect to form a continuous, percolated network. This assembly underlies macroscopic mechanical properties such as elasticity, viscoelastic recovery, and shear resistance that define gel functionality. The transition from discrete droplets to a coherent network is controlled not only by thermodynamic phase separation but also by aggregation kinetics and mesoscale connectivity. Cluster size, topology, and inter-cluster junctions govern the percolation threshold, network heterogeneity, and load-bearing capacity, meaning gels with similar compositions can exhibit markedly different mechanical behavior depending on their assembly pathway (Alrosan et al., 2025).

### Percolation thresholds as a determinant of functional gelation
  A critical transition in gel formation is the percolation threshold, marking the emergence of a system-spanning network from disconnected clusters. This threshold is sensitive to formulation and environmental conditions. Near-stoichiometric protein–polysaccharide ratios enhance intermolecular connectivity and minimize trapped aggregates, whereas deviations reduce network integrity (Ye et al., 2024). Droplet or cluster density influences collision frequency, lowering the percolation threshold at higher densities. Environmental factors, including pH, ionic strength, and temperature, modulate aggregation kinetics and the percolation boundary. Crossing this threshold is a functional transition, enabling elasticity, stress recovery, and viscoelastic dissipation, which are crucial for biomedical scaffolds, structured foods, and soft functional materials (Guo et al., 2024).


### Network topology and multiscale architecture
  Gel mechanics are governed by multiscale network architecture spanning nano-, meso-, and macroscales. At the nanoscale, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and van der Waals forces establish primary connectivity. Mesoscale cluster aggregation, droplet coalescence, and partial restructuring introduce heterogeneity, producing fractal or semi-fractal architectures. At the macroscale, these features integrate into a continuous percolated network, imparting bulk mechanical properties. Network topology parameters—branching density, pore size distribution, and crosslink heterogeneity—mediate trade-offs between elasticity, stiffness, and permeability. Highly branched networks enhance elastic recovery but reduce load-bearing stiffness, whereas dense RLCA-like networks increase rigidity at the cost of transport properties. Advanced techniques such as confocal laser scanning microscopy, atomic force microscopy, and oscillatory rheometry link mesoscale structure to macroscopic rheology, enabling predictive correlations between assembly pathway and gel mechanics (Cai et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025).


### Conceptual mechanistic framework for hierarchical assembly
  Protein–polysaccharide gelation can be conceptualized as a hierarchical sequence: LLPS → Droplet Formation → Cluster Aggregation → Percolation → Emergent Mechanics. Early-stage molecular interactions propagate through successive structural levels, determining macroscopic mechanical behavior. Gel properties (Farahani et al., 2026) are encoded throughout the assembly pathway, not solely at the final network stage. From a design perspective, this mechanistic framework allows tuning gel performance by modulating parameters at each stage, such as phase separation conditions, droplet coalescence, or aggregation arrest near the percolation threshold. Pathway-aware design strategies enable programmable gels with tailored elasticity, porosity, and responsiveness, moving beyond empirical formulation and positioning protein–polysaccharide gels as advanced functional soft materials (Ye et al., 2024; Alrosan et al., 2025).


## Environmental and formulation controls over gelation
  Protein–polysaccharide gelation is controlled by the interplay between environmental conditions and formulation parameters, which together regulate molecular interactions, LLPS, cluster aggregation, and network formation. These factors are strongly coupled, such that small molecular-scale changes can propagate through hierarchical assembly and substantially alter macroscopic mechanical and functional properties. Understanding these couplings is essential for rational design of gels with predictable behavior (Guo et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025).


### pH and ionic strength as electrostatic regulators
  pH dictates the charge state of proteins, influencing electrostatic attraction and repulsion. Near the protein's isoelectric point (pI), reduced net charge weakens repulsion, facilitating cluster growth and network formation, whereas pH far from the pI promotes extended complexes, solubility, and may suppress LLPS (Shibaev et al., 2021). Ionic strength modulates these effects through Debye screening: low salt favors long-range electrostatics and dense droplet formation, accelerating percolation, while high salt screens attractions, delaying or inhibiting gelation. Multivalent cations (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+) can transiently bridge charged moieties, enhancing connectivity, stiffness, and relaxation dynamics (Turgeon et al., 2003). Together, pH and ionic strength provide continuous, tunable control over gel structure and mechanics.


### Protein–polysaccharide ratio and network architecture
  The relative ratio of protein to polysaccharide governs network topology and mechanical performance. Near-stoichiometric ratios maximize connectivity and yield robust gels. Protein-rich systems form dense networks with smaller pores, increasing stiffness and elasticity, while polysaccharide-rich gels are more hydrated and porous but mechanically weaker. Deviations from the optimal ratio often produce incomplete or heterogeneous networks, emphasizing that gelation depends not just on total concentration but on the balance between interacting species (Yiasmin et al., 2024a; Zhou et al., 2025).


### Thermal and mechanical stimuli as dynamic modulators
  Temperature affects protein stability, polysaccharide flexibility, and LLPS kinetics. Moderate heating can promote partial unfolding, exposing hydrophobic domains that enhance droplet cohesion and cluster connectivity, while excessive heat can cause irreversible aggregation and network collapse. Mechanical forces such as shear influence droplet alignment, cluster orientation, and collision frequency. Controlled shear promotes homogeneous network formation, whereas excessive stress can disrupt droplet integrity, producing anisotropic or defective gels (Cai et al., 2025).


### Integrative environmental tuning for rational gel design
  Collectively, pH, ionic strength, protein–polysaccharide ratio, and thermal/mechanical inputs define a multidimensional design space for gel tuning. By navigating this space, researchers can control network density, pore size, droplet maturation, mechanical properties, water retention, and molecular diffusion. This integrative approach enables mechanism-driven, rational gel design, supporting applications in food structuring, drug delivery, tissue engineering, and wound healing (Guo et al., 2024; Chen and Wang, 2022).


## Toward a predictive framework and future perspectives
  Developing a predictive framework that quantitatively links molecular-scale interactions to macroscopic gel mechanics remains a central challenge in protein–polysaccharide systems (Zheng et al., 2024b; Yiasmin et al., 2024b). Although significant progress has been made in understanding electrostatic complexation, LLPS, and rheology, these insights remain fragmented. An integrated, multiscale model is needed to capture the dynamic, hierarchical, and non-equilibrium nature of gel formation, where early molecular interactions constrain later network architecture and emergent mechanical properties (Fig. 3) (Guo et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025).Fig. 3Simplified multiscale framework linking molecular interactions, network formation, and gel properties. ### Multiscale mechanistic modeling
  At the molecular level, atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Widmer and Stelling, 2018) reveal binding modes, charge distributions, hydrogen-bond lifetimes, and counterion effects. For instance, carboxyl-rich polysaccharides such as pectin interact with positively charged protein domains, and counterion release contributes significantly to the free energy landscape (Zheng et al., 2024a; Chen and Wang, 2022). While MD provides mechanistic detail, its computational cost limits direct application to large assemblies.

  Mesoscale phenomena, including LLPS (Chen et al., 2025), droplet growth, and cluster compaction, are captured using coarse-grained simulations, field-theoretic approaches, and Monte Carlo models. These methods reproduce experimentally observed phase boundaries, droplet coalescence, and percolation kinetics (Li et al., 2018; Cai et al., 2025). At the macroscopic scale, constitutive rheological models such as generalized Maxwell, fractional Kelvin–Voigt, and yield-stress formulations describe relaxation spectra, elasticity, and creep recovery (Zhou et al., 2025), though they rarely incorporate molecular detail. Integrating molecular and mesoscale descriptors with continuum rheology offers a path toward predictive structure–property relationships. Emerging machine-learning approaches can accelerate this process while preserving interpretability.


### Current challenges and knowledge gaps
  Key gaps remain in understanding LLPS nucleation under crowded conditions, where multivalent interactions dominate classical nucleation theory. Ion-specific effects of multivalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe3+) further complicate network formation by inducing bridging or partial crosslinking, shifting LLPS boundaries and modifying stiffness (Guo et al., 2024). Aging and long-term network evolution, driven by bond rearrangements and cluster coalescence, also affect mechanical stability, syneresis, and porosity. Single-molecule techniques, including AFM force spectroscopy and optical tweezers, provide crucial nanometer-scale insight into unbinding forces and kinetics, bridging atomistic and bulk behaviors (Zheng et al., 2024a; Zhou et al., 2025).


### Practical applications and design opportunities
  A predictive framework would transform gel design across applications. In food systems, it would allow control over texture, water retention, and structural stability, enabling low-fat and plant-based products with optimized sensory properties (Cai et al., 2025). In pharmaceuticals, gel architecture could be tuned for sustained or rapid drug release by manipulating RLCA-versus DLCA-like networks (Guo et al., 2024). Tissue engineering would benefit from gels with hierarchical porosity and tunable stiffness, supporting cell infiltration, adhesion, and differentiation (Zhou et al., 2025). Stimuli-responsive gels leveraging reversible LLPS and crosslinking could enable smart packaging, sensors, and on-demand therapeutic delivery (Guo et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025).

## Conclusions and perspectives
  Hybrid protein–polysaccharide gels are unique soft materials whose properties arise from hierarchical assembly driven by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic forces, van der Waals attractions, and covalent crosslinking. Initial complexation triggers LLPS, forming coacervate droplets that mature into clusters and eventually a percolated 3D network. Environmental factors like pH, ionic strength, temperature, protein–polysaccharide ratio, and mixing critically influence droplet size, cluster morphology, network architecture, and macroscopic mechanics, highlighting the tunability of these systems.

  Key challenges remain in understanding LLPS kinetics, ion-specific effects, aging, and nanoscale binding pathways. Predictive, multiscale frameworks combining molecular simulations, mesoscale modeling, and rheology are needed to enable rational design of gels with tailored mechanics, porosity, and stimuli-responsiveness. Such insights open applications in food structuring, pharmaceuticals, and tissue engineering, supporting targeted drug delivery, bioprinting, and functional foods. Overall, these gels offer a versatile platform for both fundamental research and technological innovation. This review uniquely contributes by integrating molecular-to-macroscopic perspectives into a single mechanistic roadmap, highlighting the interplay of early complexation, LLPS, droplet aggregation, and network percolation. It clarifies how modulation of environmental and formulation variables can be systematically leveraged for designing gels with predictable mechanical and functional properties, advancing beyond prior literature.
