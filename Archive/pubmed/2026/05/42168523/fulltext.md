# Deciphering the role of tubulin’s C-terminal tail in kinesin binding using computational and clustering approaches


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54092-0.


## Introduction
  Microtubules, composed of α- and β-tubulin heterodimers, and kinesin motors form a critical partnership that facilitates intracellular cargo transport, mitotic progression, and cytoskeletal organization1,2. These highly regulated interactions are essential for normal cellular function, and their dysregulation can lead to a variety of diseases3–6.

  The carboxy-terminal tails (CTTs) of α- and β-tubulin are intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) that extend outward from the microtubule surface7. Their high content of glutamate and aspartate residues contributes to a substantial negative charge to the tubulins interface, which is beneficial for interactions with kinesin7–10. Biochemical experiments have shown that the cleavage of CTTs decreases the processivity and velocity of kinesin movement along microtubules11,12. Computational models further support the crucial role of CTTs in kinesin motility, suggesting that these regions may guide kinesin motion by providing a weak tethering13–15. However, little attention is paid to the specific role and mechanistic details of the CTTs on the binding process between kinesin and microtubules.

  The high motor ability of most kinesins limits discovery of the binding process between kinesin and microtubules. Kinesin family member 7 (KIF7) is a non-canonical kinesin, which has a defect in chemomechanical coupling that prevents it from moving in microtubules and have high binding ability to microtubules. KIF7 acts as a microtubule tip tracker and a signaling scaffold within the primary cilium, where it regulates the Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway by controlling the trafficking and processing of GLI2 and GLI3 transcription factor16,17. KIF7 is essential for embryonic patterning, including neural tube specification, limb development, and cerebral corticogenesis18. Since KIF7 plays important roles in embryonic development, embryonic related tubulins are suitable for discovering the binding features with KIF7.

  Among the multiple α- and β-tubulin isoforms expressed in human cells, β-tubulin 8 (TUBB8) is primate-specific and predominantly expressed in oocytes and early embryos, where it serves as the principal β-tubulin subunit for meiotic spindle assembly19,20. Mutations in TUBB8 are now recognized as the most common monogenic cause of human oocyte maturation arrest, leading to female infertility characterized by metaphase I arrest, fertilization failure, and early embryonic cleavage arrest20,21. Likewise, α-tubulin 4A (TUBA4A) is one of the most abundantly expressed α-tubulin isoforms in human oocytes and functions as the primary dimerization partner for TUBB8 during spindle microtubule polymerization22. Recent clinical and functional studies have demonstrated that TUBA4A mutations cause human zygotic arrest and early developmental failure by disrupting oocyte spindle morphology and chromosome alignment22.

  Thus, the KIF7 motor domain (the head of KIF7) and TUBB8–TUBA4A heterodimer, which represent the predominant structural unit of the human oocyte meiotic spindle, making it an ideal model system for investigating tubulin CTTs–kinesin binding process.

  Using steered molecular dynamics simulations in TUBB8-TUBA4A-KIF7 complex, we found that the β-tubulin CTT helps prevent kinesin from detaching from microtubules when an external force is applied, and then facilitate the recapture and rebinding of kinesin upon force release. This behavior is mediated through conformational changes in the CTTs. Further mechanistic investigations revealed that the presence of the CTTs enhances the physicochemical properties of kinesin-microtubule interface, thereby promoting complex stability.


## Method

### Modelling and molecular dynamics simulation
  The protein sequences of TUBA4A (UniProt: P68366, full length, α-tubulin), TUBB8 (UniProt: Q3ZCM7, full length, β-tubulin) and KIF7 (UniProt: Q2M1P5, head sequence 12-358) were obtained from UniProt database. SWISS-MODEL was used to predict the 3D structure of KIF7 head and AlphaFold 3 was used to predict the 3D structure of TUBA4A and TUBB8 in the presence or absence of CTTs (TUBA4A CTT sequence 435-448: VGIDSYEDEDEGEE; TUBB8 CTT sequence 425-444: YQDATAEEEEDEEYAEEEVA, select based on biochemical experiment7). Then we matched these structures of tubulins and kinesin based on the kinesin-microtubule complex (PDB: 6MLQ) and visualized using Chimera (Fig. S1). The initial complex structure (PDB: 6MLQ) contains KIF7 in the ADP-bound state and tubulin in the GDP-bound state, both of which favor kinesin-microtubule binding. The modeled structure was subsequently subjected to molecular dynamics (MD) simulation to study the binding state of tubulin and kinesin with and without CTTs and analyze the role of CTTs in regulating the binding state between tubulin and kinesin.

  Multiple MD simulations were applied in this work, including steered MD (SMD) simulations for kinesin-tubulin dimer and all-atom MD simulations for single β-tubulin, kinesins and kinesin-microtubules complexes with and without CTTs. The CHARMM-GUI23 webserver was used to create the simulating system. For each model simulation, CHARMM36m force field24 was applied, and the atoms in the system were parameterized by CHARMM36 force field. The system was solvated with TIP3P water with thickness of 15 Å and ionized with 150 mM NaCl. Periodic boundary condition was applied on the solvation box and PME was used for the long-range electrostatic interaction. Finally, NAnoscale Molecular Dynamics (NAMD) software was used to simulate the molecular dynamics of the system. Each simulation was performed in two steps: The equilibration (NVT) and the production runs (NPT). We set the system temperature and pressure to 310 K and 1 atm using Langevin thermostat method with a damping coefficient of 1/ps and Nosé–Hoover Langevin piston barostat with a decay period of 25 fs, respectively. The cutoffs for non-bonding (van der Waals and electrostatic) interactions were 12 Å. The switch distance was 10 Å.

  In biphasic SMD simulations, the pulling direction was aligned along the line connecting the centers of mass of the kinesin motor domain and the tubulin dimers. The α-carbon atoms of the tubulin dimers were fixed, except for the CTTs, while the α-carbon atoms of the kinesin motor domain were selected as the point of force application. Initially, a constant force of 6.96 pN was applied to pull the kinesin motor domain away from the tubulin dimers for 500 ps; the force was then doubled and maintained until complete separation was achieved. Incremental work was calculated as the product of the applied force and the displacement, the latter determined from the differences in consecutive centers of mass. Cumulative work was obtained by summing the incremental works.

### Electrostatic potential calculation
  The electrostatic potential at 0 ns and 100 ns of the complex structure of tubulins and kinesin binding with and without CTTs were calculated by DelPhi25, using finite difference method to get the numerically solving the Poisson Boltzmann equation. CHARMM36 force field was used to calculate the charge and radius of the atoms, and PDB2PQR was used to assign them, with dielectric constants of 2 and 80 for protein and water molecules, respectively. The salt concentration was set to 150 mM, and a probe of 1.4 Å in radius was used to define the dielectric boundary between the protein and the solvent. The filling ratio of protein and resolution were set to 0.70 and 1.5 grids/Å separately. The electrostatic surface potentials were visualized in Chimera with a color gradient from − 1.0 kT/e (red) to 1.0 kT/e (blue) .

### Hydrogen bonds analysis
  The hydrogen bond analysis was performed using VMD, based on the last 10 ns of the 1000 frames simulation. The cutoff distance and the angle for hydrogen bond analysis were set as 3.5 Å and 20°, respectively.


### Biomolecular structural k-means clustering (BSKC)29
  Here we applied BSKC method to analyze the conformational changes of intrinsically disordered CTTs. The method begins with the collection of structural frames from the simulation trajectory, followed by the computation of a pairwise structural distance matrix using a modified RMSD metric that emphasizes inter-protein contacts and IDRs. In this study, centroids were updated iteratively during k-means clustering, with the maximum number of clusters set to 10. The preliminary number of clusters was two for both the kinesin–microtubule complexes and the β-tubulin monomer. The number of members in each cluster was calculated, and the cluster representatives were selected as the members exhibiting the minimal average distance to all other members within their respective clusters.


## Results

### The β-tubulin CTT captures KIF7 and stabilizes the kinesin-tubulin binding
  As predicted by AlphFold3, the CTTs adopt an α-helix conformation both in kinesin-microtubule complex and in β-tubulin monomer (Figs. S1 and 1a, β-tubulin at 0 ns). According to the binding state of kinesin-microtubule cryo-EM structure (PDB: 6MLQ)16, KIF7 has a closer proximity to β-tubulin’s tail compared to that of α-tubulin (Fig. 1a, β-tubulin in complex at 0 ns), so we mainly focus on β-tubulin CTT-KIF7 interaction. MD simulations with BSKC method calculated two representatives for both monomer and the complex (Table S1. class 1 and class 2 for β-tubulin and the complex), indicating this helical structure remained stable within the kinesin-microtubule complex but became loosened in the initial stage and then transitioned into a completely disordered state by the fourth stage in the absence of kinesin (Fig. 1a–c). Notably, the RMSF of β-tubulin CTT decreased significantly upon kinesin binding (Fig. 1d–e), suggesting that the tail adopts a more stable conformation in the presence of kinesin. The structural transition from disordered state to a stable helix implies a functional role for the β-tubulin CTT in the binding process.Fig. 1The flexibility of CTTs. (a) The conformational changes of β-tubulin CTT in MD simulations with and without KIF7, where the class 1 and 2 are the representative structures calculated by BSKC. (b) the RMSF of β-tubulin for different stages. (c) The RMSF of the β-tubulin CTT in different stages. (d) The RMSF of tubulin dimer with and without CTTs. (e) The RMSF of CTTs in tubulin dimer.

  To clearly understand how the CTTs was involved in kinesin binding, we performed SMD simulations. Initially, a force of 6.95 pN was applied to separate KIF7 from the microtubule. The energy of this system increased drastically at first but plateaued after 200 ps (Fig. 2a). At 305 ps, the complex remained associated, with the helical β-tubulin CTT appearing to hook KIF7, thereby resisting further separation (Fig. 2b, KIF7-CTT’s hydrogen bonds: KIF7-Asn341 : TUBB8-CTT-Val443 and KIF7-Arg294 : TUBB8-CTT-Glu436). Subsequently, the force was doubled at 500 ps, causing the separation finally. By 600 ps, KIF7 began to detach from the microtubule, while the β-tubulin CTT, which exhibited an extended helical structural, remained adhered to KIF7 (Fig. 2c, KIF7-CTT’s hydrogen bonds: KIF7-Arg294 : TUBB8-CTT-Glu433). Complete separation occurred at 1058 ps, yet residual interactions between the β-tubulin CTT and KIF7 were observed, with the disordered tail seemingly attempting recapture of KIF7 (Fig. 2d, Movie S1). Upon removal of the external force, the β-tubulin CTT pulled KIF7 back and facilitated rebinding, with an unstructured state gradually returning into an α-helix (Movie S2). By contrast, without CTTs, the microtubules failed to capture the KIF7. (Movie S3). ### The CTTs enhance electrostatic interactions and reduce binding free energy between KIF7 and tubulin
  Next, we explored the effects of CTTs on kinesin-microtubule interactions. We first examined the electrostatic potential at the binding interface in the presence and absence of the tail. Analysis of surface electrostatic potentials revealed that the CTTs not only extended the negatively charged region on tubulin surface but also induced the exposure of a more centralized and positive charge patch on KIF7 (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, a higher density of electrostatic field lines was observed between kinesin and tubulin when CTTs were present (Fig. 3b).Fig. 3The electrostatic potential, binding forces, and binding free energy between kinesin and tubulin. (a) Electrostatic potential distribution on the interface of αβ-tubulin heterodimer and kinesin. In the absence of CTTs, the kinesin-tubulins binding complex was rotated 90° out of plane to the left and right, respectively. In the presence of CTTs, the kinesin-tubulin binding complex was rotated 90° out of plane to the left and right, respectively, to observe the difference between them. (b) The electrostatic field lines between kinesin and tubulin heterodimer. Given the distinct magnitude scales of different energy terms, we plotted them using two separate vertical axes: the left axis corresponds to Coulomb energy and polar solvation energy, whereas the right axis corresponds to van der Waals energy, non-polar solvation energy, and total binding free energy.

  We also evaluated the binding electrostatic forces, defined as the projection of the total force along the line connecting the centers of mass, as calculated by the total force multiplied by cos α. The presence of the CTTs increased these forces by 15–20% across inter-distance ranges of 10–50 Å (Fig. 3c, d). Additionally, the angle, α between the force direction and the line connecting the mass centers of tubulin was larger in complex containing the CTTs than that in those without them (Fig. 3d).

  Next, we compared the binding free energy of kinesin-microtubule complex with and without the CTTs. The average binding free energy was − 25.33 kcal/mol in the presence of the tail, lower than that of the tailless complex (− 22.52 kcal/mol), indicating stronger binding (Fig. 3f, e, g). The polar solvation binding free energy contributes the most among the four components of the binding free energy, which is relatively low in complex with CTTs compared to that lacking them. Together, these results demonstrate that the CTTs favor kinesin-microtubule binding by enhancing electrostatic potential and electrostatic force at the kinesin-microtubule interface, while also stabilizing the complex through a reduction in binding free energy.


### The CTTs alters hydrogen bonding occupancy between kinesin and tubulin
  We further investigated the influence of the CTTs on hydrogen bond formation between kinesin and tubulin by comparing hydrogen bond occupancy in the presence and absence of the CTTs. Our results revealed significant rearrangements in the hydrogen bonding network when the CTTs were present (Fig. 4a, b).Fig. 4Results of hydrogen bond analysis between tubulin and kinesin. (a, b) are spatial distribution of high-occupancy (> 50%) hydrogen bonds formed between tubulins and KIF7 without and with CTTs, respectively. (c, d) are hydrogen bonds (> 10%) formed between tubulin and KIF7 without and with CTTs, respectively.

  For instance, the hydrogen bonding occupancy between arginine 308 of KIF7 and glutamine 410 of TUBB8 increased substantially from 7.92% to 76.24% in the presence of CTTs (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the occupancy between arginine 302 of KIF7 and asparagine 417 of TUBB8 decreased from 85.15% to 14.85% in the complex with CTTs (Fig. 4c, d). This reduction may be attributed to the involvement of asparagine 417 in salt-bridge interactions with other tubulin (TUBA4A) residues. Certain hydrogen bonds, such as that between glutamine 421 of TUBB8 and arginine 302 of KIF7, remained stable regardless of the presence of the CTTs (Fig. 4c, d).

  These findings suggest that the reorganization of hydrogen bond occupancy induced by the CTTs contributes to the enhanced binding between kinesin and tubulin.


## Discussion and conclusion
  The CTTs of the αβ-tubulin heterodimer are critical regulators of kinesin-microtubule binding stability30. Although AlphaFold predicts an α-helical conformation for the CTTs, which is consistent with their structure in the kinesin-bound state. Our MD simulations reveal that, in the absence of kinesin, these tails adopt disordered, whip-like conformations and in the presence of kinesin, the structure present an α-helical conformation. This context-dependent structural behavior confirms their intrinsically disordered nature31,32 and underscores that static structural prediction cannot capture the full functional repertoire of these regions. Crucially, this disorder is not a structural artifact but a functional necessity, which may enable the CTTs to adaptively modulate the KIF7-tubulin binding. Through SMD simulation, we revealed that β-tubulin CTT is involved in this process via a two-step mechanism: CTT first facilitates initial contact and subsequently stabilize the bound complex.

  RMSF in multiple dynamic stages indicated the high flexibility of these disordered tails, contributing to the mechanistic features and functions. Notably, the β-tubulin CTT, in particular, exhibits a significant reduction in fluctuations upon kinesin binding (Fig. 1d, e), identifying it as the primary stabilizer. It is supported by Xie and Li’s finding that β-tubulin E-hooks exhibit approximately two-fold stronger attractive forces toward kinesin than α-tubulin by using DelPhiForce and flexibility analysis32. This dominant role is supported by the enriched high-occupancy interactions at β-tubulin’s interface (Fig. 4), such as KIF7-Arg308 : TUBB8-Gln410 (76.24% vs. 7.92%, with vs. without CTTs), and persistent hydrogen bonds like KIF7-Arg302 : TUBB8-Gln421 (> 50% occupancy). Conversely, the reduced occupancy of KIF7-Arg302 : TUBB8-Arg417 (14.85% vs. 85.15%, with vs. without CTTs), further indicates that CTTs selectively remodel the binding interface to favor more stable contacts. The publication reporting the KIF7-tubulins cryo-EM structure also identified enhanced interactions between KIF7 and β-tubulin compared to its binding with α-tubulin, a binding mode distinct from that of conventional kinesin-116. Additionally, a previous study has shown that human kinesin-2 exhibits enhanced affinity for the α-tubulin CTT33. These findings indicate the effect of the tubulin CTT on KIF7 differs from that on the motile kinesin-1 and kinesin-2 in kinesin binding process.

  Surface electrostatic potential analysis reveals that glutamate-rich CTTs extend the negatively charged tubulin interface, enhancing complementarity with kinesin’s positively charged motor domain (Fig. 3a). CTTs induce conformational changes in kinesin, centralizing its positive charge, which thereby amplifies electrostatic interactions. Unlike previous work with total force32, we took the binding component which shows a more accurate result. The results showed that the binding component increased significantly, indicating tighter interactions (Fig. 3c). Meanwhile, the angle between the force direction and tubulin’s mass centers also increased in the CTTs system (Fig. 3d). Thermodynamically, MM/PBSA calculations reveal stronger binding affinity with CTTs. The higher standard deviation of binding free energy in the CTTs region arises from larger fluctuations in the binding free energy components (Fig. 3f, g). These fluctuations reduce entropic penalties by allowing the CTTs to explore multiple conformations, adapting to kinesin’s interface, particularly for β-tubulin, which contributes more significantly to stability. This result is consistent with the “soft landing” hypothesis31.

  Mizuhara and Takano showed via coarse-grained MD that CTTs generate a ratchet-like energy landscape biasing KIF1A toward the microtubule plus-end, and that CTTs truncation abolishes both binding affinity and directional bias13; our 15–20% enhancement of electrostatic binding forces and reduction in binding free energy are conceptually consistent with this mechanism, extended here to the KIF7 system.

  Most recently, Nguyen et al. proposed a “catch-and-guide” model in which CTTs transiently tether the unbound motor head near the microtubule surface during the stepping cycle14. Our SMD data show that the CTT “hooks” the kinesin head during forced dissociation (Fig. 2b) and facilitates recapture upon force release. It provides direct structural evidence for this model, with the disorder-to-α-helix transition upon kinesin contact representing its plausible molecular basis. At the continuum electrostatics level, the Li and Alexov groups demonstrated that the binding component of the electrostatic force is a more accurate predictor of binding affinity than the total force26,34, our quantitative results align with and validate this methodological framework.

  Our study also introduces several distinctions relative to this body of work. First, we focus on KIF7, a non-processive, signal-transducing kinesin whose CTT-binding behavior has not previously been examined computationally, in contrast to the canonical processive motors (kinesin-1, KIF1A, KIF5) studied previously. Second, we model the TUBA4A–TUBB8 heterodimer, the predominant isotype in human oocytes and early embryos, whereas prior studies have employed somatic tubulin isotypes. Since CTT sequences diverge across isotypes, our results offer isotype-specific insights relevant to spindle assembly and kinesin regulation in embryonic development. However, whether the different tubulin CTTs and nucleotide state have a similar role in binding kinesin still requires further investigation. Besides, the selected complex model, in which KIF7 is closer to β-tubulin, limits discovery of α-tubulin CTT binding roles with kinesin.

  Collectively, the convergence of our findings with prior computational evidence supports a unified mechanistic model in which tubulin CTTs function as electrostatically active, conformationally dynamic tethers that enhance kinesin–microtubule interactions through synergistic mechanisms: transiently catching and guiding unbound kinesin motor heads, extending the negatively charged binding interface and allosterically remodeling the hydrogen-bonding network at the kinesin–tubulin interface. Our work extends this framework to the developmentally relevant TUBA4A–TUBB8–KIF7 system, providing atomistic insight into how tubulin isotype diversity and CTTs conformational plasticity collectively tune kinesin behavior in embryonic cells.
