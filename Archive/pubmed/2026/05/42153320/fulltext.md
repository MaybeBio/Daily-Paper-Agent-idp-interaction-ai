# MuFGPS: enhancing liquid–liquid phase separation protein prediction through multi-level features and ensemble learning


## Abstract
  Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a key mechanism driving the assembly of membrane-less organelles and is increasingly recognized for its involvement in essential cellular functions and various diseases. However, existing computational approaches largely rely on sequence-level descriptors and often fail to explicitly incorporate structural topology information, limiting their ability to capture the complex determinants of LLPS behavior. Accurate identification of LLPS-capable proteins remains challenging due to their sequence diversity and complex structural determinants. Here, we present MuFGPS (Multi-level Feature Graph-based Predictor for Phase-Separating proteins), a predictive framework integrating sequence-derived physicochemical features, Define Secondary Structure of Proteins-annotated secondary structures, and graph-based structural embeddings from AlphaFold residue contact maps via a multi-head Graph Attention Network. Class imbalance is addressed using Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), and classification is performed through a stacking ensemble of Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. Benchmarks against six representative methods demonstrate that MuFGPS achieves superior performance across all metrics, with notable gains in F1-score and matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). Ablation analyses confirm the synergistic contributions of structural features and ensemble learning to accuracy and robustness. MuFGPS offers a scalable and high-accuracy framework for proteome-wide LLPS protein prediction.


## Introduction
  Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) serves as an essential physicochemical process driving the formation of membrane-less organelles within cells, such as nucleoli, stress granules, and P-bodies. It occurs primarily through multivalent weak interactions among biomacromolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids, leading to the spatial and temporal enrichment of specific components and the formation of dynamic structures lacking lipid membrane encapsulation. By providing a segregated environment, LLPS enables biomolecules to efficiently carry out biochemical reactions and interactions, thereby regulating essential biological processes including transcription, DNA repair, and signal transduction [1, 2]. Dysregulation of LLPS has been implicated in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, as well as cancer and viral infections [3–6]. In these conditions, aberrant LLPS can result in pathological aggregation or amyloid-like fibril formation, disrupting cellular processes and promoting disease progression [7]. Thus, elucidating the molecular basis of LLPS and identifying related proteins is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies for these diseases.

  Significant progress has been made in understanding the mechanisms underlying LLPS. Studies have shown that LLPS often depends on features such as intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) and low-complexity regions (LCRs) in proteins. Due to their high flexibility and adaptability, these regions can mediate a variety of non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π interactions [8–10]. These interactions provide the driving forces for protein aggregation, ultimately leading to droplet formation. In addition, protein charge distribution and hydrophilicity play important roles in LLPS [8, 11]; electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged molecules can promote aggregation, while enrichment of hydrophobic residues can further strengthen intermolecular interactions, facilitating droplet formation. While these mechanistic insights have substantially advanced our understanding of LLPS at the molecular level, translating them into accurate proteome-wide predictive models remains challenging. The multivalent, context-dependent, and structurally modulated nature of LLPS interactions makes it difficult to capture phase separation propensity using simple compositional descriptors alone.

  To address this, various computational prediction methods have been developed, each leveraging different theoretical frameworks and feature representations. For example, PLAAC [12] detects prion-like domains by calculating the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) of amino acid compositions relative to prion sequences, indirectly linking prion-like properties to phase separation capability, but its applicability is limited for LLPS proteins lacking such domains. catGRANULE [13] integrates disorder propensity, RNA-binding capacity, and arginine-glycine-glycine (RGG) motifs into a weighted scoring system, achieving high accuracy for RNA-binding LLPS proteins but performing less effectively for LLPS driven by other mechanisms. PScore [14] quantifies the propensity for π–π interactions between aromatic residues, a key driving force of LLPS, but overlooks other important interactions such as electrostatics and hydrophobicity. DeePhase [11] employs deep learning to capture nonlinear relationships from sequence-derived physicochemical features, improving generalization capability, but remains constrained by dataset dependency, potential overfitting, and limited interpretability. FuzDrop [7] estimates droplet formation probability based on statistical potentials derived from disorder and sequence features. Seq2Phase [15] combines pre-trained protein language models with deep neural networks to capture latent sequence patterns related to LLPS. Similarly, MambaPhase [16] integrates Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM) with a Mamba architecture to extract sequence features and predicts phase separation behavior. Although these computational methods have made notable progress in LLPS protein prediction, most approaches rely predominantly on sequence features and specific interaction motifs, with relatively limited integration of higher-order structural information. This reliance may overlook spatial organization and residue-level topology, which are critical for understanding multivalent interaction networks underlying LLPS. As a result, existing models may struggle to fully capture the structural determinants governing phase separation behavior. In parallel, recent deep learning frameworks in molecular modeling have demonstrated that integrating complementary representations and attention-based architectures can substantially enhance predictive performance [17, 18]. Given the close relationship between protein phase separation behavior and spatial structure, ignoring structural information may limit predictive accuracy and generalization capability. In recent years, AlphaFold has emerged as the leading method for three-dimensional protein structure prediction, achieving near-experimental accuracy across a wide range of proteins [19]. The public release of the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database has enabled large-scale access to high-confidence structural models [20]. Notably, despite the high intrinsic disorder of many LLPS-prone proteins, AlphaFold2 can still generate reliable structural predictions under such challenging conditions [21]. The availability of high-confidence structural models therefore provides an unprecedented opportunity to incorporate residue-level spatial context into LLPS prediction frameworks, potentially bridging the gap between sequence-driven models and structure-informed mechanisms.

  In this study, we present MuFGPS (Multi-level Feature Graph-based Predictor for Phase-Separating proteins), a predictive framework that integrates multi-level feature representations with advanced learning strategies. The framework captures not only compositional and physicochemical properties derived from protein sequences, but also secondary structure information and residue contact maps generated from high-accuracy structural models predicted by AlphaFold. To extract high-order structural features, we employ a multi-head Graph Attention Network (GAT), enabling a more comprehensive characterization of the structural context associated with phase separation propensity. To mitigate class imbalance, we incorporate the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), and further enhance predictive capability through a stacking ensemble learning architecture that combines multiple complementary classifiers. Comparative evaluations demonstrate that MuFGPS consistently outperforms existing methods, achieving superior overall predictive performance.


## Results

### Overview of MuFGPS
  To achieve accurate identification of LLPS proteins, this study constructs a predictive model, MuFGPS, that integrates multiple feature representations and multi-stage learning strategies (Fig. 1). The overall process includes sequence feature extraction, structural feature modeling, graph neural network representation learning, and a stacking ensemble classification module.

  First, various features based on physicochemical properties and functional tendencies are extracted from the protein’s raw sequence, including disorder propensity, hydrophobicity, solubility, residue composition preferences, and charge distribution. Relevant features are computed using tools such as ESpritz [22], the SEG low-complexity region detection algorithm [23], catGRANULE [13], PLAAC [12], PScore [14], and LocalCIDER [24]. In addition, secondary structure information, including α-helix, β-sheet, and β-turn, is extracted from structure files in Protein Data Bank (PDB) format using the Define Secondary Structure of Proteins (DSSP) tool, enhancing the model’s ability to capture local conformational features.

  To further incorporate three-dimensional structural information, residue contact maps are constructed based on PDB coordinate data, where each node represents a residue. An edge is added between two nodes if the distance between the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${C}_{\alpha }$\end{document} atoms of the two residues is less than 8 Å. The initial representation of each node is generated by the pre-trained language model SeqVec, which is based on bidirectional context language modeling and captures the sequence-level semantic features of the residues.

  The constructed protein graph is then input into a three-layer stacked multi-head GAT for structural feature extraction. The first two layers use three attention heads, and the third layer uses a single attention head. After graph convolution and global pooling operations, a fixed-dimensional graph structure vector of length 128 is obtained, effectively integrating the protein’s contextual information in spatial topology.

  After concatenating sequence features, secondary structure features, and graph structure features into a unified representation, the SMOTE method is applied to synthetically augment positive samples in the training set to mitigate the class imbalance issue. Finally, a two-level stacked ensemble learning framework is constructed for the classification task. The first layer consists of three complementary tree models: Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM. Each base learner models the data and outputs prediction probabilities. After concatenating the outputs from the three models into a secondary feature matrix, it is fed into the second layer’s logistic regression meta-learner, which outputs the final prediction result.


### Comparative analysis of feature distributions between positive and negative samples
  To assess the effectiveness of various handcrafted features in distinguishing LLPS proteins from non-LLPS proteins, we conducted a statistical analysis of the distribution differences of 15 biophysical and structural features extracted from proteins, and evaluated their statistical significance using the Mann–Whitney U test (Fig. 2). The analysis results indicate that most features exhibit significant differentiation between positive and negative samples, suggesting their important role in identifying LLPS proteins.

  LLPS proteins typically display higher intrinsic disorder and lower sequence complexity, which is particularly evident in the scores obtained from the ESpritz and SEG algorithms (P < .001). This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting that IDRs are enriched in LLPS proteins and play a central role in driving phase separation through multivalent weak interactions [8, 25]. The granule formation propensity score calculated by catGRANULE and the PLAAC score are significantly higher in the positive samples, further validating that these sequence features are enriched in LLPS proteins and may contribute to regulating their liquid–liquid phase separation behavior. Several physicochemical features calculated by LocalCIDER also show distinct differences. Compared to non-LLPS proteins, LLPS proteins have significantly longer sequences and higher Omega values, indicating more structural heterogeneity in the distribution of charged and special residues (such as prolines). Charge patterning and sequence length have been reported as key determinants influencing electrostatic interactions and conformational behavior in LLPS proteins [8]. Expansion-promoting residue fraction (EPRF) and disorder-promoting residue fraction (DPRF) values are significantly elevated, suggesting that residues contributing to chain extension and disorder formation are more prevalent in LLPS proteins, which facilitates the construction of flexible structures and the formation of multivalent weak interaction networks. The polyproline II (PPII) propensity is also significantly enhanced in positive samples, indicating that flexible regions may participate in the phase separation process through specific conformations. Mean Hydropathy is significantly reduced in LLPS proteins, reflecting a more hydrophilic sequence environment, which aids in maintaining diffusibility and reversible aggregation behavior in solution. Reduced overall hydropathy has been observed in many LLPS proteins, distinguishing them from aggregation-prone structured proteins [25]. Furthermore, the secondary structure features annotated by DSSP also show some discriminative power. The results reveal that the α-helix content is significantly lower in LLPS proteins, while the β-sheet and β-turn contents slightly increase. This suggests that LLPS proteins tend to reduce the formation of ordered structures and accumulate in regions that are more likely to adopt flexible or turn-like conformations, enhancing their structural dynamics and interaction capabilities, which provides a structural basis for phase separation. The frequency of π–π interactions (PScore) is significantly higher in positive samples, reflecting that aromatic residue stacking interactions are more prevalent in LLPS proteins. Aromatic π–π stacking interactions have been experimentally demonstrated as key driving forces in biomolecular phase separation [25]. Finally, protein solubility (Percent-Sol) is generally lower in positive samples, consistent with the biophysical characteristic of LLPS proteins, which are more likely to form condensed phases and have reduced solubility. Such reduced solubility and reversible condensation behavior are widely recognized physicochemical features of phase-separating proteins [26].


### SMOTE-based positive protein feature sample generation
  To address the class imbalance, aiming to boost the classifier’s performance on rare classes, we introduced the SMOTE algorithm for oversampling the positive samples. To intuitively analyze the impact of SMOTE on the distribution characteristics of positive class samples, we used the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) method to reduce the dimensionality and visualize the original positive samples alongside the synthetic positive samples, as shown in Fig. 3. From the figure, we can observe that the synthetic samples generated by SMOTE are evenly distributed around the original positive samples in the feature space, forming multiple localized expansion areas. This phenomenon suggests that SMOTE can effectively extend the distribution range of positive samples in the feature space while maintaining the original distribution structure. The synthetic samples do not exhibit isolated or discrete cluster distributions, but rather integrate naturally into the local structure of the original samples. Additionally, the synthetic samples are generated near several dense areas of the original positive samples, significantly enhancing the expression density of these local regions in the feature space, thereby contributing to improved model learning stability and better boundary characterization for the positive class during training.

  To rigorously assess the robustness of the SMOTE strategy and to exclude the possibility that performance improvements arise from overfitting to synthetic samples, we further evaluated the model on an independent test set that was entirely excluded from model training and hyperparameter optimization.

  As illustrated in Fig. 4A and B, the SMOTE-enhanced model exhibited substantially improved sensitivity toward the positive class on the test set. Specifically, recall increased from 0.586 to 0.793, with the number of false negatives reduced from 12 to 6. This indicates that augmenting the minority class during training effectively improved the model’s ability to identify LLPS-capable proteins. Although the number of false positives increased moderately (from 0 to 10), resulting in a decrease in precision, this trade-off reflects a more balanced decision boundary rather than overfitting behavior.

  In addition, the SMOTE-enhanced model achieved slightly higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) values compared with the model trained on the original dataset (Fig. 4C and D). The consistent improvement in AUPRC is particularly noteworthy given the imbalanced nature of the data, as AUPRC more accurately reflects minority class performance. Collectively, these results obtained on an independent dataset demonstrate that the observed performance gains are attributable to improved minority class representation during training, suggesting that the performance gains are unlikely to be solely attributable to overfitting to synthetic samples. The SMOTE strategy therefore enhances model generalization and minority class detectability without substantially compromising overall discrimination capability.


### Comparative performance analysis of MuFGPS against state-of-the-art LLPS prediction methods
  To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed MuFGPS model, we conducted comparative experiments against six representative LLPS protein prediction methods: catGRANULE, PScore, DeePhase, FuzDrop, Seq2Phase, and MambaPhase. All methods were evaluated on the same benchmark dataset, and their classification performance was comprehensively compared using five commonly used metrics as shown in Table 1.

  Overall, MuFGPS achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics, with an accuracy exceeding 91%, representing an improvement of ~0.8%–10% over existing methods. Compared with the second-best method, MambaPhase, MuFGPS achieved a relative accuracy gain of about 0.8%, alongside Precision and Recall improvements of ~1.7% and 1.6%, respectively. The simultaneous enhancement of Precision and Recall contributed to the highest F1-score (0.8356), indicating a more balanced classification capability. In addition, MuFGPS achieved an matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.7749, markedly higher than those of the other methods, demonstrating its superior overall discriminative ability and robustness, particularly under class-imbalanced conditions.

  To further assess statistical significance, we conducted paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests based on fold-level cross-validation results. Since MuFGPS achieved higher performance than each baseline model across all five folds, the test yielded its minimal attainable exact P-value (Supplementary Table S1).


### Ablation study

#### Contribution of structural feature components
  To evaluate the contribution of different structural feature components in MuFGPS, we conducted a set of ablation experiments by selectively removing DSSP-derived secondary-structure features and GAT-based graph structural embeddings. In addition to the full MuFGPS model, three variants were considered: No-Struct, in which all structural features were removed; No-DSSP, in which only DSSP-derived features were excluded; and No-GAT, in which only graph structural features learned by the GAT were removed.

  As shown in Fig. 5A, removing all structural features (No-Struct) resulted in consistent performance degradation across all five metrics. Compared with the full MuFGPS model, Accuracy decreased from 0.911 to 0.844, while MCC dropped markedly from 0.775 to 0.595, corresponding to a relative reduction of ~23.2%. This indicates that structural information provides critical discriminative cues beyond sequence-level features alone.

  When structural components were removed separately, both No-DSSP and No-GAT underperformed the full model, but to different extents. Specifically, removing GAT-based graph embeddings led to a decline in Recall (from 0.824 to 0.716) and MCC (from 0.775 to 0.673), while removing DSSP-derived features resulted in Recall and MCC values of 0.730 and 0.676, respectively. These results suggest that both structural components contribute substantially to predictive performance, with graph-based structural representations showing slightly greater impact on Recall. Nevertheless, the best overall performance was achieved only when both types of structural features were jointly incorporated, highlighting their complementary contributions.


#### Effect of different protein language model embeddings
  To assess the robustness of MuFGPS with respect to different protein sequence embedding strategies, we compared the original model using SeqVec embeddings (MuFGPS-SeqVec) with a variant employing ProtBERT embeddings (MuFGPS-ProtBERT), while keeping all other components unchanged.

  As illustrated in Fig. 5B, both variants achieved strong and comparable performance across all metrics. MuFGPS-SeqVec slightly outperformed MuFGPS-ProtBERT, with MCC values of 0.775 and 0.756, respectively. The relative difference between the two models remained below 3% for all metrics, indicating that the proposed framework is not overly dependent on a specific protein language model. These results demonstrate that MuFGPS can effectively exploit different pretrained sequence representations while maintaining stable predictive performance.


#### Impact of the decision-level ensemble strategy
  We further investigated the role of the decision-level stacking ensemble strategy by comparing MuFGPS with alternative decision modules. Four configurations were evaluated: No-Struct-No-Ensemble, in which both structural features and the ensemble module were removed; Random Forest (RF), where all features were retained but the stacking ensemble was replaced with a single Random Forest classifier; multilayer perceptron (MLP), in which the ensemble classifier was replaced by a multilayer perceptron operating at the decision level; and the full MuFGPS model.

  As shown in Fig. 5C, introducing structural features without ensemble learning (RF) substantially improved performance compared with the No-Struct-No-Ensemble baseline. For example, MCC increased from 0.562 to 0.698, corresponding to a relative improvement of ~24.2%. Replacing the ensemble with a decision-level MLP yielded an MCC of 0.670. However, this variant still underperformed the full MuFGPS model, which achieved the highest MCC of 0.775.

  Overall, compared with the No-Struct-No-Ensemble baseline, the full MuFGPS model achieved improvements of ~9.4% in Accuracy, 14.8% in Precision, 35.5% in Recall, 25.3% in F1-score, and 37.9% in MCC, demonstrating that the stacking ensemble strategy provides additional benefits beyond single-classifier decision modules by more effectively integrating heterogeneous predictive signals.


## Conclusion
  We developed MuFGPS, a predictive framework that integrates sequence-derived physicochemical descriptors, DSSP-annotated secondary structure information, and graph-based structural features extracted from AlphaFold-predicted residue contact maps via a multi-head Graph Attention Network. By combining SMOTE oversampling with a stacking ensemble strategy, MuFGPS achieves superior performance over six representative LLPS prediction methods, with consistent improvements across all metrics. Ablation experiments highlight the synergistic contributions of structural features and ensemble learning to both robustness and predictive accuracy. MuFGPS offers a scalable and accurate approach for proteome-wide LLPS protein identification. While MuFGPS demonstrates strong predictive performance, several directions merit further exploration. Future work may involve evaluating the framework under more stringent redundancy thresholds as larger LLPS datasets become available, incorporating dynamic structural or disorder–structure transition features, and integrating interaction context information such as protein–RNA associations. In addition, experimental validation of high-confidence predictions would further strengthen the biological applicability of the proposed framework.


## Materials and methods

### Dataset construction
  The positive dataset used in this study was collected from four experimentally validated or predicted LLPS databases: LLPSDB [27], PhaSepDB [28], PhaSePro [29], and DrLLPS [30]. To meet the requirements for model training and testing, we selected scaffold proteins capable of undergoing phase separation and excluded proteins for which no complete structure could be found in the AlphaFold database. Additionally, proteins that could not be processed or predicted by PScore [14] due to length limitations were also excluded. We applied the Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance (CD-HIT) [31] algorithm to eliminate sequence redundancy, with a similarity threshold set to 0.8, resulting in 366 positive samples. Proteins not recorded or reported to exhibit phase separation tendencies were considered as negative samples. We randomly selected 150 candidate negative protein sequences from each of 10 representative species. After applying the same preprocessing and filtering procedures as used for the positive dataset, 1302 negative sequences were retained. Furthermore, we further collected 29 positive and 124 negative samples as an extra test set.


### Sequence features extraction
  To comprehensively characterize the functional potential of proteins in LLPS, this study systematically extracted multidimensional physicochemical features at the sequence level, covering aspects such as disorder, charge distribution, hydrophobicity, and aggregation propensity, in order to capture molecular properties closely related to phase separation. These properties are strongly linked to phase separation phenomena [10]. We used the ESpritz [22] program to predict IDRs in protein sequences, with a threshold set at a 5% false positive rate. The SEG [23] toolkit, using default parameters, was applied to detect LCRs. For quantitative representation, the scores of IDRs and LCRs were defined as the proportion of amino acid residues in the corresponding regions relative to the total sequence length. The LocalCIDER [24] toolkit provides methods to compute various properties of disordered sequences, and we applied it to calculate several key protein metrics, including sequence length, the distribution pattern of charged residues and Omega value, the propensity for the formation of PPII helices (propensity), average hydrophobicity, the EPRF, and the DPRF. Here, the Omega value quantifies the linear patterning of charged residues and prolines along the sequence, reflecting the degree of residue clustering, which is associated with electrostatic interactions relevant to phase separation. The PLAAC [12] tool was used to identify prion-like domains in proteins, with several statistical outputs, from which the LLR score was selected as the primary reference value. PScore [14] was used to detect possible π–π stacking interactions between aromatic residues in proteins, which are one of the key driving forces for the formation of multivalent networks and play an important role in the LLPS mechanism. The catGRANULE [13] tool integrates intrinsic disorder and local residue composition features of protein sequences, and we used the catGRANULE score to characterize the likelihood of participation in the formation of cytoplasmic granules, such as stress granules. The Protein-sol [32] software package was used to obtain protein solubility scores, quantifying the ability of proteins to remain in a stable dispersed state in solution. The relevant tools and parameter settings are shown in Table S2.


### Representation of secondary structure features
  DSSP [33, 34] is an authoritative annotation tool based on hydrogen bond geometry rules and topological features. The structural proportion information extracted by DSSP serves as a quantifiable indicator of protein structure stability, folding state, and conformational diversity, providing structural-level supplementary evidence for identifying potential phase separation driving factors. We retrieved high-quality predicted protein 3D structural models from the AlphaFold database and further extracted their secondary structure information based on the downloaded PDB-format structure files. Specifically, the DSSP program was used to analyze each protein structure, identifying and quantifying the distribution of typical secondary structure elements, such as α-helix, β-sheet, and β-turn.

### Stacking-based ensemble classification model
  To construct a discriminative model for LLPS proteins, we integrated three different sources of features based on the aforementioned feature extraction. These include traditional sequence features derived from protein sequence physicochemical properties, secondary structure features annotated by DSSP, and graph embeddings extracted by the graph neural network. The concatenated multimodal feature vector incorporates sequence information, local conformations, and spatial topology, serving as the input representation for the classification model.

  Considering the severe class imbalance in the training samples, which may adversely affect model performance, we introduced the SMOTE [36] strategy during the training phase to oversample the minority class samples. SMOTE over-sampling was performed exclusively within the training subset of each cross-validation fold after feature concatenation. This method generates new synthetic samples by performing linear interpolation between the original positive class samples in the feature space, effectively alleviating the class imbalance issue and improving the model’s ability to recognize positive classes.

  In the classification modeling phase, we employed a stacking ensemble learning [37] approach to construct a two-level classification framework. The first level consists of three complementary tree models: Random Forest [38], XGBoost [39], and LightGBM [40]. These models differ significantly in feature partitioning strategies, sample sampling methods, and modeling mechanisms, which helps capture diverse information in the feature space, enhancing the model’s expressive power and robustness.
