# Thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor mediates peripheral–central neuroimmune crosstalk in autoimmune thyroid diseases


## Background
  Organ-specific autoimmune diseases, particularly Graves’ disease (GD) and its extrathyroidal manifestation, Graves’ orbitopathy (GO), are characterized by systemic autoimmunity that may extend its impact to the central nervous system (CNS). While thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) is the primary driver of pathological remodeling in the thyroid and orbital tissues, emerging evidence suggests it is also expressed in the brain and may participate in neuroimmune signaling. However, the molecular mechanisms linking peripheral TSHR-driven autoimmunity to these extended systemic features remain unclear. Thus, GD and GO provide a unique window to investigate how peripheral autoantibodies influence CNS involvement as part of its broader pathological spectrum.


## Methods
  Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and post-GWAS analyses were integrated with bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, and brain imaging phenotypes to comprehensively characterize peripheral and central alterations in GD and GO. Mendelian randomization was applied to test causal relationships between genetic variants and brain signatures. Structural biology analyses were further conducted including protein–protein docking, small-molecule docking, and normal mode dynamics to identify prospective modulators of TSHR. Immunofluorescence staining was performed in a GO mouse model to validate the colocalization of potential interacted proteins in the specific brain region.


## Results
  Brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) alterations in GO and GO were systematically analyzed to identify neuroanatomical and functional alterations. TSHR was further identified as a shared genetic driver across peripheral and central compartments. TSHR was expressed in spiny projection neurons, microglia, and peripheral T cells, with cell–cell communication analyses highlighting TSHR-mediated interactions among neurons, endothelial cells, and microglia. Immunofluorescence staining in a GO mouse model confirmed the colocalization of TSHR with FN1 and GNAS in the basal ganglia, providing tissue-level validation of the computationally predicted ligand–receptor interactions. Immune profiling further showed immune alterations in GD and GO. Structural modeling supported plausible physical interfaces between TSHR and interacting proteins, and small-molecule screening identified three repurposable compounds — venetoclax, irinotecan, and dutasteride — with predicted favorable docking scores and stable binding poses in our simulations.


## Conclusions
  These findings demonstrate that TSHR acts as a molecular hub mediating peripheral–central neuroimmune crosstalk in GD and GO. The results support a broader “disease–molecule axis” framework that links genetic susceptibility with multi-level immune and neural mechanisms. This work provides mechanistic insights relevant to the development of TSHR-targeted therapies, with implications for both peripheral immune modulation and central regulation. However, the limited sample size, lack of longitudinal follow-up, and absence of in vivo validation warrant cautious interpretation and further investigation.


## Background
  Autoimmune diseases represent a diverse group of disorders that can involve multiple organs, including the central nervous system (CNS). While CNS manifestations are well established in systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus. Emerging evidence suggests that organ-specific autoimmune diseases—classically viewed as localized to peripheral organs—can also induce systemic neuroimmune alterations, particularly in autoimmune thyroid diseases [1]. Patients with autoimmune thyroid disease frequently present with neuropsychiatric manifestations, including anxiety, depression, and cognitive decline, yet the mechanisms linking peripheral autoimmunity to brain dysfunction remain poorly defined [2]. Considerable research has explored neuroimmune crosstalk, describing signaling pathways, organ-level interactions, and immune dysregulation, but a cardinal question is whether molecular hubs exist to serve as a nexus between peripheral immune dysfunction and CNS alterations [3, 4].

  The thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR) represents a compelling candidate for such investigations. As the central autoantigen in thyroid autoimmunity, TSHR is the driver of both Graves’ disease (GD) and its extrathyroidal manifestation, Graves’ orbitopathy (GO), also known as thyroid eye disease (TED). GD and GO provide a rare system in which one well-defined autoantigen is responsible for the thyroid pathology. TSHR is not only expressed in the thyroid and orbit but also in the brain, where it has been linked to psychiatric and neurocognitive disorders [5–7]. This makes TSHR an ideal paradigm gene to interrogate how a specific molecular hub can mediate systemic neuroimmune interactions.

  Several neuroimaging studies have described structural and functional brain alterations in GD and GO, implicating regions such as the prefrontal cortex, limbic system, and visual pathways [8–10]. However, these findings remain largely descriptive, and the molecular basis for CNS involvement is poorly understood. While T cell dysfunction and immune activation are vital to GD and GO pathogenesis, the neuroimmune mechanisms linking peripheral autoimmunity to brain changes remain unidentified [11, 12].

  Multi-omics approaches coupled with functional neuroimaging now allow the integration of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) with transcriptomic and spatially resolved data to dissect brain alterations in autoimmune diseases [13–16]. ## Methods

### Study cohorts and data acquisition
  Publicly available datasets were first incorporated in this study. GWAS summary statistics for GD (Ncase = 3,962, Ncontrol = 496,386, E4_GRAVES_STRICT) and GO (Ncase = 858, Ncontrol = 7,920, finngen_R12_GRAVES_OPHT) were obtained from the FinnGen biobank. The FinnGen study is a large-scale genomics initiative that has analyzed over 500,000 Finnish biobank samples and correlated genetic variation with health data to understand disease mechanisms and predispositions. The project is a collaboration between research organisations and biobanks within Finland and international industry partners [17]. Brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs; N = 33,224) were sourced from the UKB [18].

  In addition, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from human striatal tissues, including four caudate and six putamen samples, were downloaded from the CellxGene database [19, 20].

  In parallel, original data were prospectively generated in our study. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) was collected from a local imaging cohort comprising 12 GD patients, 32 GO patients, and 28 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) using a 3·0 Tesla MRI scanner.

  Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from a local bulk RNA cohort including 20 GD patients, 20 GO patients, and 20 matched HCs for RNA sequencing. ScRNA-seq data of PBMCs were generated from one GD patient, one GO patient, and one HC. Detailed clinical data and eligibility criteria for GD, GO, and HC groups in imaging cohort and bulk RNA cohort are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods and Additional file 2: table S1–2 [21].


### Genetic association and causal inference
  To evaluate the causal relationship between GD, GO and brain IDPs, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were conducted using TwoSampleMR (v0·6·29). Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸), low linkage disequilibrium (r² < 0·001), and high F-statistics (F > 10) [22]. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately for each IDP category, retaining results with FDR < 0·05. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary causal inference approach, complemented by MR-Egger and Cochran’s Q test for sensitivity analyses [23–25]. Only phenotypes showing unidirectional significance were retained. Bidirectionally significant phenotypes were excluded from the final interpretation. Details of SNP filtering and F-statistic calculation are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods [22, 26, 27].


### Neuroimaging data acquisition and analysis
  Rs-fMRI data were acquired using a 3·0 T MRI scanner. Preprocessing steps included motion correction, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing using standard pipelines (DPARSF and SPM12). ALFF (amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation) and ReHo (regional homogeneity) metrics were calculated and normalized to global brain means. Quality control measures excluded individuals with excessive head motion.

  Further acquisition parameters and preprocessing details are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods [28, 29].


### Transcriptome-wide integration
  To map genetic signals onto brain spatial architecture, genetically informed spatial mapping of cells for complex traits (gsMap) was applied to project GD and GO GWAS summary statistics onto a mouse E16·5 spatial transcriptomic atlas [16]. Gene-level associations were analyzed using Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA), aggregating SNP-level signals into gene-level statistics and enabling gene-set enrichment analysis [30]. The jVenn tool was employed to visualize and analyze the intersections and unions of gene sets between GD and GO [31]. Pathway enrichment and tissue-specific expression profiles were further assessed using Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA), a platform that annotates GWAS results and links them to biological pathways and tissues [32]. To prioritize putative causal genes, transcriptome-wide Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analyses were performed using large-scale blood and brain expression Quantitative Trait Loci (eQTL) datasets from eQTLGen and GTEx v8, testing whether disease-associated variants exert their effects via gene expression [33]. Enrichment and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed based on SMR-significant genes [34, 35]. To further evaluate whether GWAS and eQTL association signals share a common causal variant, Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed using the coloc R package (v5.2.0) [36]. Given the substantially larger sample size of the eQTLGen consortium relative to GTEx brain tissues, eQTLGen provides higher statistical power and more precise effect estimates for colocalization inference. To minimize false-negative results due to limited power in smaller brain eQTL datasets, colocalization analyses were therefore performed using eQTLGen data only. For each locus, summary statistics from GWAS and eQTLGen blood eQTL datasets were extracted within ±1 Mb of the lead SNP. Five posterior probabilities were calculated (PP.H0–PP.H4), with PP.H4 representing the probability that both traits share a single causal variant. Evidence of colocalization was defined as PP.H4 > 0.75, and strong evidence was defined as PP.H4 > 0.9.


### Bulk and single-cell RNA-seq profiling
  Bulk RNA-seq was performed on PBMCs from GD, GO, and HC participants using DNBSEQ technology, with ERCC spike-in controls. Data were processed using fastp, HISAT2, GATK, and StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Limma package (v3·62·2), followed by functional and cell types enrichment analysis with clusterProfiler (v4·12·6) and enrichr [34, 37, 38].

  For scRNA-seq, PBMCs were profiled using the BD Rhapsody platform. Seurat (v5·1·0), Omicverse (v1·6·2) and CellTypist (v1·7·1) were used for data preprocessing and immune cell annotation [39, 40]. To integrate GWAS findings, we applied single-cell Disease Relevance Score (scDRS) projection based on MAGMA gene-level results and visualized gene expression density using Nebulosa (v1·14·0) [15, 41]. Additional quality control and batch correction steps are detailed in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods [42].

  Intercellular signaling was inferred using CellChat (v1·5·0) and LIANA (v0·1·13), integrating multiple ligand-receptor inference frameworks [43, 44].


#### Immunofluorescence validation in an adenovirus-induced GO mouse model
  Following established paradigms in the previous study [45], the GO mouse model was developed by immunizing mice with a recombinant adenovirus encoding the TSHR A-subunit. This approach is widely recognized for its efficacy in replicating key GO pathological features

  To provide tissue-level validation of the computational predictions, immunofluorescence staining was performed on brain tissues obtained from GO model mice. Brains were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, cryoprotected in sucrose solution, and sectioned coronally to include the caudate nucleus and putamen regions.

  For cellular localization analysis, double immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against TSHR together with cell-type–specific markers, including the neuronal marker NeuN (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# ABN90), the microglial marker IBA1 (Novus Biologicals, Cat# NB100-1028), and the astrocytic marker GFAP (Millipore, Cat# AB5541). NeuN, IBA1, and GFAP antibodies were each used at a dilution of 1:500.

  For triple immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against TSHR (Proteintech, Cat# 14450-1-AP), FN1 (Abcam, Cat# ab45688), and GNAS (Bioss, Cat# bs-8160R). These primary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000. As all three primary antibodies were rabbit-derived, tyramide signal amplification (TSA) was applied to enable sequential multiplex staining according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

  After incubation with primary antibodies, sections were treated with species-compatible HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies followed by TSA fluorophore deposition. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings across all experimental groups.


### Structural biological analysis and drug screening
  To investigate critical targets involved in cell signaling, key proteins were selected for molecular docking studies. The three-dimensional structures of these target proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org). Protein–protein docking was performed using ZDOCK (https://zdock.wenglab.org), a rigid-body docking algorithm that predicts the binding modes of two proteins based on shape complementarity and electrostatics [46]. Resulting complexes were visualized and analyzed in PyMOL to identify key interaction residues.

  To evaluate the conformational dynamics of protein complexes, we employed two computational tools: iMODS (https://imods.iqf.csic.es) and CABS-flex 2.0 (https://biocomp.chem.uw.edu.pl/CABSflex2) [47, 48]. iMODS applies normal mode analysis (NMA) to estimate collective motions and structural stability. CABS-flex 2.0 calculates root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), which quantifies the average positional fluctuation of individual residues around their mean positions during molecular dynamics simulations and reflects the local structural flexibility of the protein.

  For small-molecule screening, Drugrep (http://cao.labshare.cn:10180/DrugRep/php/index.php) was used to predict potential TSHR-binding compounds from the FDA-approved drugs, based on ligand homology and binding site conservation [49]. Drugrep automatically identifies potential binding sites and sets the docking box parameters accordingly, streamlining the docking process. Molecular docking was then performed on the top three candidate ligands, and their binding poses and affinities were evaluated.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4·4·3) or Python (version 3·10·16). Group comparisons were assessed using Student’s t test for continuous variables. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR, with FDR < 0·05 considered significant unless otherwise stated. For rs-fMRI data analyses, multiple comparison correction was conducted within the whole brain, and significant clusters were identified using a voxel-level threshold of p < 0·01 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0·05 (Gaussian Random-Field correction) with a two-tailed test. MRlcroGL version 1·2·20220720 (https:/www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl) and BrainNet Viewer version1·7 (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/bnv) were used to visualize group differences and correlation results [50, 51]. For RNA-seq analyses, significance thresholds for DEGs were set at |log2 fold change | > 1 and adjusted p < 0·05. Statistical analyses specific to neuroimage and MR are described in the relevant sections above.


## Results

### Bidirectional MR analysis of GD, GO and IDPs
  Bidirectional MR analyses were conducted to explore the causal relationships between GD and GO with various categories of IDPs. These MR analyses constitute the primary layer of causal inference in this study, as they leverage genetic instruments to estimate directional effects between autoimmune traits and brain imaging phenotypes. Subsequent transcriptomic and cellular analyses are intended to provide biological interpretation of these genetically inferred associations.

  The effects of GD were localized. GD was predominantly associated with a negative causal relationship with cortical thickness in regions including the orbitofrontal, lingual, and insular cortices. Moreover, GD showed a negative causal relationship with node amplitude in a specific rs-fMRI component Independent Component Analysis (ICA) 38, and T2* signal in the right pallidum. Changes in white matter microstructure were also evident, with GD leading to a decrease in mode of anisotropy in the left cingulum (Fig. 2a; Additional file 1: Figs. S1–2; Additional file 2: Tabel S3–4). The cingulum is a major limbic white matter tract connecting the cingulate cortex with the hippocampus and prefrontal regions, and is critically involved in emotion regulation and cognitive control [52].   The effects of GO exhibited widespread and diverse causal effects on brain structural and functional traits. GO showed a positive causal relationship with cortical surface area in regions such as the right inferior parietal cortex and the occipital middle gyrus, while a negative causal relationship was observed in the entorhinal cortex. For cortical grey-white contrast, GO was causally linked to decreased contrast, particularly in the parietal, precentral, and cingulate regions. In terms of cortical thickness, GO was consistently associated with reduced thickness, especially in the fusiform, insular, lateral orbitofrontal, lingual, and cingulate cortices. Subcortical and non-T1 volumetric changes also reflected both positive and negative causal directions; for instance, GO was positively associated with volume in the bilateral pallidum, while showing negative associations with the entorhinal and medial orbitofrontal cortices. In white matter microstructure, GO demonstrated increased axial and mean diffusivity in the sagittal stratum and internal capsule, alongside a decrease in orientation dispersion, suggesting complex changes in white matter integrity. The sagittal stratum contains long association fibers linking occipital and temporal cortices, whereas the internal capsule conveys corticospinal and thalamocortical projections [53, 54]. Alterations in these tracts may reflect disrupted sensorimotor integration and visuospatial processing, consistent with the motor and visual dysfunction observed in advanced orbitopathy. Additionally, GO exhibited a positive causal association with resting-state functional connectivity, including elevated ICA component signals (Fig. 2b; Additional file 1: Figs. S3–4; Additional file 2: Tabel S5–6).

  Overall, these results indicate that both GD and GO involve alterations in cortical and subcortical brain structures, with GO exhibiting more extensive and pronounced changes, while GD effects remain relatively regionally restricted.


### Rs-fMRI and cross-species spatial transcriptomics validation
  To independently validate the causal brain signatures suggested by GWAS-based MR, we conducted rs-fMRI analyses using our in-house dataset. These neuroimaging analyses were designed to provide convergent phenotypic support for genetically inferred associations, rather than to establish direct mechanistic causality. For ALFF, significant group effects (GD vs. GO vs. HC) were detected in the right middle frontal gyrus, right superior temporal gyrus, left postcentral gyrus, and bilateral paracentral lobules. GD showed reduced ALFF in the anterior cingulate and insula compared with GO, consistent with MR evidence of cortical thinning in these regions. GO exhibited increased ALFF in the caudate, thalamus, and frontal cortices relative to HCs, in line with MR findings of subcortical enlargement. GD also showed increased ALFF in the paracentral lobule compared with HCs, suggestive of compensatory motor hyperactivity, in line with white matter alterations in the cingulum (Fig. 2c; Additional file 1: Fig. S5; Additional file 2: Tabel S7).

  For ReHo, group differences were widespread across frontal, parietal, temporal, and sensorimotor cortices. Both metrics converged on alterations in the paracentral lobule and middle frontal gyrus. GD showed higher ReHo in paracentral, parietal, and frontal regions compared with GO or HCs, while GO exhibited increased ReHo in the inferior temporal gyrus relative to HCs, paralleling MRI evidence of temporal cortical disruption (Fig. 2d; Additional file 1: Fig. S6; Additional file 2: Tabel S7).

  Overall, neuroimage analysis discovered that both ALFF and ReHo metrics revealed overlapping patterns of abnormalities across sensorimotor, frontal, and temporal cortices, indicating that alterations in both spontaneous neural activity and local synchrony occur in GD and GO. Furthermore, the MR analysis and neuroimaging findings were concordant. In GD, significant alterations were localized to the right anterior cingulate cortex, right insular cortex, left superior parietal lobule, and right middle frontal gyrus, highlighting these regions as key nodes of CNS involvement. These regions are implicated in emotional regulation, attention, and executive function, which may correspond to the subtle neuropsychological and affective changes occasionally observed in GD patients [55–57]. In contrast, GO exhibited a broader pattern, predominantly affecting the bilateral caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, and the right inferior temporal gyrus, suggesting extensive disruption of the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuit and temporal cortical regions, potentially underlying motor coordination, visuospatial processing, and higher-order cognitive impairments observed in patients with more severe orbital involvement (Fig. 2e–f) [58, 59].

  To place these findings in a developmental context, we integrated them with gsMap analysis of embryonic spatial transcriptomics (E16·5 mouse brain). Genes associated with GD from GWAS displayed strong spatial correlation with the choroid plexus, a key site for cerebrospinal fluid production and neuroimmune signaling (Fig. 2g). While cross-species differences limit direct translation, such developmental enrichment may suggest early-life neuroimmune susceptibility in GD. In contrast, GO did not show strong embryonic brain correlations, consistent with the notion that CNS alterations in GO are thought to arise after the onset of orbital manifestations, whereby changes such as proptosis and optic nerve compression impose secondary effects on the brain, rather than being present during early embryonic development. (Additional file 1: Fig. S7).

As shown in the Venn diagram, 178 genes were shared between GD and GO, while 220 genes were uniquely associated with GD and 11 genes were specific to GO (Fig. 3c). This overlap suggested partially shared genetic architecture between the two phenotypes, along with distinct trait-specific components.

  Gene Ontology enrichment analysis further identified the function of these genes. Shared genes between GD and GO were predominantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including “MHC class II protein complex assembly”, “lymphocyte-mediated immunity,” and “antigen processing and presentation”.   To explore the biological processes underlying these transcriptomic changes, Gene Ontology enrichment analyses were conducted for DEGs in each comparison. In the HC vs. GD comparison, significantly enriched pathways were primarily related to phospholipid metabolism and mitochondrial processes, reflecting early metabolic disruptions in GD (Fig. 4b). In the HC vs. GO contrast, in addition to phospholipid metabolism, enriched Gene Ontology terms included those involved in epithelial morphogenesis and reproductive system development, suggesting broader systemic involvement in GO (Fig. 4c). On the other side, up-regulated genes in GO were predominantly associated with branching morphogenesis, signal transduction, axonogenesis, and synapse organization, indicating impaired structural development and signal transduction (Fig. 4e).


### SMR and colocalization analyses prioritize candidate genes for GD and GO
  To further investigate gene-level causal mechanisms underlying GD and GO, we performed SMR analysis by integrating GWAS results with peripheral blood eQTL data from eQTLGen and multiple brain regions from GTEx. In GD, causal genes were identified across both central and peripheral compartments. In the brain, BTN3A2, ZSCAN26, and ZSCAN23 showed consistent associations across multiple GTEx tissues, LEMD2 displayed a GD-specific feature in the cerebellar hemisphere, and NBL1 was implicated in the caudate, cerebellum, and nucleus accumbens—regions tied to motor coordination and neuroimmune regulation. In peripheral blood, GD was associated with ICOS, BCL6, NRG1, RTKN2, and PRDM11, genes enriched in T cell costimulation and differentiation pathways. By contrast, GO showed a distinct signal, with CTLA4 emerging as a causal gene in peripheral blood, suggesting immune checkpoint dysregulation specific to the orbitopathy phase (Fig. 4f).

  Importantly, PTPN22 showed a causal relationship with GD in both peripheral blood and the cerebellum, while TSHR expression was causally associated with both GD and GO across multiple regions including peripheral blood, the caudate, and the putamen. These findings suggest that key immune genes such as PTPN22 and TSHR may exert systemic and central nervous system-mediated effects in disease etiology (Additional file 1: Figs. S15–17).

  To explore the functional relationships among these genes, a PPI network was constructed (Fig. 4g). The analysis revealed a highly connected module involving PTPN22, ICOS, BCL6, CTLA4, CEP126, and TSHR, all of which are known to regulate various aspects of T cell activation, differentiation, and immune tolerance. Another cluster involving ZSCAN23 and ZKSCAN3 suggests transcriptional regulatory interactions potentially relevant to immune cell lineage specification.

  To further assess whether GWAS and eQTL signals share a common causal variant, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis using the coloc.abf method. For TSHR, strong colocalization evidence was observed between eQTLGen blood eQTLs and GO GWAS signals (PP.H4 = 0.986), whereas only weak evidence was detected for GD (PP.H4 = 0.259). In contrast, PTPN22 did not show convincing colocalization with either GD or GO (PP.H4 < 0.01), suggesting that the SMR associations at this locus may reflect linkage rather than a shared causal variant (Additional file 2: Tabel S8; Fig. 4h).


### PBMC scRNA-seq revealed immune landscape changes of GD and GO
  To further explore the immune mechanisms and the functions of PTPN22 and TSHR in peripheral blood, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on PBMCs from the GD, GO, and HC groups. ## Discussion
  In this study, we systematically showed that organ-specific autoimmune disorders represented by GD and GO were associated with alterations in regional brain morphology and functional signaling. We identified GD-specific, GO-specific, and shared genes, and mapped their enrichment across brain regions, neural and peripheral immune cells. Notably, TSHR emerged as a shared hub gene spanning central and peripheral compartments, while PTPN22 was specific to GD. Colocalization analysis further refined these findings. Although PTPN22 showed significant SMR associations in GD, the lack of strong colocalization suggests potential pleiotropic effects or linkage rather than a single shared causal variant. In contrast, TSHR demonstrated robust colocalization between blood eQTLs and GO GWAS signals, supporting a shared causal variant driving both gene expression and orbitopathy susceptibility at this locus. Together, these results highlight distinct genetic architectures underlying GD and GO despite their shared autoimmune background. Moreover, FN1–TSHR and GNAS–TSHR interactions mediated communication among neurons, immune cells, and somatic cells in the putamen and caudate nuclei, the brain structures most prominently altered in GD and GO. These findings advance the concept of a “disease–molecule axis,” whereby susceptibility molecules such as TSHR act as entry points linking peripheral immune dysregulation and CNS pathology. This work represents the first systematic integration of brain alterations, target cells, gene variants, and transcriptomic data to uncover molecular hubs in autoimmune thyroid disease. Conceptually, our analytical framework is hierarchical: Mendelian randomization, SMR, and colocalization analyses provide genetic evidence for causal inference; bulk and single-cell transcriptomics offer cellular and pathway-level interpretation; neuroimaging supplies translational phenotypic support; and structural modeling explores potential molecular interaction feasibility. This layered design is intended to integrate complementary evidence streams while avoiding over-extension of inference beyond each method’s scope.

  GD and GO were selected as model endocrine autoimmune diseases because their relatively pathogenesis converges on TSHR-driven autoimmunity, providing a tractable paradigm to investigate neuroimmune mechanisms in organ-specific autoimmunity. Accumulating evidence has indicated that the TSHR is not confined to thyroidal and orbital tissues but is also expressed within the central nervous system, particularly in the amygdalae, cingulate gyri, frontal cortex and hippocampus, plays an important neurophysiological role in controlling emotion [60, 61]. TSHR is also identified to modulate angiogenesis in human dermal microvascular endothelial cell line [62], and cause CD8 + T cell exhaustion and immune invasion in colorectal cancer [63]. However, despite these mechanistic insights in non-autoimmune contexts, TSHR’s functional roles and the underlying cell-cell interactions have not been systematically investigated in the setting of autoimmune disease.

  TSHR may act as the molecular nexus across nervous system and immune system in GD and GO in our research. TSHR is known as a glycoprotein hormone receptor characterized by an extracellular α-subunit containing multiple leucine-rich repeats. Its transmembrane domain consists of seven α-helices, and the intracellular region activates adenylate cyclase via the Gs protein, promoting cAMP production [64]. Given the widespread expression of TSHR, it is present in multiple brain regions, potentially interacting with different ligands and signaling mechanisms, a finding supported by both previous studies and our current results [65–68]. Notably, emerging studies have suggested that additional synaptic regulatory molecules may also participate in eye–brain communication. For instance, Synaptotagmin-9 (SYT9), a calcium-dependent regulator of neurotransmitter release involved in GABAergic signaling, has been implicated in neuronal activity within visual and striatal circuits [69, 70]. Although SYT9 was not directly investigated in the present study, these observations are conceptually consistent with our findings highlighting striatal involvement and support the possibility that coordinated eye–brain interactions in GD and GO may extend beyond immune signaling to include neurotransmission-related mechanisms. Together, these findings support a bridging role of TSHR in neuroimmune crosstalk underlying GD and GO. Building on these insights, we propose the novel concept of a “disease–molecule axis,” in which susceptibility molecules such as TSHR act as pivotal entry points linking peripheral immune dysregulation and CNS pathology. Similarly, this neuroimmune pivot is also exemplified by the role of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD), as well as by the functions of myelin basic protein (MBP), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG), and proteolipid protein (PLP) in multiple sclerosis [71–73].

  New therapies for GD and GO are emerging, including the human monoclonal TSHR antibody K1-70™, as well as rituximab, tocilizumab, teprotumumab, and TSHR peptide immunotherapy [74–77]. These strategies underscore the therapeutic relevance of targeting TSHR. Our study extends this perspective by demonstrating that TSHR is expressed in both peripheral immune cells and specific CNS cell types, where it mediates neuroimmune interactions. This suggests that TSHR-targeted interventions may influence not only peripheral autoimmunity but also central processes. However, current clinical studies have not incorporated CNS alterations as an outcome measure. Furthermore, TSHR-targeted interventions may carry a risk of CNS-related side effects. Future trials should therefore evaluate both peripheral and central effects of TSHR-directed therapies, to fully capture their efficacy and safety profiles in GD and GO.

  Although previous studies have linked GD and GO to neurocognitive symptoms and brain imaging alterations, the underlying neural changes remain incompletely characterized. In this study, we verified these CNS changes and further explored their functional relevance, linking specific regional alterations to corresponding cognitive and emotional dysfunctions. Structural abnormality, biochemical changes and neuropsychiatric manifestations of GD and GO have been reported separately by a series of studies [57]. GD was depicted to have aberrant changes in frontal lobe, temporal lobe, parietal lobe and limbic system [78, 79], while GO was found to involve occipital lobe, frontal lobe, temporal lobe, insular cortex and limbic system [80–83]. This study confirmed frontal lobe deficits in both disorders and basal ganglia abnormalities in GO. Spatial transcriptomics implicated embryonic choroid plexus in GD pathogenesis, which was a new inspiration to existed theories. Both GD and GO involved brain regions associated with higher cognitive functions including emotion regulation, decision-making, reward processing, memory, and attention, as well as visual processing, interoception, and motor control [84–87]. Both GD and GO exhibited neural alterations, though GO demonstrated more extensive neural alterations and might specifically engage brain areas related to spatial cognition and sensory integration, likely reflecting the closer anatomical relationship of orbital pathology to the brain and potential involvement of the optic nerve.

  Notably, caudate nuclei and putamen were deciphered to be the key regions involved in GD and GO pathogenesis. The putamen is more prominently involved in motor regulation [88, 89], whereas the caudate nucleus shows stronger associations with cognitive functions such as decision-making and reward processing [89]. Emerging evidence has documented cognitive and motor impairments in GD and GO. The cognitive deficits primarily involve prefrontal, hippocampal, and limbic systems, manifesting as executive dysfunction and emotional disturbances [9, 82]. In contrast, motor abnormalities are associated with basal ganglia, particularly the putamen, and brainstem motor pathways, presenting with ocular motility disorders and generalized motor coordination deficits [90, 91]. SPNs represent the neuronal cell type most enriched for GD and GO gene expression, constituting the principal neuronal population (90 to 95%) within the striatum of the basal ganglia and play a central role in motor control, habit formation, decision-making, and reward processing [92–94]. The interplay between microglia and SPNs constitutes a vital regulatory mechanism in basal ganglia circuitry. These interactions occur both structurally through synaptic pruning and synaptic ensheathment, and functionally via inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species-dependent signaling [95–98]. A recent study demonstrated that microglial activation induced negative affective states through prostaglandin-mediated modulation of striatal neuronal activity [99]. Our data on microglia cells and SPNs substantiated the hypothesis concerning disease-related CNS immunity. It is important to acknowledge that the brain single-nucleus transcriptomic datasets used in this study were derived from non-disease tissues. As such, they reflect baseline cellular expression landscapes rather than dynamic transcriptional changes occurring during active GD or GO. Due to ethical and practical constraints, obtaining disease-stage human brain tissue from GD or GO patients remains highly challenging. Future studies incorporating disease-relevant CNS tissues, longitudinal neuroimaging-linked transcriptomics, or experimental models will be essential to further validate these neuroimmune mechanisms.

  In organ-specific autoimmune diseases, brain alterations may represent downstream effects of peripheral immune dysregulation, although this remains a hypothesis requiring further investigation. Clarifying the specific features of peripheral immune responses is therefore critical for better contextualizing CNS involvement. This study confirmed the central role of T cells in the autoimmune responses of GD and GO, and further reveals their function as primary expression vehicles for disease causal genes within the nervous system. GD and GO are driven by autoantigen TSHR and have considerable immune disorder like Th cell hyperproliferation and autoantibodies [100]. The present study corroborated previous findings regarding alterations in peripheral immune cell subsets [101]. Analyses of pivotal pathogenic genes (PTPN22 and TSHR) revealed T cell subsets as the most strongly correlated immune populations. ScRNA analysis further supported the significant role of T cell subsets in GD and GO. In GD, multiple genes exhibited robust causal relationships across diverse brain regions involved in neuroimmune regulation, which also partaked in T cell co-stimulation, transcriptional regulation of T follicular helper cells, and cytokine-mediated signaling in peripheral blood. In GO, the significant association between negative regulation of T cell activation and disease in peripheral blood suggests potential immune checkpoint dysregulation. Further exploration discovered that T cell activation, differentiation, and immune tolerance were enriched in gene modules significantly causal associations with GD and GO in both peripheral and central tissues.

  From a structural biology viewpoint, our docking and dynamics simulations suggest the ligand-binding potential of TSHR and nominate three clinically approved drugs (venetoclax, irinotecan and dutasteride) as candidate modulators with favorable in silico profiles. These computational findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature, and do not establish direct biochemical binding or therapeutic efficacy. Venetoclax has been reported in a case of hypothyroidism during treatment, hinting at unexpected thyroid-axis interactions [102]. Irinotecan, used in solid tumour chemotherapy, has seen exploratory use in thyroid-cancer regimens, though not as a TSHR-directed agent [103]. Dutasteride has demonstrated endocrine-modulating effects in prostate-related studies, with possible implications for thyroid regulation [104]. However, it should be emphasized that these findings are derived from computational modeling rather than direct biochemical or pharmacological testing. Functional validation experiments, such as cAMP signaling assays, receptor activation or inhibition tests, and direct ligand-binding assays, will be necessary to determine whether these compounds can modulate TSHR activity in cellular or physiological contexts. Future studies integrating biochemical binding assays and cell-based functional analyses will therefore be essential to evaluate the therapeutic potential suggested by our computational predictions.

  This study has several limitations. First, although analyses of large population cohorts and patient-derived samples in multi-omics integration suggested potential neuropathogenic mechanisms, the lack of experimental validation in disease models limits deeper mechanistic insights. In particular, the limited sample size of the single-cell datasets confers an exploratory character to the cellular analyses, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to broader patient populations. Second, limited clinical data and the absence of longitudinal follow-up—particularly regarding neuropsychiatric symptoms and quality of life—restrict phenotypic interpretation. Third, our analyses were centered on GD, GO, and TSHR as a representative paradigm for neuroimmune alterations; however, this focus inherently narrows the scope of inference. Other autoimmune diseases and immune mediators beyond TSHR may also shape brain–immune interactions, and the extent of these broader mechanisms remains largely unexplored. Future work will focus on refining its functional roles, identifying mechanistic mediators, and exploring how neural-immune interactions influence physiology and cognition. Finally, while the expression of TSHR and its ligand colocalization within the basal ganglia were rigorously validated through histological analysis, direct comparative fMRI analysis between mice and humans was not performed in the present study. Future imaging studies are warranted to verify the consistency of functional alterations across wider brain regions and establish cross-species correspondence.


## Conclusions
  This study systematically maps the neuroimmune landscape of TSHR-drive autoimmunity through integrated analyses of genetic, transcriptomic, and neuroimaging data. We identify distinct yet overlapping brain alterations in GD and GO, with TSHR emerging as a key molecule linking peripheral autoimmunity to central nervous system changes. Our findings support the existence of a TSHR-mediated axis involving spiny projection neurons, microglia, and immune cells, providing new insights into the pathogenesis of neuroimmune alteration in autoimmune diseases. These results lay the groundwork for future experimental validation and may inform the development of neuroimmune-targeted therapies.
