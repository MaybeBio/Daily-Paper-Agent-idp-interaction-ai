# Computational identification of conserved dengue virus envelope protein epitopes for vaccine design and immunodiagnostic platform


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37744-z.


## Introduction
  Dengue has become a major global health challenge in recent decades, causing a significant and increasing disease burden worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) lists dengue among the top ten global public health threats, and it is now recognized as the fastest-spreading mosquito-borne viral disease1. Global incidence has surged dramatically in recent decades: reported dengue cases increased from about 500,000 in 2000 to 14.6 million in 2024, the highest annual total on record. Modeling studies estimate that approximately 390 million dengue infections occur each year, with nearly half of the global population living in areas at risk. Dengue is endemic in over 100 countries and causes periodic epidemics that strain public health systems, highlighting the urgent need for improved preventive and diagnostic measures2.

  A major challenge in dengue control is the viral diversity of its four serotypes (DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, DENV-4). Each serotype can cause symptoms ranging from mild fever to severe, life-threatening illness. Recovery from one serotype provides lifelong protection against that specific serotype, but not against the others1. In fact, sequential infection by a different serotype can predispose individuals to severe dengue due to immunopathological mechanisms such as antibody-dependent enhancement2. This phenomenon, combined with > 30% amino acid differences between serotypes leading to distinct antigenic profiles, means that cross-protective immunity is difficult to achieve.

  Effective vaccines and treatments remain limited. There is currently no specific antiviral therapy for dengue3. Vector control (targeting the Aedes mosquitoes that transmit dengue) can reduce transmission but has proven challenging to sustain. Vaccine development for dengue has been notably difficult because an ideal vaccine must safely induce robust immunity against all four serotypes simultaneously. To date, only a couple of dengue vaccines have been licensed, and their use is constrained. The first-generation vaccine showed limited efficacy and safety in seronegative individuals, being recommended only for certain ages and those with prior dengue infection. A newer live-attenuated tetravalent vaccine has demonstrated protection and gained approval in some regions, but its deployment is still in early stages. Overall, current vaccines do not yet offer broad, long-lasting protection across all serotypes4. This gap highlights the need for alternative vaccine design strategies that can elicit a balanced immune response to DENV-1–4.

  Accurate diagnostics for dengue are another pressing need, particularly tools that can pinpoint which serotype is causing an infection. Early diagnosis is vital for patient management and outbreak control, yet traditional methods have significant shortcomings. Molecular tests (e.g. RT-PCR) are effective only in the narrow window of viremia, while widely used serological tests (detection of dengue-specific IgM/IgG or NS1 antigen) suffer from cross-reactivity. Antibodies raised against one dengue serotype or related flaviviruses (such as Zika virus) can cross-react in these assays, leading to false positives or an inability to distinguish serotypes4. Such cross-reactive immune responses make it difficult to definitively identify the infecting serotype using conventional immunoassays. Therefore, there is a strong incentive to discover unique antigenic signatures for each DENV serotype that could be harnessed in next-generation diagnostic tests, improving specificity in endemic areas where multiple flaviviruses co-exist4.

  Immunoinformatics offers a powerful approach to address these challenges by identifying viral epitopes that could be targeted in vaccines or diagnostics. The dengue virion’s envelope (E) protein is an attractive focus for such analyses, as it is the major surface glycoprotein responsible for virus entry into host cells and a principal target of the immune response (including neutralizing antibodies and T cells). Importantly, T lymphocytes (both CD4⁺ helper and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells) play a critical role in dengue immunity by killing infected cells and modulating the antiviral response. However, the specific T-cell epitopes that could confer broad protection or aid in differentiating serotypes remain insufficiently characterized. Mapping these epitopes experimentally is labor-intensive given dengue’s diversity, but computational methods can rapidly screen the viral proteome to pinpoint promising candidates. By comparing the genomes of all four DENV serotypes, one can identify regions of the E protein that are highly conserved (identical or very similar in sequence) or, conversely, regions that are unique to each serotype. Such analysis can reveal conserved epitopes shared across DENV1–4 that might form the basis of a universal T-cell vaccine, as well as type-specific epitopes that serve as molecular fingerprints of each serotype.

  In this study, we employed a comprehensive in silico strategy to identify and characterize T-cell epitopes from the dengue E protein across all four serotypes. Our analysis was designed with a dual purpose: (1) to discover conserved T-cell epitopes present in all DENV serotypes, which are candidates for a universal tetravalent dengue vaccine, and (2) to find serotype-unique epitopes absent from the other serotypes, which could be developed into highly specific immunodiagnostic tools. Ultimately, the computational approach and findings presented here underscore a promising pathway to develop a broadly protective dengue vaccine and more accurate serotype-specific diagnostic assays, addressing key gaps in the fight against this re-emerging global disease. ## Method and materials
  In this study, we adopted a comprehensive immunoinformatics framework aligned with standard vaccine design pipelines, as described in recent literature5. The workflow integrates evolutionary screening, epitope prediction, and structural validation. Specifically, we performed (i) evolutionary analysis via high-fidelity multiple sequence alignment (MSA) to map conserved regions; (ii) epitope identification coupled with rigorous safety profiling (toxicity, allergenicity, and human homology); and (iii) structural validation, utilizing AlphaFold modeling, molecular docking, and Normal Mode Analysis (NMA). Unlike pipeline stages focused solely on chimeric vaccine construction with linkers, our protocol was optimized to validate individual epitope-HLA stability, ensuring the applicability of the selected candidates for both multi-epitope vaccine formulations and peptide-based diagnostic assays.


### Retrieval and analysis of protein sequences and human homology evaluation
  The complete amino acid sequences of the E protein of DENV 1, 2, 3, and 4 were aligned using Clustal Omega 1.2.4 software implemented in Jalview 2.11.2 software6,7.

  To minimize the risk of autoimmune cross-reactivity or immune tolerance, a human homology analysis was conducted for all sequences. The BLASTp module of the NCBI BLAST server was used to compare each epitope sequence against the Homo sapiens proteome (taxid: 9606)8. An E-value threshold of 0.05 was applied to evaluate sequence similarity. Peptides showing no significant matches (E-value > 0.05) to human proteins were considered non-homologous and were therefore selected as candidate epitopes for further immunoinformatics analyses.


### Subcellular localization, signal peptide, and post-translational modification (PTM) prediction
  The E protein sequences from DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 were analyzed using TargetP 2.0, accessed on 1 October 2025 and SignalP 6.0, accessed on 1 October 20259,10. The sequences were submitted in FASTA format (min length ≥ 10) to the servers’ default options; in TargetP 2.0, the Non-plant organism group and Long output were chosen. These tools predict the presence of subcellular localization and signal peptides patterns through deep learning models trained on experimentally validated datasets.

  In addition, transmembrane helix prediction was performed using the TMHMM 2.0 and standard v2 (default values) (accessed on 1 October 2025) to identify potential membrane-spanning regions within the E proteins11.


### Analysis of physicochemical parameter
  The sequences of DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 were analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam online tool (accessed on 15 October 2023)12. This tool is designed to calculate various physical and chemical parameters for a specific protein stored in Swiss-Prot or TrEMBL or for a user-entered sequence. The parameters calculated include molecular weight, theoretical pI (isoelectric point), amino acid composition, atomic composition, extinction coefficient, estimated half-life, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average hydropathy (GRAVY). These parameters provide a comprehensive understanding of the properties of the protein.


### Prediction of T cell epitopes
  T cell epitopes for MHC class I were predicted using the NetMHCI 4.0 server (accessed on 17 October 2023), designed to identify epitopes with MHC I binding affinity in sequences. NetMHCI employs artificial neural networks, trained with affinity data from the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource (IEDB), covering a broad range of MHC alleles. This method enhances prediction accuracy through position-specific scoring matrices, making it a reliable tool for peptide-MHC interaction analysis13. In this study, a representative panel of 34 HLA class I alleles was selected for prediction, including HLA-A*01:01, HLA-A*02:01, HLA-A*02:04, HLA-A*02:11, HLA-A*02:19, HLA-A*24:02, HLA-A*24:03, HLA-A*26:01, HLA-A*31:01, HLA-A*68:01, HLA-B*07:02, HLA-B*08:01, HLA-B*15:01, HLA-B*15:04, HLA-B*15:07, HLA-B*27:05, HLA-B*35:01, HLA-B*35:04, HLA-B*35:05, HLA-B*35:11, HLA-B*35:18, HLA-B*39:01, HLA-B*39:03, HLA-B*39:05, HLA-B*39:06, HLA-B*39:09, HLA-B*40:02, HLA-B*40:04, HLA-B*48:01, HLA-B*48:03, HLA-B*51:01, HLA-B*51:04, HLA-B*52:01, HLA-B*58:0114. These alleles collectively represent the major supertypes (A1, A2, A3, A24, A26, B7, B8, B27, B39, B44, B58, B62), covering the most frequent HLA distributions across global populations.

  The same set of alleles was consistently applied in the NetCTL 1.2 server and the peptides identified by NetMHCI were further verified using the NetCTL 1.2 server (accessed on 17 October 2023). NetCTL 1.2 integrates predictions of peptide binding to MHC class I, C-terminal proteasome cleavage, and antigen transport-associated protein (TAP) transport efficiency. This server predicts cytotoxic T cell (CTL) epitopes restricted to 12 MHC class I supertypes, using artificial neural networks for MHC class I binding and proteasomal cleavage predictions, and a weight matrix for TAP transport efficiency15.

  For MHC class II, T-cell epitopes were predicted using the NetMHCII 2.3 tool (accessed on 17 October 2023). This tool utilizes artificial neural networks to predict peptide binding to various HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, HLA-DP, and mouse MHC class II alleles. It supports predictions for 25 HLA-DR alleles, 20 HLA-DQ, 9 HLA-DP, and 7 mouse H2 class II alleles, with results expressed in nM IC50 values and percent rank compared to a set of 1,000,000 random natural peptides16. The Immune Epitope Database’s MHCII tool (accessed on 17 October 2023) was employed to confirm these epitopes. IEDB MHCII allows users to input protein sequences in FASTA format and choose from various prediction methods, including several versions of prediction algorithms and the combinatorial Library Sturniolo16,17. In this study, we selected a representative panel of 11 HLA-DRB1*01:01, HLA-DRB1*03:01, HLA-DRB1*04:01, HLA-DRB1*07:01, HLA-DRB1*08:02, HLA-DRB1*09:01, HLA-DRB1*11:01, HLA-DRB1*13:02, HLA-DRB1*14:02, HLA-DRB1*15:01, HLA-DRB1*16:01), which collectively cover the major HLA-DR supertypes relevant to global populations18.

  The CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell epitopes were selected for immunodiagnostic purposes based on several critical criteria: (i) exclusivity for each subtype, ensuring specificity; (ii) demonstrated significant antigenic properties and (iii) demonstrated high immunogenic potential. In addition, for epitopes to be considered in vaccine development for all serotypes, they must meet the following requirements: (i) ubiquity in each subtype to ensure broad applicability; (ii) significant antigenic properties; (iii) robust immunogenicity; (iv) absence of allergenicity; and (v) non-toxicity.

  The antigenicity of the E proteins from all dengue virus subtypes was predicted using the Vaxijen v2.0 server (accessed on 17 October 2023), which applies the auto-cross-covariance (ACC) transformation of protein sequences19. For allergenicity assessment, the AllerTOP server (accessed on 17 October 2023) was employed, analyzing protein sequences based on ACC, focusing on hydrophobicity, size, and flexibility20. The ToxinPred server (accessed on 17 October 2023) was also used to evaluate the potential toxicity of the proteins by comparing their amino acid sequences with a database of known toxic and non-toxic peptides21. In addition, immunogenicity was assessed using two tools from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB): the IEDB T cell Class I Immunogenicity tool (accessed on 17 October 2023), designed to predict the immunogenicity of peptides bound to MHC Class I molecules (CD8⁺ T-cell), and the IEDB CD4episcore tool (on 17 October 2023), which predicts the likelihood of peptides binding to MHC Class II molecules and their potential to elicit a CD4⁺ T-cell response22,23. Finally, the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) (accessed on 10 October 2025) was used for conservancy analysis, which plays a key role in the development of peptide-based broad-spectrum vaccine against a specific virus. This tool determines the level of conservation of an epitope within a given protein sequence24.


### Prediction B-cell epitopes
  Linear B-cell epitopes for DENV were identified using the antigenicity scale of Kolaskar and Tongaonkar (accessed on 20 October 2023)25. This scale is a semiempirical method for epitope prediction that has an accuracy rate greater than 75%. Peptides that met or exceeded a threshold of 1.05 were considered potential antigenic epitopes. Sequences classified as antigenic for DENV using the Kolaskar and Tongaonkar scales were confirmed for further evaluation on another online platform, VaxiJen (accessed on 20 October 2023)19,25.

  Linear B-cell epitopes were selected for immunodiagnostic applications based on the following parameters: (i) exclusivity of subtype, ensuring specificity, and (ii) demonstrated significant antigenic properties. For the development of a vaccine effective against all serotypes, epitopes must also meet the following conditions: (i) commonality among subtypes to ensure broad efficacy, and (ii) significant antigenic properties.


### Population coverage evaluation
  Population coverage serves as a key measure of vaccine effectiveness across different geographic regions, reflecting the frequency of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles that interact with the epitopes under study. To estimate the population coverage of the selected epitopes, the IEDB Population Coverage tool (accessed on 08 October 2025) was used. This tool accounts for the distribution of MHC-binding alleles, enabling the identification of peptides capable of binding to a broad range of HLA molecules. In this study, which focused on the Latin American population, population coverage was determined based on the proportion of individuals predicted to mount immune responses to epitopes binding both MHC-I and MHC-II alleles26.


### Homology modelling and tertiary structure refinement and validation
  The 3D building models were performed using Alphafold (accessed on 25 October 2023), which enables the construction of 5 final models based on homology modeling. The 3D structures were refined using the GalaxyRefine tool (accessed on 26 October 2023)27. This server is based on a refinement method that performs short molecular dynamics relaxations (MD) after repeated side-chain repack perturbations, allowing larger motions. The validation of the models was conducted using PDBsum server (accessed on 09 October 2025)28.


### Prediction of discontinuous B-cell epitopes
  The DiscoTope tool (accessed on 20 October 2023) was used to predict discontinuous B-cell epitopes from the three-dimensional structures of proteins. This tool calculates surface accessibility estimated in terms of contact numbers and a novel epitope propensity amino acid score29.


### Predictive analysis of disordered regions and secondary structural protein
  In the context of immunodiagnostics, peptides located within disordered regions are of particular interest. Their inherent flexibility, not being constrained within rigid protein structures, makes them more accessible for antibody interactions, potentially enhancing their utility in diagnostic applications. Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) for DENV peptides were predicted using the Predictor of Natural Disordered Regions (PONDR) tool (accessed on 26 October 2023)30. PONDR employs artificial neural networks to anticipate these IDRs in proteins.

  The Garnier–Osguthorpe–Robson (GOR) tool was utilized to analyze the secondary structure of the DENV 1, 2, 3 and 4. This tool employs the principles of information theory and Bayesian statistics for the examination of secondary protein structures. The objective of integrating multiple sequence alignments with the GOR method is to enhance our understanding and facilitate better differentiation of secondary structures.


### Molecular docking and CTL modeling analysis
  To evaluate the binding affinity, selected MHC-I epitopes were docked with their corresponding HLA alleles. The 3D structures of the CTL epitopes were modeled using the PEP-FOLD4 server (accessed on 10 October 2025), which applies a de novo approach optimized for short linear peptides (5–50 residues)31. The five top-ranked conformations were generated, and the energy of each structure was determined using SWISS-PDB VIEWER, and the model with the least energy was selected for subsequent assessments32. The human HLA alleles HLA-B*39:01 (PDB ID: 9C6X), HLA-A*01:01 (PDB ID: 6AT9), HLA-B*40:02 (PDB ID: 5IEK), and HLA-A*26:01 (PDB ID: 8XES) were considered for MHC-I docking, corresponding respectively to the epitopes LKYSVIVTV, LTDYGALTL, AETQHGTVL, and DTAWDFGSI. The crystal structures of the selected alleles were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank in PDB format33. Protein preparation was performed using the Discovery Studio 2016 Protein Preparation Wizard by removing bound ligands and water molecules. Docking simulations were carried out using the HADDOCK 2.4 web server (accessed on 10 October 2025), and molecular interactions were visualized and analyzed through Discovery Studio. Due to the limited availability of crystallographic structures for certain HLA alleles, docking was restricted to epitopes with experimentally resolved HLA templates available in the PDB34,35.


#### Normal mode simulation
  The iMODS online server (accessed on 1 October 2025) was used to perform normal mode analysis (NMA) and predict the atomic motions of the docked antigen–receptor complex. This approach provides insights into the intrinsic flexibility, stability, and collective conformational changes of the complex by evaluating its deformability, B-factor, eigenvalue, and covariance matrix36.


## Results and discussion

### Protein sequence and analysis
  Sequence analysis revealed that the genomic identity among the four DENV serotypes ranges from 63.84 to 77.37%, confirming previous reports that DENV-1 to DENV-4 share approximately 65–70% genome similarity due to conserved regions within the envelope (E) protein that are essential for vaccine design37. The amino acid sequences used for alignment were retrieved from the NCBI database: DENV-1 (Accession: NP_722460.2; 2493 sequences), DENV-2 (NP_739583.2; 1835 sequences), DENV-3 (YP_001531168.2; 963 sequences), and DENV-4 (NP_740317.1; 53 sequences) (see Table S1).

  Distinct sequence variations were identified for each subtype, while conserved motifs were maintained across the envelope (E) protein, particularly in the central and C-terminal domains involved in receptor binding and immune recognition, which are critical for the rational design of broad-spectrum dengue vaccine candidates. ### Determination of physical parameters of the proteins
  The physicochemical properties of E proteins of DENV types 1, 2, 3, and 4 were calculated using a web-based server. The proteins consist of 495, 495, 493, and 495 amino acids for types 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. The molecular weights range from 53,850.12, 54,438.04, 53,681.72 and 53,988.10 to Daltons for types 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively. ### Epitopes across serotypes
  The identification and in-depth analysis of conserved epitopes in different dengue virus serotypes, represents a major advance in the field of vaccine development. These epitopes, capable of eliciting strong immune responses, pave the way for the development of innovative vaccines that provide comprehensive protection against the different DENV serotypes. A study conducted on the comparative genomics of DENV identified 100% conserved epitopes in the Envelope protein and other non-structural proteins across all DENV serotypes39. In addition to the above, we employed immunoinformatics and computational vaccinology to develop multi-epitope-based vaccines, focusing on both SARS-CoV-2 and Mayaro virus. This work showcases our expertise and innovative approach in the field of vaccine development, demonstrating our ability to tackle diverse viral threats effectively40–42.

  A review of recent advances in the development of dengue virus vaccines has shown the complexity of immune responses against the four antigenically distinct DENV serotypes43. Such a strategy is particularly important for dengue control, as the virus is divided into four distinct serotypes, each of which affects the efficacy of single-strain vaccines. The remarkable ability of these universal epitopes to elicit strong immune responses, particularly the production of neutralizing antibodies, greatly enhances the prospects for the development of more effective and durable dengue vaccines. This breakthrough in the identification of cross-serotype epitopes improves our understanding of the immune response to dengue and sets a new standard for future vaccine formulations.

  Bashyam and colleagues reported that the E213 (FLDLPLPWL) epitope is conserved across all dengue serotypes, eliciting a response in Dengue Virus-specific CD8+ T cells44. However, our studies indicate that this conservation extends only to DENV-1, DENV-2, and DENV-4. This discrepancy highlights the evolutionary dynamics of the virus, as variants emerge over the years43. Furthermore, our analysis shows a marked decrease in the antigenicity of the DENV-2 epitope compared to DENV-1 and DENV-4, following the L221T mutation, suggesting that this mutation selectively affects antigenicity within these serotypes.

  The epitopes (DENV-157, DENV-4135, DENV-1213, DENV-4313, DENV-2417, and DENV-2447) offer broad serotype coverage, high predicted immunogenicity, and a low risk of adverse reactions. Conservancy analysis confirms their presence, with minimal conservative substitutions, in DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4 sequences, meeting a key objective of pan-serotype vaccine design. Their high antigenicity scores and predicted MHC-binding affinities indicate strong potential for immune stimulation. All are predicted to be non-allergenic and non-toxic by in silico tools, suggesting they should be safe as peptide immunogens. These characteristics, such as conserved sequence, strong antigenicity, appropriate length, and favorable biochemical properties support their inclusion in a potential multi-epitope dengue vaccine construct.

### Exclusive epitopes for immunodiagnostic precision
  Immunoinformatics has transformed viral diagnostics by improving the identification of epitopes crucial for the development of targeted monoclonal antibodies and diagnostics. This technology has led to the production of specific antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 that do not show cross-reactivity with similar viruses as they focus on the nucleocapsid protein39. Furthermore, it has enabled the generation of peptide-based proteomic microarrays to characterize the full epitope profile of SARS-CoV-2, which supports the development of diagnostics45.

  In addition, immunoinformatics has been instrumental in the development of a monoclonal antibody-based biosensor for SARS-CoV-2 that has high accuracy in clinical samples46. In parallel to these advances, antigenic peptides of Rocio and Mayaro viruses have been successfully predicted, improving the differentiation of these infections in regions affected by multiple arboviruses47,48. Taken together, these breakthroughs underline the crucial role of immunoinformatics in the field of diagnostics, especially for complex pathogens with different strains.

### Normal mode simulation
  The normalized eigenvalue distributions for the four epitope–HLA complexes (Figs. 9, 10, 11 and 12A) reveal marked differences in their rigidity. Normal mode analysis shows that higher eigenvalues correspond to stiffer (less deformable) modes, whereas lower eigenvalues indicate softer, more flexible motions56. In our data, the HLA-A01:01–LTDYGALTL and HLA-A26:01–DTAWDFGSI complexes exhibited the largest first-mode eigenvalues, suggesting they are relatively rigid. In contrast, HLA-B40:02–AETQHGTVL showed the smallest first-mode eigenvalue (and HLA-B39:01–LKYSVIVTV was intermediate), implying enhanced deformability. Thus, HLA-A01:01–LTDYGALTL and HLA-A26:01–DTAWDFGSI have the highest stiffness (highest eigenvalues), while HLA-B40:02–AETQHGTVL and HLA-B39:01–LKYSVIVTV have the lowest (highest flexibility). This inverse relationship between eigenvalue magnitude and flexibility is consistent with theory. The low-frequency (low-eigenvalue) modes generally correspond to large-scale, collective motions, whereas high-frequency modes are localized vibrations around a stable conformation56. Across all complexes, we observe strong correlated motions (warm colors) within each HLA domain, and between the peptide and nearby helices, reflecting concerted domain movements. By contrast, HLA-B*40:02–AETQHGTVL displays more patchy correlation patterns with pronounced anti-correlations (cool colors) between different segments.

#### Limitations of the study
  In the landscape of recent immunoinformatics research, methodological strategies vary significantly depending on the volume of genomic sequences analyzed. For instance, while Roohparvar Basmenj et al. analyzed nearly 380,000 SARS-CoV-2 sequences, necessitating the development of a custom in-house Java program to calculate epitope repeat rates due to the massive volume of data57. In contrast, our study analyzed approximately 5,300 DENV sequences, a dataset size constrained by the availability of high-quality complete genomes for specific serotypes (e.g., DENV-4). While we did not employ custom high-throughput software, the scale of our dataset allowed for the utilization of the standardized IEDB Conservancy Analysis tool following Clustal Omega alignment. This protocol was specifically selected to ensure robust identity mapping, allowing for the precise differentiation between pan-serotype conservation (required for vaccines) and serotype-specific divergence (required for diagnostics), a level of granular analysis that is well-supported by standardized tools for datasets of this magnitude.

  In silico tools have inherent accuracy limitations: predicted high-affinity binders may not elicit T-cell responses, and our analysis does not account for antigen processing or post-translational modifications. Moreover, structural modeling (docking and normal-mode dynamics) simplifies the true flexibility of peptide–HLA–TCR interactions. Although HADDOCK includes short molecular-dynamics–based refinement during docking (semi-flexible torsion-angle refinement and final explicit-solvent refinement) and we complemented docking with iMODS normal mode analysis to probe collective motions and intrinsic flexibility, we did not perform long-timescale explicit-solvent all-atom molecular dynamics production runs (e.g., ≥ 100 ns). Therefore, long-term conformational stability and dynamic descriptors such as RMSD/RMSF profiles, hydrogen-bond occupancy, and energetic convergence remain to be assessed in future work.

  Our sequence analysis, while extensive, was also constrained by data availability (for example, only 53 DENV-4 E sequences were available), which may bias conservancy estimates. Critically, as noted in similar immunoinformatic studies, all predicted vaccine epitopes require experimental validation. In addition, dengue immunity is complex: prior exposure shapes T-cell reactivity in ways that our model cannot predict. Bashyam et al. have shown that even single-residue differences in epitopes from heterologous serotypes can change the magnitude and quality of CD8+ T-cell cytokine responses44. Thus, while our conserved epitopes are promising candidates, their true immunogenicity and protective efficacy remain to be verified through laboratory experiments.

  Although HADDOCK includes short molecular-dynamics–based refinement during docking (semi-flexible torsion-angle refinement and final explicit-solvent refinement) and we complemented docking with iMODS normal mode analysis to probe collective motions and intrinsic flexibility, we did not perform long-timescale explicit-solvent all-atom molecular dynamics production runs (e.g., ≥ 100 ns). Therefore, long-term conformational stability and dynamic descriptors such as RMSD/RMSF profiles, hydrogen-bond occupancy, and energetic convergence remain to be assessed in future work.

  These limitations, from predictive tool biases to the absence of wet-lab validation, are important caveats of our computational framework.


## Conclusion
  This study provides an extensive computational analysis of the envelope protein from four dengue virus serotypes, with a focus on identifying conserved T cell epitopes that are critical for effective immune recognition and response. Our findings reveal a significant conservation of certain CD8⁺ (E57, E135, E175, E313, and E417) and CD4+ (E133, E134, and E234) epitopes that maintain antigenic and immunogenic properties despite sequence mutations. This conservation is indicative of a robust cellular immune response that is capable of recognizing and responding to various serotypes, offering a beacon of hope for the development of a universal dengue vaccine with broad efficacy. While this study focused on the identification and structural validation of individual epitopes to support both vaccine and diagnostic applications, future studies should focus on the construction of a multi-epitope chimeric vaccine using flexible linkers and adjuvants, following the validated candidates identified here.

  In the study of the immunodiagnostic framework of dengue virus, different epitopes were identified for each serotype, in particular for DENV-2 CD8+ (CD4 +) E424 (E236, E267); DENV-3 CD8+ (CD4 +) E274, E204, E311, E410, E350 (E229); and DENV-4 CD8+ (CD4 +),E65, E446 (E403, E402). These results underline the transformative potential of immunoinformatics in improving the precision of diagnostics. By revealing serotype-specific epitopes and their antigenic and immunogenic shifts, we are paving the way for the development of diagnostic and therapeutic interventions with superior specificity and efficacy. Molecular docking and normal mode analyses confirmed the structural stability and favorable energetics of epitope–HLA complexes, particularly for LTDYGALTL–HLA-A*01:01 and DTAWDFGSI–HLA-A*26:01, which exhibited optimal binding conformations and strong intermolecular interactions within the peptide-binding groove.

  In conclusion, our study improves the understanding of the immunological dynamics of dengue virus and provides valuable insights that could inform future vaccine strategies and diagnostic tools and bring the medical community closer to combating this widespread tropical disease.
