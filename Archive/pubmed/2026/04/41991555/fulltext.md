# Folded domains impose structural heterogeneity and attenuated dynamics in biomolecular condensates


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates are widely studied using simplified analogues formed by purified low complexity domains (LCDs) or intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). However, the exclusion of folded domains fails to capture natural molecular complexity and limits their physiological relevance. Here, we performed molecular dynamics simulations to systematically investigate the internal structure of condensates formed by full-length multidomain proteins (MDPs) in comparison to IDR-only systems. In contrast to uniformly expanded conformations observed in IDR-only condensates during tens of microseconds simulation, MDP-based systems exhibit significant heterogeneity. The heterogeneous microenvironment imposed by folded domains results in component-specific coupling and a wide range of conformations sampled by the IDRs. Strikingly, residue interaction specificity remains conserved between the condensed and solution states, even in the presence of MDPs. Finally, both local and global dynamics are attenuated in MDP-based condensates, opposing the heterogeneous retardation and unperturbed local dynamics observed in IDR-only systems. Our findings propose folded domains to act as architectural keystones in natural condensates, and enrich our understanding of their internal structural and dynamic properties.


## Introduction
  Biomolecular condensates, membraneless organelles formed via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), have emerged as an important organizer of cellular biochemistry over the past decade1–5. These dynamic compartments spatially concentrate proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites to regulate processes such as transcriptional control6–9, RNA processing4,10–12, and stress responses13–16. Furthermore, aberrant condensate formation is implicated in numerous cellular pathologies, including neurodegenerative diseases as well as cancer17–22. Therefore, elucidating the internal structural and dynamic properties of condensates is of primary importance to understand their roles in cellular physiology and potential as therapeutic targets23–26.

  To date, much of our understanding of condensate formation and organization has been derived from simplified in vitro and computational models, employing IDR-only condensates formed by purified low complexity domains (LCDs) or intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). These systems provide fundamental insights into phase separation mechanisms, enabling the characterization of condensate behavior through frameworks such as sticker-and-spacer model and phase separation coupled to percolation (PSCP)27–29, and linking the propensity of LLPS to protein sequence30. Additionally, network-like structures with inhomogeneous spatial organization and small-world-like topologies were illustrated within IDR-only condensates31,32. However, there is still a large gap to capture the molecular complexity of endogenous condensates, which are rarely governed by disordered regions alone. Instead, natural condensates incorporate multi-domain proteins (MDPs) with folded domains that contribute critical interaction interfaces, encode specific client molecule recruitment, or introduce structural rigidity through stable interaction networks and large excluded volume33–36. For example, mutations in or deletion of the dimerization domains of G3BP1 and PGL1 block the assembly of stress granules and P granules, respectively, highlighting the functional importance of folded domains in condensate formation16,37. Additionally, folded RNA-binding domains play essential roles in recruiting RNAs and regulating RNA processing in different Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) granules4,10,12. These observations underscore the need to integrate folded domains into condensate studies to bridge the gap between IDR-only models and physiological reality.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful tool to reveal the phase separation behavior and structural organization of condensates29,38,39. These simulations can operate at varying resolutions, from full atomistic detail to simplified descriptions using a coarse-grained (CG) representation40–43. For studying natural biomolecular condensate systems containing MDPs, characterized by large spatial scales and increased relaxation dynamics, atomistic simulations become computationally prohibitive. In such cases, CG models are crucial by reducing computational costs while retaining critical physico-chemical features44,45. For instance, CG simulations of the folded RNA recognition motifs (RRM) within hnRNPA1 revealed modification of the phase behavior by electrostatic interplay with its disordered LCD46, and condensation of RNA-binding protein TDP-43 was proposed to be governed by a diverse set of interactions at the domain level47. Furthermore, simulations of N130 pentamer (residues 1–130 of mouse nucleophosmin) and cationic arginine-rich peptide rpL5 heterotypic condensate, minimal facsimiles of nucleolar granular components, formed network-fluid condensates featuring spatial inhomogeneities across different length scales that reflect the contributions from distinct protein and peptide domains48. These studies provided important insights into the phase separation driving force and mesoscale architecture of MDP-based condensates. However, a systematic internal molecular structure investigation and comparison to simplified IDR-only condensates is lacking, leaving the question how folded domains affect condensate internal structure and organization unresolved.

  In this study, we employed the recently developed CG Martini3-IDP model49 to investigate the internal architecture of both IDR-only and full-length MDP-based condensates, explicitly incorporating folded domains into our models. By direct comparison of IDR-only condensates of the low complexity domain of protein Fused in Sarcoma (FUSLCD) to full length FUS protein condensates, we reveal that the presence of the folded domain of FUS significantly alters condensate hierarchical organization and monomer conformation inside. Simulations of more full-length MDP-based condensates (small heat shock protein 2 HSPB2 and P granule scaffolding-1 PGL1) demonstrate that while contact specificity is conserved between condensate and solution environment, similar to the observation in IDR-only condensates, folded domains imposed greater heterogeneity in the internal molecular structure. Moreover, the incorporation of folded domains leads to a pronounced slowdown in both translational diffusion and local molecular motions, contrasting with the heterogeneous retardation of protein dynamics in IDR-only condensates with global motion being strongly slowed down whereas local dynamics is being retained42. Our findings deepen the understanding of endogenous condensates and posit that folded domains act as architectural keystones in natural condensates, shaping their structural and dynamic properties. By elucidating how ordered and disordered regions cooperate to define condensate structure and function, this work promotes a more holistic view for biomolecular condensates.


## Results

### Martini3-IDP model captures condensate formation with near-quantitative accuracy
  The recently developed Martini3-IDP model49 addresses a critical limitation of the Martini3 force field by resolving the conformational collapse of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) through systematic recalibration of bonded terms. Since the same protein beads assignment and interaction matrix underlying Martini3 were used, this advancement enables accurate representation of IDP conformations while remaining seamlessly integrated into the broad chemical space covered by the standard Martini framework, including its structured proteins, lipid membranes, and small molecules as validated in Martini3-IDP work49,50. Such integration makes Martini3-IDP uniquely suited to probe biomolecular condensates with different complexity and in particular condensates formed by MDPs, where structured and disordered regions coexist.

  In this study, we employed a further refined Martini3-IDP model incorporating optimized protein side-chain interactions to better capture the driving forces relevant to phase separation. The side-chain binding propensities were systematically evaluated and calibrated by umbrella sampling-derived 1D potentials of mean force (PMFs), benchmarked against reference all-atom force fields51,52 (model details in Supplementary Discussion 1, Supplementary Figs. 1–3). As validation, the predicted saturation concentrations (i.e., dilute phase concentration) of most systems from a diverse set of IDR-only condensates deviated by less than 1.5 logarithmic units from experimental measurements (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1). The sole exception was A1LCD, whose collapsed conformation relative to experimental measurements and overestimated phase separation propensity may arise from its abundant Arg-Phe interactions which are slightly overestimated in this refined Martini3-IDP model (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, the simulated dense phase protein concentrations (~334 ± 19 and ~406 ± 8 mg/ml for FUSLCD and TIA1LCD, respectively) closely aligned with the experimental measurements of typical protein condensates, such as the 477 mg/ml FUSLCD protein concentration measured by NMR40,53, implying these condensates are well hydrated. We also observed a strong correspondence between the experimental and simulated radius of gyration (Rg) for a large set of both IDPs and MDPs (Supplementary Fig. 5). ### IDR-only condensates exhibit extended monomer conformations
  Before moving to realistic condensates, for reference, we set out to probe the conformational landscape of monomers within IDR-only condensates. To this end, we analyzed FUSLCD and TIA1LCD dynamics inside a slab-geometry condensed phase over a 28 μs production simulation (Supplementary Table 2). For comparison, we also performed a 15 μs simulation of a single chain in solution, representing the dilute phase (Supplementary Table 3). We first analyzed the Rg of these IDRs (Fig. 1b). As can be appreciated from the width of the distributions, individual monomers sampled a broad range of conformations, consistent with the disordered conformational nature of these condensates observed in NMR studies53. The minor inter-monomer heterogeneity observed in the condensed phase (average inter-monomer Rg distribution KL divergence 0.193 and 0.071 for FUSLCD and TIA1LCD) shows that tens of microseconds are near-sufficient for each monomer to exhaustively sample the accessible conformational landscape. Additionally, the individual chains undergo rapid configurational interconversions on a sub-microsecond time scale (estimated end to end distance correlation time 469 and 545 ns for FUSLCD and TIA1LCD monomers within the condensate, being only ~20 times slower than their solution-state monomer with correlation times of 28 and 21 ns, respectively), as evidenced by the decay of the autocorrelation function of the monomer end-to-end distances (Supplementary Fig. 6). This is in line with recent findings from combined single-molecule spectroscopy and all-atom simulations, pointing to rapid conformational reconfiguration dynamics despite the overall high viscosity of the condensate phase42.

  Interestingly, the average Rg significantly differs between the dilute and condensed environment. Monomers within condensates are seen to adopt expanded conformations compared to their solution-state counterparts. The extent of this effect depends on the type of protein. For FUSLCD, the mean Rg increased from 2.21 nm (solution) to 4.82 nm (condensate), an increase of more than 100%, while TIA1LCD only expanded by ~30% from 3.01 nm to 3.91 nm (Fig. 1b). To probe the importance of protein length (TIA1LCD being shorter, measuring 97 residues versus 163 for FUSLCD), we also performed an intrachain distance-sequence separation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7). This data revealed that the conformation expansion for condensate-embedded FUSLCD and TIA1LCD monomers converged to a comparable level, pointing to a different behavior of these proteins in the dilute phase, with TIA1LCD adopting less compact conformations compared to FUSLCD (Fig. 1b). The conformational expansions observed here align with prior observations in elastin-like peptide condensates54,55 and suggest a shared biophysical principle governing IDP expansion in homotypic condensate environments, where individual monomers are extensively solvated by surrounding ones.

  To dissect the molecular details of conformational adaptation within condensates beyond rough global dimension estimation, we next analyzed 2D intrachain contact maps of monomers in condensates compared to their solution-state counterparts (Fig. 1c). This analysis revealed that the observed expansion is not a uniform swelling but instead accompanied with spatially heterogeneous rearrangements. Long-distance interactions (i.e., end-end contacts and contacts between FUSLCD terminus and its central hotspots) were significantly diminished in condensates, while short-range contacts were preserved more and remained relatively more prevalent, creating localized structural wrinkles in the contact maps. The suppression of long-range contacts suggests that in contrast to solution-state dynamics where unrestrained conformational sampling is allowed, condensate environments impose topological constraints on IDPs, which may arise from the internal meshwork formed by multivalent interactions.


### Interaction specificity and kinetics are conserved in IDR-only condensates
  To shed more light on the nature of the interactions of the monomers with the environment, we additionally analyzed inter-chain interactions. In this case, the dilute reference state consisted of a pair of monomers in solution, simulated over 30 μs in triplicate to obtain sufficient sampling (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast to the intra-chain contacts, which were heterogeneously rearranged as shown above, residue interactions hot spots for both proteins are more or less the same between dilute and condensed phase (integrating both intrachain and interchain interactions). This is evident from the residue-specific contact number profiles, which show similar regions of preferential interactions irrespective of the environment (Fig. 2a). A similar conclusion was recently reached for the prothymosinα (ProTα) interaction profile within a ProTα-histone H1 mixture condensate using all-atom simulations42 and for two LCD systems using residue-level coarse-grained simulations56. To further dissect interaction specificity, we analyzed 2D intermolecular contact maps (Fig. 2b), which showed conserved residue-pair interaction hotspots and distributions between condensates and solution-state dimers, quantified by low KL divergence (0.084 and 0.078 for FUSLCD and TIA1LCD, respectively) and lower than the divergence of the intrachain contact mode (0.44 and 0.15 for FUSLCD and TIA1LCD). The interchain residue-pair contacts of FUSLCD resemble a 3×3 block matrix, suggesting three separate binding motifs in both condensates and solution. In the case of TIA1LCD, the contact hot spots are clustered near residue 60 and residue 80.   Surprisingly, the crowded condensate environment did not amplify residue interaction density equally. For the weakly self-associating TIA1LCD, the contact number increased substantially upon condensate entry, whereas the strongly self-associating FUSLCD exhibited only modest gains (Fig. 2a). Both proteins utilize the polar and aromatic residues for self-association53,59 (Supplementary Fig. 8), and the richer polar residue composition of FUSLCD compared to TIA1LCD (74.2% vs. 52.6%) underpins its stronger inherent association. This buffering-like effect of interaction density within a crowded condensate, where interaction density saturates despite differences in self-association strength, may arise from the similar protein concentration in the condensate phase, limiting the amount of residue interactions that can be formed.

  Finally, inspired by the fast local dynamics probed in the prothymosinα (ProTα)-histone H1 mixture condensate using all-atom simulation42, residue association kinetics was also measured in our CG resolution simulations of FUSLCD and TIA1LCD condensates (Fig. 2c). Similar to the reported findings of the all-atom simulations, we observed that the intermolecular contact lifetimes in condensates matched those of the solution-state dimers, with typical lifetimes in the nanosecond range. Random, non-attractive collisions (contacts with lifetime ≤ 1 ns) appeared somewhat increased inside the condensate, probably due to the higher protein density. These findings confirm that, despite their highly viscous nature, local motions remain less hindered in condensates, supporting their dynamic plasticity.


### Full length FUS protein imposes different condensate structure and FUSLCD segment behavior
  After having analyzed the behavior of FUSLCD in detail, we now turn our focus on the full length protein. Although FUSLCD is capable of LLPS alone in vitro at the µM concentration level53,60–62, the contribution from other domains cannot be overlooked when considering endogenous condensates incorporating FUS proteins. A critical unresolved question is whether the condensate structure and material properties from synthetic IDR-only condensates could be extrapolated to natural condensates mostly governed by full length MDPs.

  To address this issue, we simulated the LLPS of full length FUS proteins, which incorporates a folded RNA-recognition motif (RRM) and one extra low-complexity domain (Supplementary Fig. 9). Instead of a slab geometry, here we used a cubic box leading to the formation of a condensate droplet which remained stable during a 20 μs production simulation (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 3a). We reverted to this droplet setup as it turned out to be difficult to equilibrate MDP-based condensates inside a slab geometry, often resulting in multiple disconnected clusters of proteins rather than a fully connected phase. To rule out differences between the slab versus droplet geometry setup, a reference FUSLCD spherical condensate was obtained for comparison with its slab geometry condensate investigated above. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10, both condensate geometries yielded nearly identical structural properties (e.g., conformations, interaction specificity), confirming the robustness of our simulation findings in both simulation geometries. Strikingly, in contrast to the extended conformation observed in both droplet-geometry and slab-geometry synthetic IDR-only FUSLCD condensate, most FUS monomers adopted quite compact and near-spherical conformations within the condensate, as quantified by asphericity measurement (Fig. 3b). These computational findings align with experimental observation from native ion mobility mass spectrometry, which identified compaction of FUS monomers when forming a condensate, with its tyrosine-rich N-terminal LCD and the glycine/arginine-rich C-terminal LCD located at the surface of the protein driving the self-assembly63.   Analysis of the radius of gyration shows that the corresponding FUSLCD fragments within FUS protein condensates exhibited narrower and more heterogeneous conformation distributions compared to the FUSLCD in the IDR-only counterpart (Fig. 3c). Hence, due to the interplay with the folded RRM domain and the other low-complexity domain, the conformational ensemble of the FUSLCD is strongly affected.

  The stark conformation contrast between IDR-only FUSLCD and full-length FUS condensates underscores the transformative role of folded domains in restructuring condensate internal structure. Specifically, the RRM domain appears to act as a conformational constraint, limiting LCD flexibility and redirecting interaction networks. Collectively, these results highlight the limitations of synthetic IDR-only models in capturing the structure complexity of endogenous condensates and the necessity of elucidating higher molecular-complexity condensates formed by full length MDPs to promote our understanding of endogenous condensates.


### Full-length MDP-based condensates preserve interaction profile despite offering a heterogeneous environment
  As FUS proteins are mostly not considered as scaffold of cellular granules, despite their roles in RNP granules and ability to self-assemble into liquid-like condensates10,64,65, we next turned to two other MDPs to investigate structural hallmarks of full-length MDP-based condensates: the small heat shock protein 2 (HSPB2) and the P granule scaffolding-1 (PGL1) (Supplementary Fig. 9). These systems were selected for their ability to form functional, liquid-like condensates, recapitulating core biophysical features of natural condensates66–68.

  HSPB2, a chaperone with a structured α-crystallin domain (ACD), undergoes phase separation to form nuclear condensates that regulate lamin A localization and chromatin organization67,68. Similarly, PGL1, a scaffold protein incorporating multiple folded dimerization domains and disordered regions, forms simplified P granules capable of recruiting Argonaute WAGO-1 regulator protein and repressing recruited mRNA expression66. Crucially, both proteins are sufficient to self-assemble into condensates with liquid-like dynamics in vitro, mirroring the material properties of their endogenous counterparts. This makes them ideal models for probing the structural principles of natural condensates with molecular complexity above synthetic IDR-only systems and clarifying how folded domains help encode condensate architecture.

  As shown in Fig. 4a, spherical condensates were formed by both 100 HSPB2 and 33 PGL1 protein copies after 28 μs production simulations in a cubic box. In line with the results obtained for full-length FUS, the inclusion of folded domains introduced two critical perturbations absent in IDR-only systems: (1) stronger, more specific folded domain-mediated interactions (folded-folded and folded-IDR interactions) within the condensate, and (2) pronounced steric effects from the excluded volume of structured regions. As a consequence, significant inter-monomer conformational heterogeneity was observed for the N-terminal IDR of HSPB2 (average inter-monomer Rg distribution KL divergence of 0.752), with Rg distribution peaks of individual monomers differing more than 2 nm (Fig. 4b).   Additionally, there is another layer of conformational heterogeneity: in contrast to the N-terminal IDR region of HSPB2, the C-terminal IDR tail of HSPB2 displayed much smaller inter-monomer heterogeneity (average inter-monomer Rg distribution KL divergence=0.145), with the overall Rg distribution resembling that of both solution-state and IDR-only condensate counterpart (KL = 0.021, Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 12). This dichotomy highlights how distinct protein regions couple differently to the condensate environment: while the N-terminal IDR experiences strong modulation by the condensate environment, the C-terminal tail retains solution-like flexibility. Such nonuniform coupling implies that condensate environments could selectively affect the behavior of internal components based on their intrinsic biophysical properties (e.g., composition, charge, interaction valency). In case of the C-terminal IDR of PGL1, no evident conformation heterogeneity difference was observed between the two condensate contexts (Supplementary Fig. 13), with significant inter-monomer conformational heterogeneity observed in both IDR-only (KL = 1.789) and MDP-based (KL = 1.624) condensate. Due to its quite extended chain length, we argue that the spread of conformations is mostly a result of the limited sampling during the accessible simulation timescale, even in case of the IDR-only condensate, obscuring potential effects of the scaffold.

  The interface between the coexisting dilute and bulk condensate regions constitutes a distinctive microenvironment by itself, characterized by a gradual solvation gradient as shown in Supplementary Fig. 14. Concomitantly, there is a monotonic Rg transition for both HSPB2 N-ter and C-ter IDRs going from the condensate core through the interface into the dilute phase (Supplementary Fig. 15). The most expanded conformations are found in the core region, whereas the most compact ones are in the dilute phase, with the interface showing intermediate behaviour. Comparing this to the hydration analysis (Supplementary Fig. 14) implies the configurations in the core region are more extended despite being less hydrated, which might seem counter-intuitive. The reason is that the extension in the core region is driven by favorable interactions with the other condensate components. Upon approaching (and entering) the dilute phase, these favorable interactions are replaced by less favorable interactions with water, resulting in more collapsed configurations yet higher hydration levels.

  To understand how the spatially heterogeneous microenvironments within full-length MDP-based condensates impact the monomer interaction profiles, we analyzed the average number of contacts along the protein chain. As expected, the interaction profiles also displayed certain heterogeneity between the monomers (Fig. 5a), particularly noticeable for the N-terminal IDR part of HSPB2 protein, and contrasting the conserved interaction profiles observed in IDR-only condensates above. Note that in case of PGL1, due to insufficient sampling of its solution-state, a direct condensate-solution comparison could not be performed.   Together, these simulation findings reveal that full-length MDP-based condensates are structurally inhomogeneous, where folded domains impose spatially varying microenvironments, with the behavior of individual components governed by their local coupling to the condensate matrix. Nevertheless, we observe a conserved interaction profile between MDP-based and IDR-only condensates, implying that condensate environments may preserve molecular recognition codes even amid conformational heterogeneity.


### Both attenuated global motion and local dynamics in full-length MDP-based condensates
  In the final section, we focus on the question how the heterogeneous nature of MDP-based condensates affects their local dynamics and residue binding kinetics. Presumably, folded domains would retard MDP motion within condensates due to excluded volume effects. The rigid structured domains preclude large-scale structure rearrangement and diffuse through the voids of the condensate network (as observed previously in a host p53 tetramer condensate by fluorescent imaging and MD simulations69), unlike the reptation-like dynamics which enables disordered regions to navigate the condensate meshwork (e.g., observed within RNA condensates by coarse-grained simulation70). Supporting this, a recent dynamic light scattering microrheology study reported significantly higher viscosity in condensate containing globular green fluorescent protein36.

  We start out by analyzing the translational diffusion of the folded domains of HSPB2 and PGL1 by means of their mean-square displacement (MSD), as shown in Fig. 6a. Although our simulations are not probing long enough timescales to observe the long-time diffusion of the proteins, a local effective diffusion constant within the micro-environment of the condensate can be obtained from fitting the MSD curves across the 5–20 ns regime. Surprisingly, the short-time diffusion constant of the folded domains, Dshort ≈ 3–5 × 10−12 m2s−1 (Supplementary Table 4), displayed less than an order of magnitude reduction in mobility compared to synthetic IDR-only condensate systems (e.g., pLys/pGlu coacervate and FUSLCD condensate from previous Martini resolution simulations with similar box dimensions), where diffusion coefficients typically range from ~20 to ~40 × 10−12 m2s−1 at room temperature43,49. A similar observation we made when comparing the translational diffusion of the IDR-only FUSLCD condensate to the FUS protein condensate, where translational diffusion within the FUS condensate was ~3 fold slower than that of the corresponding FUSLCD condensate (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, these apparent diffusion coefficient estimations for comparison were all calculated in simulations of consistent cubic box dimensions (box size 30 ~ 34 nm), diminishing effects due to the system-size dependence of diffusion coefficients in MD simulations with periodic boundary conditions71. These results suggest that MDP condensates balance structural organization with retaining dynamic fluidity. Furthermore, we noted that the spatial-dependent solvation within the condensate (Supplementary Fig. 14) gives rise to divergent dynamics, with significantly slower dynamics (2–3 fold reduction of the diffusion constant) observed for monomers in the less-solvated core region compared to the interface of condensates (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 4). A consistent feature was also observed in IDR-only LCD condensates (Supplementary Fig. 18).   In addition, we analyzed the lifetimes of protein-protein contacts. Interestingly, all residue contacts in full-length MDP-based condensates, even the IDR-IDR contacts, exhibited prolonged lifetimes compared to those in solution-state dimers (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 19). Specifically, contact lifetime prolongation increased monotonically from IDR-IDR contacts to folded domain-IDR and folded domain-folded domain contacts, which may arise from the steric effect for contact association/dissociation events in the crowded condensate. For instance, the mean residue contact lifetime was increased 2.5-fold/2.6-fold for IDR-IDR contacts to 5.5-fold/10.5-fold for folded domain-folded domain contacts in HSPB2 and PGL1 condensates, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 19).

  The slowdown of local motion, despite preserved interaction specificity, suggests that MDP-based condensates create viscous microenvironments where kinetic bottlenecks may regulate activity rates, in contrast to the unperturbed local fluctuations observed within IDR-only condensates42. This interaction mode duality (specificity and viscosity) provide insights to understand how condensates spatially organize biochemical reactions while modulating their efficiency, and form the mechanistic basis for context-dependent functional regulation.


## Discussion
  Biomolecular condensates, serving as dynamic cellular compartments, require rigorous investigation of their internal architecture and dynamics to elucidate their role in regulating biological processes. While IDR-only condensates formed by LCDs and IDRs have dominated recent studies due to their in vitro phase separation ability at low concentrations31,32, their relevance to endogenous condensates remains limited. Evidence suggests that IDRs alone are insufficient to recapitulate in vivo condensate assembly, lacking the interaction and client recruitment specificity observed in natural systems72,73. Moreover, folded domains absent in IDR-only condensates were identified to govern the assembly of cellular granules in mounting recent work, such as dimerization domains of G3BP1 and PGL110,16,37. To start filling this gap, we systematically compared the internal structure of IDR-only condensates and full-length MDP-based condensates incorporating folded domains, revealing emergent structural and dynamic features due to natural complexity.

  In our simulations of IDR-only FUSLCD and TIA1LCD condensates, inter-monomer similarity and expanded conformations were observed, resembling random coil behavior of polymers in ideal solution. However, we found the conformational swelling not to be uniform; long-distance communications were strongly inhibited within condensates while short-range contacts remained prevalent, creating localized structural wrinkles. Subsequent direct comparison to a corresponding full-length FUS protein condensate, where FUSLCD flexibility is constrained by the folded domains, demonstrates how folded domains reconfigure condensate architecture, highlighting the limitations of existing IDR-only condensates investigation. Contrary to FUSLCD, FUS proteins adopt globular conformations inside the condensate, resulting in more heterogeneous and less expanded conformations of the FUSLCD regions.

  Full-length MDP-based condensates formed by multidomain HSPB2 and PGL1 further revealed hierarchical structural heterogeneity. Specifically, large inter-monomer conformation heterogeneity was observed for the N-terminal IDR region in HSPB2, while the C-terminal IDR tail displayed minimal inter-monomer heterogeneity with an overall Rg distribution even resembling that of the solution-state counterpart. This dichotomy suggests that folded domains impose spatially heterogeneous microenvironments, and individual components could couple differentially to the condensate matrix. Surprisingly, despite structural heterogeneity, the interaction specificity (quantified by 1D interaction propensities and 2D contact maps) remained conserved between condensates and solution-state counterparts. Together, these results suggest that full-length MDP-based condensates may maintain functional fidelity by conserving interaction specificity. This conservation would enable crowded condensates to retain context-dependent functions, such as client recruitment or enzymatic activity.

  Finally, we observed considerable slowdown in both translational diffusion and local molecular motions in full-length MDP-based condensates, in contrast to the heterogeneous dynamics retardation reported for IDR-only condensates42. Presumably, stronger interactions mediated by the folded domains, together with the larger excluded volume of these structured regions, are causing the retarded dynamics, aligning with recent evidence demonstrating that dynamic properties of the molecular network within the condensates are significantly determined by the binding affinity and valence of the interactions74,75. Interestingly, spatial location-dependent dynamics was observed, with motion in the condensate core region even more attenuated, aligning with the spatial solvation gradient. This pronounced slowdown may imply reduced activity, particularly in the core of large-sized condensates.

  We note that the slowed internal dynamics and finite simulation timescales inherent to studying dense condensates pose challenges for achieving full equilibrium of individual monomers; and millisecond timescales are probably required to probe mesoscale rearrangement phenomena (Supplementary Fig. 21). Our analysis presented herein focuses on relative comparisons of structural and dynamic properties that are accessible within the simulation timescales, as well as ensemble-averaged contact profiles integrating the behavior of tens of protein copies within the condensates, as discussed in previous work42. Future integration of these simulation models with emerging experimental observations will be essential to build a complete, multiscale understanding of condensate structural dynamics in cellular contexts.

  There are also some limitations inherent to the chosen system setup. The Martini model employs a reaction-field method with a fixed relative dielectric constant, which may not fully capture potential dielectric changes upon condensation76. Although this effect’s influence on simulated phase separation propensity is only minor to modest according to prior investigations76,77, and recent atomistic simulations revealed similar 1D interaction pattern conservation along protein sequences42,78, the potential impact of local permittivity change on interaction specificity still deserves more investigations. Furthermore, the investigation of single scaffold component systems in the current study may exaggerate the dynamics slowdown, as competitive interactions in multi-component composition of natural condensates could buffer protein dynamics79.

  In conclusion, our results demonstrate that full-length MDP-based condensates are heterogeneous, resulting in component-specific coupling to the condensate micro-environment. Besides, we identify interaction mode duality, with conserved interaction specificity and attenuated dynamics. Our findings enrich the understanding of endogenous condensates internal structural and dynamic properties, and advance a more holistic view for biomolecular condensates by elucidating cooperative contributions from both structured and disordered regions. Clearly, more effort is required to simulate condensates including all necessary scaffolds, clients and metabolites, to understand how endogenous condensates balance structural organization with dynamic fluidity and to help resolving the ongoing debates regarding the functional distinctions between dynamic hubs and LLPS-driven condensates which both are primarily driven by IDRs33–36,72,73,79,80.


## Methods

### MD simulation protocol
  The Martini3-IDP model49 was used for all condensate simulations in this study, with the inclusion of some optimized side chain parameters (details in Supplementary Discussion 1). Specifically, IDR-only IDPs (FUSLCD and TIA1LCD) and IDRs within multidomain proteins (FUS, HSPB2 and PGL1) were modeled using Martini3-IDP + optimized side chain parameters; structured protein domains within multidomain proteins (FUS, HSPB2, and PGL1) were modeled using standard Martini3 protein + optimized side chain parameters.

  Martini topology and initial structure construction followed the tutorial in our previous work using Polyply and Martinize249,81,82. IDPs and the IDRs identified in multidomain proteins were generated by Polyply, based on sequence fasta files. The folded domains were coarse-grained to Martini by Martinize2 and reference AA structures were taken from the RCSPDB database or predicted by Alphafold83 (FUS:AF-P35637, HSPB2:AF-Q16082, PGL1:AF-Q9TZQ3 in current work). An intradomain elastic network model was applied with Martinize2 to keep the folded domain intact, using harmonic potentials of 700 kJ mol−1 nm−2 between backbone beads within 0.9 nm cutoff. Secondary structure was assigned with DSSP84 in Martinize2. The -scFix flag was used to restrict side chain orientation in the folded domains based on reference AA structure.

  In the Martini simulations, following the standard protocol85, the Verlet-scheme was used with the default energy drift buffer. The Verlet neighbor search algorithm is used to update the neighbor list every 20 steps with a buffer tolerance of 0.005 kJ mol−1ps−1. Note, in simulations of large size HSPB2 and PGL1 condensates, nstlist = 20 and rlist = 1.35 were explicitly defined to diminish neighbor list artifacts in molecular dynamics simulations as suggested in recent work86. By default, nrexcl = 1 setting is applied in Martini, implying 1–4 non-bonded interactions are on. Potential shift was used to calculate van der Waals interactions with 1.1 nm cutoff and Coulomb interactions were treated by a reaction-field with relative dielectric constant 15 and 1.1 nm cutoff. Production simulations were run with 20 fs step size, using velocity-rescaling thermostat (temperature coupling constant 1 ps) and Parrinello-Rahman barostat (pressure coupling constant 12 ps). In direct-coexistence LLPS simulations, semi-isotropic pressure coupling was used keeping the XY plane uncompressed. Isotropic pressure coupling was used in all other systems. All simulations were executed by Gromacs, versions 2021.7, and 2022.687.


### Simulations setup
  Direct-coexistence simulations for synthetic IDR-only condensates, FUSLCD and TIA1LCD, were performed in a rectangular box88. The X/Y axis dimensions were set to 12 and 10 nm, with the Z-dimension 80 nm, as listed in Supplementary Table 2. Based on finite-size analysis, 8 nm lateral dimension was enough to obtain robust coexisting phases for FUSLCD modeled with the Mpipi force field89. Depending on the protein, between 30 and 60 copies of the proteins were included to keep the dense phase thick enough and simulation tractable. The protein copies were inserted into a relatively small slab region as initial configuration to accelerate LLPS and simulation convergence. The simulation box was then expanded to 80 nm along the Z axis. For FUSLCD and TIA1LCD, 28 μs production simulations were obtained as listed in Supplementary Table 2 after discarding initial equilibration, long enough to make sure the density profiles remained unchanged when evaluated over successive time windows (Supplementary Fig. 4).

  Droplet geometry simulations of the IDR-only FUSLCD condensate and multidomain condensates were performed in a cubic box. The box size and protein copies are listed in Supplementary Table 2. In simulation of FUSLCD and FUS condensates, 54 FUSLCD and 18 FUS proteins were randomly inserted into cubic boxes of 25 nm and 22 nm edge lengths as initial configuration. The boxes were subsequently expanded to 30 nm and 32 nm to accommodate molecular organization. 28 μs and 20 μs production simulations were obtained for FUSLCD and full length multi domain FUS condensates, as listed in Supplementary Table 2 after discarding initial equilibration. In the simulation of HSPB2 and PGL1 condensates, initially, small condensates consisting either of 40 HSPB2 or 20 PGL1 protein copies were pre-formed in a cubic box. The formed condensates were subsequently supplemented with additional protein copies (reaching 100 HSPB2 and 33 PGL1), and placed in a larger final box of 32 and 34 nm for HSPB2/PGL1, respectively. To accelerate the relaxation into a spherical droplet, 24 μs equilibration simulations at elevated temperatures (333 K for HSPB2, 363 K for PGL1) were performed. Finally, 28 μs production simulations under physiological 303 K condition were obtained for HSPB2 and PGL1 condensates as listed in Supplementary Table 2 after discarding initial equilibration.

  In simulations of solution-state single chain monomer and dimer counterpart systems, IDPs and MDPs were inserted into a dodecahedron box with system-dependent box size, avoiding periodic interference as listed in Supplementary Table 3 (except for HSPB2 dimer systems where a 22 nm edge cubic box was implemented). Solution-state single chain monomers systems were simulated between 10 and 25 μs as listed in Supplementary Table 3, ensuring simulation convergence using blocking analysis. Solution-state dimer systems were simulated for triplicate 30 μs to ensure convergence, except the PGL1 dimer system which was run 30 μs as a single replica as only residue contact life time was analyzed.


### Analysis of MD simulations
  Rg and asphericity of each monomer within condensates was calculated by the MDAnalysis package90,91 with 1 ns frequency. Rg of solution-state single chain monomer was calculated by gmx gyrate with 0.2 ns frequency. End to end distance was calculated by gmx distance with 0.2 ns frequency for solution-state monomers and 0.5 ns frequency for monomers within condensates. End to end distance autocorrelation function was calculated by gmx analyze -ac with single-exponential fitting, and correlation times were estimated by integrating the fit to the normalized autocorrelation function.

  In the contact profile analysis, two residues were considered to be in contact if the minimum distance between their backbone beads was within 1 nm cutoff, following the setting in preceding work42. Based on the cutoff analysis in this work, the contact analysis conclusions should be robust to the choice of cutoff42. Distances between backbone beads were used instead of the commonly used distances between all beads of the residues to facilitate efficiency42. In intrachain contact analysis, contacts between neighboring residues within three residues along the sequence were excluded. The contact propensity of a specific residue was treated as the number of residues in contact with itself. 1D and 2D contact profiles in this study were analyzed using the MDAnalysis package with 1 ns frequency. In the finer resolution BB-BB, SC-SC and BB-SC contact analysis, two residues were considered to be in contact if the distance between any bead pair from the two residues was within 0.6 nm cutoff.

  Lifetimes of residue-residue contacts were calculated by a transition-based approach42,92. Specifically, a dual-cutoff scheme was used to determine the formation and breaking of contacts instead of using a single distance cutoff to distinguish bound versus unbound states, which tends to underestimate contact lifetimes. For each pair of residues, a contact was still defined based on the minimum distance between their backbone beads. A contact formation was defined to occur when this distance dropped below 0.8 nm; then an existing contact was considered to remain in the formed state until the distance increased to more than 1.2 nm. The step size for interchain contact lifetimes in this study was 0.5 ns. Contact lifetimes of different contact types were identified as contacts formed between IDRs and structured regions: Residues 1-64 and residues 148–182 were identified as N-terminal and C-terminal IDRs of HSPB2 protein, respectively, and residues 65–147 was identified as the structured ACD domain of HSPB2 protein; Residues 1–217 and residues 223–449 in PGL1 correspond to the structured NtDD and CDD domains, and other residues were IDRs.

  The MSD of diverse domains were measured using the final 500 ns part of the trajectories by the gmx msd function. The center of mass motion of condensate was dealt by -rmcomm option in gmx msd. Periodic boundary conditions were dealt by default in the MSD calculations by gmx msd versions before 2022 (for example Gromacs versions 2021.7 used here) with raw trajectory input. The translational diffusion coefficient, D, was calculated by fitting up to 20 ns for folded domains or to 5 ns for disordered FUSLCD domains (using the central residue 76–85 segment), where there are no deviations from linearity. Core region and interface splitting in translation dynamics analysis was determined by the radial protein beads density of the condensate, computed by calculating protein bead density in each radial distance bin relative to the condensate center with bin width equal to 0.3 nm, using the MDAnalysis package (Supplementary Fig. 20). The radial distance evolution of each folded domain is shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. In the HSPB2 condensate, 8.5 and 7.5 nm were set as the boundary of interface and core, with 1 nm buffer region; the domains (both folded domains and IDR) with an average radial distance to condensate center of geometry (COG) > 8.5 nm were viewed as interface components and the ones with average radial distance to condensate COG < 7.5 nm were viewed as core region components. Likewise, for the PGL1 condensate, 9 and 8 nm were set as the boundary of interface and core regions. Subsequently, any labeled component which moved from core to interface or vice versa in any frame was deleted from the analysis.

  Mass density profiles were determined along the Z-axis with protein density centered by the gmx density tool. Considering bead masses in Martini 3 are usually slightly larger than actual atom group masses, a rescaling factor (the ratio of protein molar weight and beads molar weight in Martini 3) was used to correct the mass density profile.
