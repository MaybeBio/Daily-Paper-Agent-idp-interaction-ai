## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This Perspective presents a coherent and timely argument for reframing intrinsic disorder as a design principle rather than a structural deficiency. The authors draw on a broad literature spanning IDP biophysics, biomolecular condensates, associative polymers, and colloidal science, and they make a reasonable case that these fields share conceptual foundations. The writing is clear and accessible, and the progression from physical principles to computational strategies to design rules is logical. However, the manuscript is largely synthetic rather than novel: most of the individual ideas (e.g., IDRs as associative polymers, sticker–spacer models, ensemble-based engineering) are well established in the literature. The claimed "paradigm" is presented more as an assembly of existing concepts than as a genuinely new framework. The absence of a critical examination of the limitations of the analogy between IDRs and synthetic associative polymers, and the lack of concrete worked examples or quantitative demonstrations, weaken the force of the central argument. The manuscript would benefit from a more explicit articulation of what is genuinely new in this synthesis, and from a more balanced treatment of the challenges and counterexamples.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in IDP biophysics, biomolecular condensates, soft matter physics, and biomimetic materials science would find this Perspective of interest. It offers a useful conceptual bridge between these communities and may serve as a framework for interdisciplinary collaboration. The discussion of computational strategies and design rules could be valuable for practitioners in protein engineering and materials design. The manuscript is also likely to be of interest to graduate students and early-career researchers seeking an accessible entry point into the disorder-as-design concept.
- **Major strengths** 1. The manuscript provides a clear and well-organized synthesis of concepts from IDP biophysics, polymer physics, and colloidal science, making a plausible case for their conceptual convergence. 2. The writing is accessible and logically structured, with a clear progression from physical principles to computational strategies to design rules. 3. The authors correctly emphasize the importance of collective observables (e.g., phase behavior, material properties) as design targets, which is an important and sometimes underappreciated point. 4. The discussion of transferability and the need for hierarchical modeling strategies is thoughtful and practically relevant. 5. The manuscript is well-referenced, drawing on a broad and current literature.
- **Major Concerns**  
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1  
  - **Severity** Major  
  - **Blocking** Yes  
  - **Axis** Originality / novelty of the central claim  
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "intrinsic disorder can be understood as a biomimetic design principle for molecular and materials engineering" and that this constitutes a "paradigm" shift from structure–function thinking.  
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract; Section 1, Introduction; Section 6, Conclusions  
  - **Concern** The central claim of a new "paradigm" is not supported by evidence of genuine novelty. The idea that IDPs/IDRs function through ensembles and weak multivalent interactions is well established in the literature (e.g., Wright & Dyson, 1999; Dunker et al., 2001; Uversky, 2002). The analogy between IDRs and associative polymers has been made previously (e.g., Semenov & Rubinstein, 1998; Rubinstein & Semenov, 2001; Choi et al., 2019). The sticker–spacer model has been applied to IDR phase separation by multiple groups (e.g., Wang et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2020). The authors do not identify what is genuinely new in their synthesis beyond the assembly of existing concepts. The term "paradigm" implies a fundamental shift in thinking, but the manuscript does not demonstrate that such a shift has occurred or is being proposed in a way that differs from prior articulations of similar ideas.  
  - **Why it matters** For a Perspective article, the novelty of the conceptual framework is the primary contribution. If the synthesis does not offer a genuinely new angle, the manuscript risks being a review of known ideas rather than a forward-looking perspective. The claim of a "paradigm" is a strong one that requires explicit justification.  
  - **Resolution test** The authors should explicitly state what is new in their framework compared to prior syntheses (e.g., those by Uversky, Mittag, Forman-Kay, or others). They should identify specific conceptual or methodological advances that their framework enables, and provide at least one concrete example where the disorder-as-design approach leads to a prediction or design that would not follow from existing frameworks.

  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "recent advances in coarse-grained modeling, machine learning, and inverse design further enable disorder to be treated as a controllable engineering variable."

The claim that "disorder-as-design will likely rely on layered modeling strategies" is plausible but speculative. The manuscript does not address the significant challenges in achieving quantitative accuracy in coarse-grained models of IDRs, particularly regarding transferability across conditions and the accuracy of predicted phase diagrams.  
  - **Why it matters** The feasibility of the proposed design paradigm depends critically on the maturity of computational methods. If the authors cannot demonstrate that current methods are capable of achieving the required accuracy, the practical relevance of their framework is weakened. The manuscript should either provide evidence of successful applications or be more measured in its claims about current capabilities.  
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide at least one concrete example of a successful computational design of an IDR or IDR-based material with predicted and experimentally validated properties. Alternatively, they should temper their claims about current capabilities and more explicitly frame the computational strategies as aspirational rather than demonstrated.  
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3  
  - **Severity** Major  
  - **Blocking** No  
  - **Axis** Scientific importance / critical analysis  
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "these principles closely parallel those found in associative polymers and colloidal systems" and that this analogy provides a basis for translating design strategies between fields.  
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 2, Intrinsic Disorder as Programmable Soft Matter; Section 4, Design Rules and Molecular Grammar for Disorder-Inspired Biomimetic Systems  
  - **Concern** The analogy between IDRs and associative polymers is presented as straightforward, but the manuscript does not critically examine the limits of this analogy. For example, IDRs have sequence-specific features (e.g., stereochemistry, backbone geometry, post-translational modifications) that have no direct analogue in synthetic associative polymers. The manuscript does not discuss cases where the analogy breaks down or where predictions based on polymer physics have failed for IDR systems. The treatment of the analogy is one-sided, emphasizing similarities while neglecting differences.  
  - **Why it matters** A critical examination of the limits of the analogy would strengthen the manuscript by demonstrating intellectual rigor and providing guidance on where the analogy is likely to be productive and where it is not. Without this, the manuscript risks overstating the transferability of concepts between fields.  
  - **Resolution test** The authors should include a discussion of at least two specific cases where the associative polymer analogy fails to capture IDR behavior, and explain what this implies for the design framework.
