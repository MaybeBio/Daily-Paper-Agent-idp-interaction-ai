# Condensates as Conformation
Editors of Disordered
Client Proteins


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates organize cellular biochemistry not only by passive concentration modulation of molecules but also by potentially remodeling client protein conformations. However, the systematic principles governing such conformational editing are unknown. Here, we use coarse-grained simulations to demonstrate that diverse condensate environments remodel disordered client proteins in a scaffold-specific manner. Additionally, we explore the factors of this remodeling, establishing that the linear patterning of scaffold interaction motifs along sequence (sticker clustering) plays an important role, whereas an increased scaffold chain length primarily slows client dynamics while leaving their sequence-specific intrachain contact patterns largely preserved. This conformational remodeling is mediated by varied and nonuniform client–condensate interaction maps that are not solely encoded by primary sequence grammar but are cooperatively shaped by the emergent condensate architecture. Our work reveals that condensates function as sophisticated regulators of recruited disordered client conformation, with broad implications for cellular regulation and therapeutic intervention.


## Introduction
  Living cells rely on spatial control and the local compartmentalization of molecule constituents to precisely coordinate hundreds of cellular biochemical processes. Membrane-bound organelles are widely considered as the primary form of subcellular compartmentalization, such as the nucleus and mitochondria. Over the past decade, membrane-less organelles formed via phase separation, also referred to as biomolecular condensates, have emerged as a novel subcellular organization mechanism engaging in diverse cellular biochemical processes. ,  These membrane-less organelles typically contain tens of types of molecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites, kept together by multivalent interactions without the aid of lipid membrane enclosure. ,  Characterized in detail, only a small number of components appear to be essential for the structural integrity of these organelles and are therefore termed “scaffolds”. Conversely, the remaining majority of components are recruited and reside in the organelles to exert functions as “clients”. ,

  Previous work on condensate function centers on the selective concentration, exclusion or sequestration of client molecules to thereby amplify or inhibit reactions, creating a distinct reaction phase. ,−   However, beyond this passive concentration-dependent activity modulation mechanism, the macromolecular crowding and networking properties of a condensate also create a unique microenvironment with physicochemical properties distinct from the surrounding bulk solution. −     Based on mounting evidence, such as the varied nucleic acid duplex stability observed in different condensates −   and the shift in the SOD1 protein folding equilibrium toward the unfolded state in a CAPRIN1 condensate, the unique condensate environment is increasingly speculated to be able to directly remodel client conformation, with potential implications for function. This concept of conformational remodeling also holds promise for therapeutic intervention: conventionally “undruggable” disordered proteins, whose conformation is flexible and context-sensitive, might be remodeled or stabilized after being recruited into condensates (e.g., transcription condensates consisting of various transcription factors containing disordered domains) and provide new opportunities to develop therapeutics targeting remodeled conformations. ,  Therefore, elucidating how the condensate milieu acts as a regulator, editor, of the client protein conformation is of primary importance to understand their functional mechanisms and potential as therapeutic targets.

  Addressing client conformation questions demands tools capable of probing molecular structures within a dense and heterogeneous environment. Experimental techniques, like NMR spectroscopy and single-molecule Förster resonance energy transfer, are making remarkable progress but still face challenges in resolution and throughput. −    Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, particularly advanced coarse-grained (CG) models, offer a powerful complementary approach by enabling a view on the condensate structure and dynamics at a spatiotemporal resolution sufficient to observe detailed client behavior. −        However, to the best of our knowledge, a systematic investigation of client conformational changes across diverse condensate environments is still lacking.

  In this study, we employed the newly developed CG Martini3-IDP (intrinsically disordered protein) model to track three disordered client proteins (sequences shown in Figure  A) within a panel of condensates (differing in scaffold chain length and sequence composition, Figure  B), revealing a condensate-specific manner in client conformation remodeling. By engineering condensate scaffold chain length and linear sticker patterning (i.e., residues that contribute to primary attractive interactions and drive condensate formation within the stickers-and-spacers framework), we found sticker clustering on the scaffold protein to result in significant client conformation alteration, whereas increased scaffold chain length promoted client expansion only in a subtle way while leaving their sequence-specific intrachain contact patterns largely preserved. However, the denser condensates generated by longer scaffolds slowed both the client conformation reconfiguration and local residue contact lifetimes. Furthermore, our contact analysis suggested distinct client–condensate interaction profiles contributed to the condensate-specific client conformation remodeling. Making it more complex, this client–condensate coupling was not solely encoded by the primary sequence grammar but also cooperatively shaped by the emergent internal structural properties of the condensates. Together, our findings highlight the role of condensate milieu as an active regulator in reprogramming disordered client protein conformation and suggest a comprehensive perspective in understanding client conformation remodeling.


## Results

### Condensate-Specific Remodeling of Disordered Client Protein
Conformations
  To probe client protein behavior in distinct condensate environments, we selected three scaffold proteins that span a broad range of chain lengths (from 12 residues to 163 residues) and sequence composition (Figure  B), thereby generating diverse physicochemical condensates: the synthetic WGR5 peptide, the low-complexity domain of protein Fused in Sarcoma (FUSLCD), and the mussel foot protein mfp-3S. −   Beyond their structural diversity, these scaffolds also originate from distinct biological contexts: WGR5 is a minimalistic peptide designed for condensate formation, mfp-3S is derived from adhesive proteins, and FUSLCD represents a canonical prion-like domain involved in transcription. The recently developed Martini3-IDP force field, which has been successfully used to model various condensate systems, was employed for the simulations. As further validation of this model, the simulated condensate phase protein concentrations (300–600 mg/mL, Figure S1) closely aligned with the experimental measurements of typical protein condensates, confirming their realistic hydration and structural properties. To further evaluate the nonspecific crowding effect from dense condensates, a dense phase containing an inert PEO crowder was simulated with similar volume fraction to the scaffold proteins in condensates.

  Based on 15–30 μs production simulations, the condensate interaction of three different disordered client proteins were studied: Histatin-5 (Hst5), the RS peptide, and N49 (Figure  A). Hst5 is a natural cationic antimicrobial peptide, RS a synthetic peptide based on an RS repeat unit within the serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 1 protein, ,  and N49 is a fragment of the Nucleoporin NUP49 protein. The chosen disordered client peptides are short enough (24–36 residues), assuring sampling of their conformations inside the slab geometry is unrestricted, and the conformational properties are converged (Figures S18 and S20). Additionally, their varied sequence composition (Figure  B) allows us to probe different behaviors, strengthening the generality of our conclusions. For comparison, we also performed 10 μs simulations of single-chain client proteins in aqueous solution.

  Throughout the simulations, a single copy of the client protein was placed within each condensate to avoid interference between client proteins. The client proteins resided within the condensate bulk region spontaneously (Figure S22), with only one quick leaving-recruitment exchange observed in the mfp-3S/N49 system. Upon entering the protein-dense condensates, client proteins formed extensive interchain interactions with the surrounding scaffold network. These interactions partially displaced the client’s water solvation shells and vastly dominated over their native intrachain interactions (Figures  C and S2). We found that, in all nine condensate–client systems, client proteins expanded to varying extent upon entering condensates, with slightly more pronounced extension occurring in FUSLCD and mfp-3S condensates compared to the WGR5 condensate, as evidenced by common measures ,  including the client’s radius of gyration (Figure  D), end-to-end distance, and fitted Flory-scaling exponent (Figure S3); concomitantly, conformation sphericity decreased relative to the more compact conformation in solution condition (Figure S3). In contrast, a pure crowding environment produced by the inert PEO crowder significantly compacted client conformation, suggesting two opposing factors that jointly dictate client conformation within condensate environments: the nonspecific crowding effect and solvation-like interactions of the scaffold network (Figures  D and S3). Apparently, the solvation-like term dominates for the selected condensates, causing an overall expansion of the clients. This expansion is also seen in a homotypic intrinsically disordered region (IDR)-only condensate ,  and can be attributed to the more favorable interaction environment present inside the condensate as compared to the solution state.

  To provide more detailed insight into the conformational changes that occur when a client is recruited inside a condensate, we next analyzed a 1D intrachain contact profile and 2D intrachain contact maps of clients in condensates compared to their solution-state counterparts (Figure  ). This revealed a spectrum of condensate-specific effects. Client Hst5 was found to maintain a similar 1D intrachain contact profile as its solution state, in all condensate environments (Pearson coefficient, PC > 0.90). However, a more detailed 2D contact analysis showed that only the sample within the WGR5 condensate preserved the parallel-orientation interaction between the Hst5 N- and C-terminus, which is also observed for the solution-state monomer. The RS peptide underwent significant conformation alteration in all three condensates (PC < 0.90), characterized by a strong hindrance of intrachain contacts engaging its C-terminal (RS) n  repeat region. More divergent client behavior was observed for N49, where its solution-state conformation was preserved in both mfp-3S and WGR5 condensates (PC ≥ 0.90) while being strongly remodeled within the FUSLCD condensate (PC = 0.48). Within the latter environment, the primary N-terminal interaction peaks of N49 were diminished and the intrachain interaction preference shifted toward the C-terminus, thereby suppressing the N-terminal local turn. Collectively, these results suggest that client conformation outcomes are dictated by the specific condensate–client pair, underscoring the function diversity encoded by different condensate environments in controlling client conformation.

  In line with heterogeneous dynamics slowdown previously reported in homotypic IDR-only condensates, ,  the client dynamics also showed dual behavior. Whereas the reconfiguration of clients was attenuated by around an order of magnitude relative to their solution-state counterparts as evidenced from the end-to-end distance autocorrelation function (Tables S5 and S6), the local motion, as quantified through individual residue contact lifetime between the client and scaffold, remained comparable to the solution-state heterodimers (Figure S4). The sole exception was within WGR5 condensates, where the residue contact lifetime was found to be significantly prolonged, likely due to the denser scaffold proteins and intrinsically slower scaffold dynamics characteristic of this condensate (Tables S5, S7, and S9).


### Condensate Scaffold Chain Length Affects Client Dynamics
  While increased scaffold protein chain length is known to enhance condensate viscoelasticity and stability, ,  its specific influence on the condensate internal structure and disordered client protein conformation remains unclear. To determine whether the distinct conformation disruption induced by FUSLCD in comparison to WGR5 can be attributed to the difference in scaffold chain length (163 versus 12 residues), or rather by other sequence-specific properties, we engineered two series of scaffolds with gradually increasing chain length: (mfp-3S) n  and (WGK) n  (n = 1, 2, 4). The WGK peptide, the RtoK mutant of the WGR series of peptides from the same work, was used here because of the strong interaction network and concomitant slow dynamics observed in (WGR5)2/4 condensates. As expected, scaffolds with increasing length formed more stable condensates, evidenced by slightly larger dense phase protein concentrations, lower dilute phase concentrations, and slower scaffold diffusion (Figures S5 and S6 and Table S9). This enhanced stability was accompanied by a further slowing of client conformational reconfiguration within these condensates, for N49 in both scaffold series and Hst5 in the WGK series (Table S5). Moreover, the residue contact lifetime between the client and scaffold was also found to be prolonged with increasing scaffold WGK repeats (Figure S6 and Table S5), in contrast to the behavior observed in less dense condensates (Figure S4).

  We then tracked the conformations of disordered clients Hst5 and N49, which were shown to be sensitive to the scaffold identity above, in the engineered condensate series. With scaffold sequence composition and patterning feature kept unchanged, increasing scaffold chain length exhibited a negligible effect on the client 1D intrachain contact profile for most systems (Figures   and S9A). The sole exception was N49 in the (WGK)4 condensate, whose C-terminus formed additional intrachain contacts compared to its reference WGK condensate and solution state (PC < 0.90). Interestingly, although slight client conformation expansion was observed for Hst5 in both (mfp-3S)2/4 condensates, N49 did not exhibit significant conformational changes. On the contrary, in the (WGK) n  series, both Hst5 and N49 show signs of compaction with increasing scaffold length (Figures S7 and S8). Together, these results suggest that condensate physicochemical properties tuned by scaffold chain length primarily affect client dynamics, modulating client conformation only in subtle ways and leaving sequence-specific intrachain contact patterns largely preserved.


### Condensate Scaffold Sticker Patterning Affects Client Conformation
  Motivated by previous findings that scaffold sticker patterning was related to its propensity for phase separation ,  and that linear sticker clustering affected condensate material properties, we hypothesized that this patterning may also impact the conformation of disordered client proteins within the condensate. To test this, we engineered two mfp-3S variants (mfp-3S_v2, mfp-3S_v3) with progressively increased segregation of aromatic sticker residues while keeping identical overall sequence composition (Figure  A). The patterning parameter was computed to quantify the aromatic sticker segregation, as done previously. As expected, segregated sticker residues along the scaffold sequence caused a reorganization of the internal scaffold network, leading to a more heterogeneous sticker distribution and the formation of larger sticker clusters within the condensate (Figure S10). Such reorganization may, in turn, alter the condensate’s interaction propensity with encapsulated clients.

  Again, we tracked the two disordered client proteins Hst5 and N49, which were found to be sensitive to scaffold identity as shown above, within this engineered condensate series. We observed that Hst5 conformation was largely conserved between these different environments (Figure  B, PC > 0.90), with only a slight weakening of the N-terminal local turn in mfp-3S_v3, the scaffold that possesses the most segregated sticker patterning. More pronounced conformation alteration was observed for N49 compared to both the reference mfp-3S condensate and solution state (Figures  B and S11A, PC < 0.90). With increasing sticker segregation, formation of the N-terminal local turn structure was hindered while local interactions involving the C-terminal were enhanced. Notably, a local turn near residue 20 was induced within the mfp-3S_v3 condensate (Figure  C). These results point to another possible functional role for scaffold sticker patterning: the remodeling of client conformation behavior in addition to affecting the material properties of the condensate itself.


### Nonuniform and Distinct Client–Condensate Interaction
Maps Contribute to Client Conformation Remodeling
  To provide further insight into condensate-specific client conformation remodeling, we mapped the residue-level interaction landscapes between the clients and their condensate scaffold environments (Figures  A and S11B). Our analysis revealed that client–condensate interactions are both highly divergent between different condensate pairs (PC ≪ 0.9, Table S8) and nonuniform along the client sequence, despite the dense and fluid nature of surrounding condensates. This is exemplified by a number of observations (highlighted in Figure  A): (i) the C-terminal (RS) n  repeat region of the RS peptide formed extensive interactions with FUSLCD and mfp-3S condensates but showed a much more uniform interaction intensity upon entering the WGR5 condensate, (ii) the primary interaction site for Hst5 shifted along its sequence depending on the condensate scaffold, from the central region in WGR5 toward the C-terminal region within FUSLCD and mfp-3S condensates, and (iii) similar to the RS peptide, the interaction profile of N49 in the WGR5 condensate remained uniform while showing preferred regions of interaction in the other condensates. In particular, the N-terminal fragment (residue 1–10) exhibited opposite interaction preferences between FUSLCD and mfp-3S condensates, with the C-half of this fragment interacting more with the mfp-3S condensate than its N-half region, whereas the opposite was observed for the FUSLCD condensate. The interaction preference of this fragment was also transformed upon clustering of aromatic stickers in mfp-3S_v3 (Figure S11B), getting closer to the mode observed in the FUSLCD condensate environment. Interestingly, although sticker segregation increases monotonically from mfp-3S to mfp-3S_v3, the changes in the client–scaffold interaction profiles did not follow a simple monotonic trend. Most notably, mfp-3S_v2 exhibited the weakest interaction preference for the N-terminal fragment of both the N49 and Hst5 clients while displaying the strongest preference for the central region.

  In line with the specific client–condensate interaction fingerprints, the desolvation of each client site was also not uniform, but highly correlated with the newly formed competing client–scaffold interactions within the condensate environments (Figure S12). This site-specific desolvation gave rise to local hydration levels that deviate from the average water content within the condensates (Figure S2). Collectively, our results reveal that the clients experience a spatially heterogeneous “solvation” environment, composed of complementary water solvation and scaffold-mediated “solvation”, both contributing to the client conformational remodeling.

  Next, we aimed to correlate client–condensate interactions with client conformation remodeling. To this end, we plotted the interchain interaction propensity of each residue against its change in intrachain engagement for the seven condensate–client pairs experiencing significant client conformation alteration, as defined by a PC < 0.90 between their 1D intrachain contact profile and the reference (cf. Figures  –). As shown in Figures  B, S9C, and S11C, significant negative correlations were observed in five of the seven cases (only weak correlations in the WGR5-RS peptide and the (WGK)4–N49 system, whose client–condensate interaction was near-uniform), indicating that strong scaffold interaction for a given client residue typically limits its contact with intrachain partners. This intuitive negative correlation underlines the role of the specific condensate milieu as the conformational editor of the client conformation.


### Protein Primary Sequence Is Not the Sole Encoder of Condensate–Client
Coupling
  Disordered proteins lacking fixed 3D structure engage in biomolecule condensates by multivalent residue-level interactions encoded by primary sequence grammar. ,−     However, it remains unclear whether these rules apply to explain condensate–client interaction profiles independent of other condensate features. To decouple sequence-based preferences from condensate-specific effects, we compared the condensate scaffold–client interaction profile within the condensate to that in a solution state of the heterodimer (Figure  A). To this end, 20 μs triplicate simulations for each single-chain client–scaffold protein pair in solution were performed. This comparison confirmed that the primary interaction sites on client proteins in the solution state dimer, such as the N49 motif near residue 20 and C-terminal (RS) n  repeat region of the RS peptide, were largely maintained in the corresponding condensate, with significant positive correlations for several pairs (FUSLCD, mfp-3S condensate scaffolds and N49, RS peptide client proteins, Figure  B).

  However, significant reprogramming of client–scaffold interaction landscapes was also observed in some systems. Notably, the weak interactions engaged by the client termini in solution were significantly enhanced within all condensates, pointing to a consistent change in the client terminus behavior within the dense condensate environment. Another striking example is the WGR5 condensate, which imposed near-uniform interaction intensity along all three clients despite their intrinsic sequence-encoded preferences observed in the solution-state dimer. We attribute this to the close sticker distance and high sticker density in this short WGR5 peptide sequence, creating a near-homogeneous and dense interaction phase that engulfs the client.

  Additionally, for the mfp-3S variant series with tuned sticker patterning, we also observed client–condensate interaction landscapes not predicted from their primary sequence. As shown in Figure S13, while both mfp-3S variants displayed almost identical interaction preference to N49 in the solution-state dimer, their interaction profile with the N49 N-terminal fragment diverged in the condensate condition. We hypothesize that in the mfp-3S condensate, the scaffold network hub consisting of the motif near residue 5 and the negatively charged C-terminus compete for the N49 N-terminal fragment, suppressing its interaction to the mfp-3S motif near residue 35. On the contrary, the mfp-3S_v3 with more clustered stickers generates a condensate with stronger network hubs integrating all these three motifs (i.e., the motifs near residue 5 and 35 together with the C-terminus), leading to the observed enhancement in N49 N-terminal interactions (Figure S14).

  Together, these results revealed that condensate–client coupling is not solely determined by the primary sequence grammar but also cooperatively shaped by the emergent internal structure of the condensates. The internal network formed by scaffolds may reorganize the spatial distribution of interaction motifs and, thereby, affect interactions with clients. Thus, client conformation remodeling within condensates depends on a combination of scaffold sequence features and the emergent biophysical consequences those features have on condensate structure.


## Discussion
  Membrane-less organelles, also referred to as biomolecular condensates, provide dynamic subcellular compartments. The specific functions of these compartments arise not only from passive component concentration modulation (selective concentration, exclusion, or sequestration of client molecules) but also via the potential of client conformation remodeling within this distinct microenvironment. While isolated reports have hinted at this possibility, e.g., showing nucleic acid duplexes dehybridizing in various condensates and protein conformation folding equilibrium shifting, −    a systematic investigation into the scope and drivers of client conformational alteration across diverse condensate environments is still lacking. To start filling this gap, we systematically tracked three disordered client proteins within a panel of condensates using the newly developed Martini3-IDP model, revealing condensate-specific client conformation effects and identifying some key determinants of client remodeling.

  We first established that disordered client protein conformations were indeed sensitive to the condensate environment. Across three distinct condensates with varying scaffold chain lengths and sequence compositions, clients generally adopted more expanded conformations compared to their counterpart in solution, consistent with conformation expansion previously reported in homotypic IDR-only condensates. ,  However, divergent client conformations were observed for different condensate–client pairs. For instance, the solution-state conformation mode of client N49 was preserved upon entering WGR5 and mfp-3S condensates, while its N-terminal turn structure was significantly suppressed within the FUSLCD condensate. Although client conformation expansion was observed in all 9 condensate–client pairs, this cannot be considered as a general rule which could be easily extrapolated to other systems. For instance, NMR experiments point to a compact structure of the disordered 4E-BP2 protein inside a DDX4 condensate. As another counter example, we found a poly-Leucine (Leu30) peptide to become more compact after entering WGR5 and mfp-3S condensates (Figure S17). This compaction suggests that in these cases, the condensate acts as a poor solvent for the client, yet entropic factors (release of bound solvent particles) still provide a driving force for entering the condensate. ,  Our results with an inert PEO crowder, causing compaction of the clients rather than expansion, underline these effects.

  Scaffold chain length and linear sticker patterning, two factors found to be related to condensate material properties, −     were dissected next. Surprisingly, condensates stabilized by an increased scaffold chain length attenuated both the global and local motion of clients. Together with the dual slowdown of clients within dense WGR5 condensate (∼600 mg/mL protein density), these results suggest that the local residue contact lifetime, which was reported to remain comparable to solution condition, could also be retarded within certain condensate environments. Additionally, longer scaffolds promote subtle further expansion of clients, likely driven by the more extensive client–scaffold interactions within scaffold-denser condensates (Figure S9D), yet leave sequence-specific intrachain contact patterns largely preserved. By contrast, linear sticker clustering along the sequence induced more pronounced client conformation alterations, demonstrating that the spatial organization of interaction sites exerts influence over the client structure. These findings reveal that chain length and sticker patterning could modulate client conformation through distinct mechanisms and point to an important design principle: tuning condensate material properties requires careful consideration of the potential impacts on client conformation outcomes.

  We further found that the nonuniform and distinct client–condensate interaction maps provided a reasonable explanation for condensate-specific client conformation remodeling. Negative correlations between the client’s residue-level interchain interactions with the condensate environment and client’s residue-level intrachain contacts reflect an intuitive competitive mechanism that stronger scaffold interaction of a client limits its availability for native intrachain interactions. However, this client–condensate interaction profile is not a simple readout of primary sequence grammar; the emergent condensate structure cooperatively shaped the interaction preference between the client and scaffold.

  IDP clients shorter than typical IDP systems were investigated in this work, considering the simulation box size limitation and sampling convergence. But we believe our core conclusions should be general and could be largely extrapolated to longer IDPs. The underlying competition between intrachain and interchain interactions, that governed client conformation remodeling, operates locally along the client chain, ,  regardless of chain length. Consistent with that, our Flory scaling analysis indicates that the client IDP expansion behavior within condensates follows polymer physics principles and is length-invariant. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that longer IDPs may introduce additional complexity such as long-range cooperativity. Whether and how this long-range cooperativity is being differently modulated by crowding and networking condensate microenvironments remains an important question for future investigation.

  There are also some limitations that chart a course for future work: (1) In previous studies, formation of the transient local helix in the disordered activation domain of the androgen receptor was found to enhance phase separation and form a transient pocket that facilitates ligand binding. Similarly, a poly glycine–arginine peptide exhibited an interaction-dependent secondary structure profile when using different crowders. The current Martini protein model is not able to capture the transient secondary structure changes of disordered client proteins within different condensate environments. We expect that this could be resolved with a multiscale strategy or with a foldable Martini protein model in future work. (2) Our IDR-only scaffold–client systems represent a simplification of natural condensates that often include structured domains. Future studies incorporating folded domains will be essential to understand client behavior in condensates of endogenous complexity. ,


## Conclusion
  In summary, this study systematically investigated how different condensate environments affect the conformational ensembles of disordered client proteins. We demonstrate a regulator role of condensates, reprogramming the client conformation in a condensate-specific manner. We establish that this regulatory capacity could be affected by both the sequence patterning of scaffold stickers and the chain length through distinct mechanisms. Moreover, our findings indicate the client–condensate interaction profile is not simply dictated by primary sequence grammar and suggest a comprehensive interpretation in client conformation remodeling which combines scaffold sequence features and the emergent condensate structure.


## Materials and Methods

### MD Simulation Protocol
  The Martini3-IDP model was used for all condensate simulations in this study, with the inclusion of refined protein side chain parameters (https://github.com/WangLiguo-kyrie/Martini3_sc), as proposed in our previous work. ,  Martini topology and the initial structures of disordered proteins were generated using Polyply based on sequence fasta files. The sequences of all scaffold proteins in this work are given in Table S10, and sequences of client proteins are listed in Figure  A.

  In the Martini simulations, following the standard protocol, ,  the Verlet-scheme was used with the default energy drift buffer. The Verlet neighbor search algorithm is used to update the neighbor list every 20 steps with a buffer tolerance of 0.005 kJ/mol ps. By default, the nrexcl = 1 setting is applied in Martini, implying that 1–4 nonbonded interactions are on. Potential shift was used to calculate van der Waals interactions with a 1.1 nm cutoff, and Coulomb interactions were treated by a reaction field with relative dielectric constant 15 and 1.1 nm cutoffs. Production simulations were run with a 20 fs step size, using a velocity-rescaling thermostat (temperature coupling constant 1 ps) and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat (pressure coupling constant 12 ps). In direct-coexistence LLPS simulations, semi-isotropic pressure coupling was used keeping the XY plane uncompressed. All simulations were executed by Gromacs, versions 2021.7, and 2022.


### System Setup
  Direct-coexistence simulations were performed in a rectangular box; the X/Y axis dimensions were set to 12 and 10 nm, with the Z-dimension 70 and 80 nm, as listed in Table S1. Depending on the protein, between 36 and 762 copies of the scaffold proteins were included to keep the condensate phase thick enough to recruit clients.

  To simulate clients within condensate environments, equilibrated scaffold-only condensates were prepared first before the addition of the clients. The scaffold protein copies were inserted into a relatively small slab region as an initial configuration to accelerate LLPS and simulation convergence. The simulation box was then expanded to 70 or 80 nm along the Z axis. Several microsecond simulations were run to obtain well-fused and stable protein condensates, where the density profiles remained unchanged when evaluated over successive time windows.

  A single copy of the disordered client protein was then inserted into the dilute phase, and center of mass pulling was used to accelerate the client recruitment process. The system with client protein pulled into the condensate bulk region was then equilibrated by two consecutive NPT equilibrations at 333 and 298 K. Finally, 15–40 μs production simulations under physiological 298 K condition were obtained for condensate–client systems, as listed in Table S2, ensuring simulation convergence (see R g temporal evolution in Figures S18 and S19 and blocking analysis of R g in Figures S20 and S21). Each condensate–client system contained only a single copy of the client protein to avoid interference between client proteins. The resulting volume fraction ratio between the client and scaffold was around 3:1000, ensuring that scaffold proteins dominate the condensate structure. All client proteins resided within the condensate bulk region during the simulation, with one quick leaving-recruitment exchange observed only in the mfp-3S/N49 system (Figure S22).

  In simulations of clients within the PEO crowding phase, one client IDP molecule and ∼400 mg/mL (PEO)40 molecules (of length of 40 monomers) were randomly inserted into a cubic box with system-dependent box size, avoiding periodic interference, as listed in Table S2. PEO parameters were taken from the Polyply library. Systems were simulated for 15 μs, ensuring simulation convergence.

  In simulations of solution-state single-chain monomer and dimer systems, IDPs were inserted into a dodecahedron box with system-dependent box size, avoiding periodic interference, as listed in Tables S3 and S4. Solution-state single-chain monomer systems were simulated for 5–10 μs, whereas solution-state dimer systems were simulated in triplicate for 20 μs to ensure convergence. Almost all heterodimers experienced fully dissociation–association events during each simulation replica, and parallel replicas from different initial positions exhibited close client–scaffold interaction profiles for each heterodimer, demonstrating the convergence and analysis robustness (Figures S25 and S26).


### Analysis of MD Simulations
  The R g of the disordered client proteins, both within the condensates and as solution-state single-chain monomers, was calculated by gmx gyrate. The end-to-end distance of client proteins was calculated by the gmx distance using two terminal backbone beads. The end-to-end distance autocorrelation function was calculated by gmx analyze-ac with single-exponential fitting, and correlation times were estimated by integrating the fit to the normalized autocorrelation function. The associated standard error estimate was computed by bootstrapping: the end-to-end evolution data was randomly resampled 100 times with replacement, and the error estimation was calculated as the standard deviation of the correlation times obtained.

  The asphericity metrics of client proteins was analyzed using the MDAnalysis package. ,  Flory scaling exponents, ν, were obtained by fitting  ⟨Rij2⟩=R0|i−j|v , using root-mean-square residue–residue distances,  ⟨Rij2⟩ , calculated for greater than five residue separations along the linear sequence and with R 0 = 0.55 nm as suggested for disordered proteins. ,

  In the contact profile analysis, two residues were considered to be in contact if the minimum distance between their backbone beads fell within a 1 nm cutoff, following the setting in preceding work. Distances between backbone beads were used instead of the distances between all beads of the residues to facilitate efficiency. Based on the cutoff analysis in this work, the contact analysis conclusions should be robust to the choice of cutoff. In intrachain contact analysis, contacts between neighboring residues within three residues along the sequence were excluded. The contact propensity of a specific residue was treated as the number of residues in contact with itself. 1D and 2D contact profiles in this study were analyzed using the MDAnalysis package with 1 ns frequency. ,  1D residue interaction propensity similarity along the sequence was estimated by the Pearson correlation coefficient using Scipy. The error estimate for the Pearson correlation coefficient is computed by bootstrapping: the 1D residue interaction data during simulation were randomly resampled 100 times with replacement, and the error estimation was calculated as the standard deviation of the Pearson correlation coefficients obtained.

  Lifetimes of residue–residue contacts were calculated by a transition-based approach. Specifically, a dual-cutoff scheme was used to determine the formation and breaking of contacts instead of using a single distance cutoff to distinguish bound versus unbound states, which tends to underestimate contact lifetimes. For each pair of residues, a contact was still defined based on the minimum distance between their backbone beads. A contact formation was defined to occur when this distance dropped below 0.8 nm; then an existing contact was considered to remain until the distance increased to more than 1.2 nm. The step size for interchain contact lifetimes in this study was 0.5 ns. The standard error estimate for mean contact lifetime was computed from the standard deviation of four independent blocks or, in the case of the heterodimers in solution, from the standard deviation of the three replicas.

  The solvation of individual client residues was computed as the amount of Martini3 water beads (corresponding to 4 water molecules) within 1 nm of its backbone bead. The desolvation was the solvation number loss upon entering condensates from solution.

  Mass density profiles were determined along the Z-axis, with protein density centered by the gmx density tool. Considering that bead masses in Martini 3 are usually slightly larger than actual atom group masses, a rescaling factor (the ratio of protein molar weight and bead molar weight in Martini3) was used to correct the mass density profile.

  To characterize the spatial distribution and clustering of sticker residues within condensates, we selected a bulk region of 9 × 9 × 15 nm3 around the center of geometry of each mfp-3S condensate with varying sticker patterning. The bulk region was partitioned into cubic voxels with a 1 nm edge size, and the local aromatic sticker residue density was determined by counting the number of sticker residues per voxel. Sticker clustering was measured by grouping residues whose backbone beads were within a cutoff distance of 1 nm into the same cluster, from which the probability distribution of the sticker cluster size was computed. The aromatic sticker patterning parameter was calculated referring to previous work by using the following equations. The total sequence aromatic asymmetry is first calculated as 1 σArototal=(fAro−fother)2 where f Aro reflects the fraction of aromatic sequences in the chain and f other is simply 1 – f Aro. Then, the local aromatic asymmetry (σAro local) was calculated by scanning over the sequence windows with 5-residue width. The average deviation of local aromatic asymmetry from the total aromatic asymmetry in the sequence was calculated using the following expression: 2 δAro=∑i=1N1×(σArolocal−σArototal)2N where N is the total number of windows, simply the sequence length minus the window width (5 in our case) + 1. Finally, the δAro values of a synthetic sequence with maximally clustered aromatic stickers were computed to normalize the patterning parameter Ω within the 0–1 range: 3 Ω=δAroδMAXAro
