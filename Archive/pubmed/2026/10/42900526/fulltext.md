# Complex post-traumatic stress disorder in refugees, asylum seekers, and internally displaced people: a systematic review and meta-analysis


## ABSTRACT
  Background: Forcibly displaced populations, including refugees, asylum seekers, and internally displaced persons, are frequently exposed to severe, prolonged and repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible. Such exposures increase the risk for complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), defined by core PTSD symptoms alongside disturbances in self-organisation (DSO), including affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational difficulties. However, the prevalence of CPTSD in forcibly displaced populations has not yet been quantitatively synthesised, and the added value of the CPTSD construct over PTSD in this population has not been examined.

  Methods: This systematic review and meta-analysis searched five databases from inception to January 2026. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to estimate pooled CPTSD prevalence, with pre-specified subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses.

  Results: Twenty studies met inclusion criteria, representing 17 independent samples (n = 12,504). Reported CPTSD prevalence varied from 3.0% to 74.6%. The random-effects pooled prevalence, retained as a descriptive summary of the included studies, was 20.9% (95% CI [12.6%, 32.6%]), the 95% prediction interval was [1.7%, 80.3%]. Between-study heterogeneity was very high (I² = 98.3%). Recruitment context was strongly associated with prevalence: estimates averaged 10.6% in community-based samples and 55.2% in treatment-seeking samples. Asylum seekers exhibited markedly higher prevalence than refugees. Across four studies, post-migration living difficulties, including legal insecurity, poverty, and marginalisation, were more strongly associated with DSO symptoms than with core PTSD symptoms, suggesting that ongoing structural adversity actively perpetuates complex trauma psychopathology beyond pre-migration trauma alone.

  Conclusion: Available evidence indicates marked variation in CPTSD prevalence across forcibly displaced populations rather than a single generalisable prevalence rate. Recruitment context accounts for part of this variation, but the limited and geographically uneven evidence base prevents global population-level inference. Future studies should use representative sampling and consistently report displacement, trauma, and post-migration characteristics to permit more informative explanations of between-population variation.


## 1. Introduction
  Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD) was formally recognised in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as a trauma-related disorder distinct from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (World Health Organization, 2019/2021). PTSD is characterised by re-experiencing of the traumatic event in the present, avoidance of trauma reminders, and a persistent sense of current threat (Brewin et al., 2017). CPTSD encompasses these core symptoms alongside disturbances in self-organisation (DSO), comprising affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational difficulties, reflecting the broader and more enduring consequences of sustained, repeated, or interpersonal trauma, particularly in contexts involving captivity, coercion, or dependency (Cloitre et al., 2013; Maercker et al., 2013).

  Refugees, asylum seekers, and internally displaced persons (IDPs) represent populations at particular risk for CPTSD given the severity and chronicity of their trauma exposures. Refugees are individuals recognised under international law as requiring protection after fleeing persecution across an international border (Gerasymenko et al., 2022). Asylum seekers are individuals who have fled their home country and are seeking international protection but whose claims for refugee status have not yet been legally determined (Douglas et al., 2019). In contrast, IDPs are individuals forcibly displaced within their country of origin by conflict, violence, human rights violations, or disaster (Gerasymenko et al., 2022). As of mid-2025, more than 117 million people were forcibly displaced globally, including approximately 42.5 million refugees, 8.4 million asylum seekers, and 67.8 million IDPs (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees 2025). Given the scale of global displacement, understanding the mental health consequences of these experiences represents a pressing public health priority.

  Forcibly displaced populations are exposed to severe and cumulative trauma across multiple displacement phases. Pre-migration experiences commonly include war, torture, persecution, and systematic violations of basic human rights, including the denial of safe housing and access to healthcare (Mellor et al., 2021). Post-displacement stressors such as legal uncertainty, family separation, poverty, unemployment, stigma, and exploitation compound prior trauma exposure (de Silva et al., 2021; Hecker et al., 2018). Ongoing concern for family in conflict zones, traumatic grief, and survivor's guilt may further intensify psychological burden (Cloitre, 2022; de Silva et al., 2021). This accumulation of interpersonal and chronic stressors maps closely onto the etiological conditions theorised to precipitate CPTSD (Tay et al., 2015).

  Distinguishing CPTSD from PTSD is not merely a nosological refinement. It has particular relevance for forcibly displaced populations. ICD-11 conceptualises CPTSD as the characteristic outcome of prolonged, repeated, and interpersonal trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible (Cloitre et al., 2013; Maercker et al., 2013), an exposure profile that closely matches persecution, torture, detention, and protracted displacement. Critically, the DSO symptoms that define CPTSD (affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbed relationships) are associated with greater functional impairment and psychiatric comorbidity than core PTSD symptoms alone (Cloitre et al., 2019; Karatzias et al., 2019a), and they map onto the difficulties, including shame, guilt, mistrust, and disrupted relationships, that are most likely to impede integration and help-seeking after resettlement (Gerasymenko et al., 2022; Tay et al., 2015). Prevalence research restricted to PTSD therefore risks underestimating and mischaracterising the mental health burden of forced displacement, and provides little guidance on whether the additional treatment targets implied by DSO are relevant at population scale. Establishing how frequently ICD-11 CPTSD occurs in displaced populations, and how its prevalence varies across settings, is thus a prerequisite both for epidemiological burden estimation and for treatment research asking whether displaced people with CPTSD have needs beyond those addressed by PTSD-focused care.

  Prevalence estimates of CPTSD vary substantially across populations and levels of trauma exposure. In a population-based study in the United States (US), CPTSD prevalence was estimated at 3.8% (Cloitre et al., 2019). In the United Kingdom (UK), cross-sectional studies reported a prevalence of 12.9% among trauma-exposed individuals (Karatzias et al., 2019b). In forcibly displaced populations, estimates range far more widely. For instance, a study of African refugees in Italy reported a CPTSD prevalence of 30% (Barbieri et al., 2019), while a study of asylum seekers in a humanitarian setting in Africa reported a prevalence of 74.6% (Barbieri et al., 2023). In contrast, a study of West Papuan refugees reported prevalence rates of 9.3% for CPTSD and 12.1% for PTSD (Silove et al., 2018). This heterogeneity in CPTSD presentations among forcibly displaced populations likely reflects the interplay of sociodemographic factors, such as younger age, female gender, low educational attainment, and social isolation (Hyland et al., 2021; Rawers et al., 2024), trauma-related variables, including interpersonal violence, detention and bereavement (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; Karstoft et al., 2024), as well as post-migration stressors, such as legal precarity, poverty, and marginalisation (de Silva et al., 2021; Hecker et al., 2018).

  Despite a growing body of research examining CPTSD in forcibly displaced populations, the extent and sources of variation in reported prevalence have not been systematically synthesised. Estimates are likely to differ across recruitment settings, displacement groups, conflict and resettlement contexts, and measurement approaches. The primary aim of this review was therefore to characterise the distribution and variability of CPTSD prevalence estimates among forcibly displaced adults and to examine whether available study-level characteristics accounted for this variation. The fact that reported prevalence estimates vary so widely across studies, ranging from 3% to over 70% (Barbieri et al., 2023; Hecker et al., 2018; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; Vang et al., 2020), complicates burden estimation and impedes evidence-based service planning. The present study addresses this gap by systematically reviewing the literature on CPTSD among refugees, asylum seekers, and IDPs, and quantitatively synthesising prevalence estimates as a descriptive summary of the included studies. A secondary aim was to synthesise evidence on demographic, clinical, trauma-related, and post-migration correlates of CPTSD.


## 2. Methods

### Study design
  This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (see Supplementary Table 1) (Page et al., 2021). The review protocol was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (Melkam et al., 2026).


### Search strategy
  A systematic literature search was conducted across MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Library. The search was conducted from the inception of each database through 28 January 2026. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a librarian at the University of Queensland. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), keywords, abbreviations, and acronyms of CPTSD and displacement were included. Search strings were adapted for each database and boolean operators, truncation symbols, and field tags were applied as appropriate. The core search concept combined terms for (i) CPTSD and (ii) displacement: “Complex PTSD” OR “ICD-11 complex PTSD” OR “cptsd” OR “complex post-traumatic stress disorder” OR “complex post-traumatic stress disorder” OR “C-PTSD” OR “complex trauma*” OR “developmental trauma*” OR “repeated trauma*” OR “chronic trauma*” OR “traumatic event*”AND “internally displaced” OR “internal displacement*” OR “IDP*” OR “refugee*” OR “asylum seeker*” OR “forcibly displaced*” OR “resettlement*” “forced migrant*” OR “displaced population*” OR “conflict-affected” OR “war-affected” OR “humanitarian crisis*” OR “emergency-affected” OR “emergency setting*” OR “civil war*” OR “people affected by war*” OR “war survivor*” OR “war exposure*” OR “traumatized refugee*” OR “post migration*” OR “mass casualty*” OR “post-migration*”.

  Reference lists of all included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible studies.


### Eligibility criteria
  Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (i) the study sample consisted of forcibly displaced adults (≥18 years), including refugees, asylum seekers, and IDPs, as defined by international legal frameworks (i.e. individuals who have been forcibly displaced from their homes due to conflict, persecution, or violence); (ii) the study reported the prevalence of ICD-11 CPTSD, either as a point prevalence or as a diagnostic proportion; (iii) CPTSD was assessed using a validated ICD-11-compatible instrument, such as the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) (Cloitre et al., 2018), a brief self-report measure developed specifically to operationalise ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD criteria across six symptom clusters, or other validated instruments with items explicitly mapped onto the ICD-11 CPTSD diagnostic criteria; (iv) the study employed an observational design (e.g. cross-sectional, cohort, or epidemiological survey); and (v) the study was published in a peer-reviewed journal in English.

  Studies were excluded if (i) the sample did not consist of forcibly displaced populations (e.g. general community residents affected by war, military veterans, or prisoners of war, who differ fundamentally from displaced populations in their exposure context and post-displacement circumstances); (ii) the study assessed PTSD without reporting CPTSD prevalence; (iii) the study employed an instrument which cannot yield valid ICD-11 CPTSD prevalence estimates; (iv) the study focused exclusively on children or adolescents (<18 years); (v) the study was a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT), review, case report, commentary, editorial, or qualitative study; or (vi) the study did not report sufficient data to calculate a prevalence estimate.


### Study selection and data extraction
  All identified records were imported into Covidence for screening (Hupe, 2025). Title and abstract screening was independently conducted by two reviewers (MM and CX), followed by full-text review of potentially eligible studies. At each stage, disagreements were resolved through discussion, and where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (LO) was consulted.   Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (MM and LO) using a standardised, pre-piloted extraction form developed in Excel. Discrepancy was again resolved through discussion. The following information was extracted from each study: first author and year of publication, study aims, study design, country or region of displacement origin, country of resettlement, sample characteristics (sample size, age [mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (ITQ), as reported], sex distribution, time since displacement), sample type (refugees, asylum seekers, IDPs, or mixed), and recruitment context (treatment-seeking vs. community-based). The CPTSD assessment instrument was recorded with explicit distinction between studies that used the ITQ directly and those that used other instruments with items mapped onto ICD-11 CPTSD criteria. CPTSD prevalence data were extracted as the number of cases and total sample size, or as a reported proportion. Risk factors examined, covariates adjusted for, statistical analyses, and key findings were also recorded. Where multiple publications reported data from the same or overlapping participant samples, only the study with the larger sample size was retained for the meta-analysis, to avoid non-independence of effect sizes.


### Quality assessment
  Methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies (Munn et al., 2014). Two reviewers (MM and LO) independently appraised each study, with disagreements resolved through discussion. In addition, because the validity of the CPTSD instrument in the language and population studied is critical to the interpretability of prevalence estimates, JBI item 6 (‘Were valid methods used for the identification of the condition?’) was expanded into a structured appraisal of instrument validity for every included study (informed by the approach of Hoppen et al., 2021) and adapted to self-report instruments (Hoppen et al., 2021). For each study we recorded (i) the instrument and language(s) of administration, (ii) whether translation and/or cultural adaptation procedures were reported, (iii) whether the instrument had been validated in the language(s) administered, (iv) whether it had been validated in a forcibly displaced population, and (v) the psychometric evidence reported in the study sample (Supplementary Table 2B). Item 6 was scored 1 where the instrument, in the language administered, had been validated in a forcibly displaced population, and 0 otherwise; this binary rating defined the instrument-validation subgroups used in the meta-analysis.


### Data synthesis and statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 (RStudio Team, 2023), using the meta and metafor packages. For the primary analysis, the pooled prevalence of ICD-11 CPTSD was estimated using a random-effects model. Individual study proportions were transformed using the logit transformation prior to pooling, as this approach provides appropriate confidence intervals for proportions. Study-specific 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method and results are presented as back-transformed pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, which describes the proportion of observed variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity, and τ², which provides an absolute measure of between-study variance on the logit scale. The significance of heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. A 95% prediction interval was also calculated, providing a more clinically interpretable measure of heterogeneity by estimating the range of prevalence values within which the true prevalence of a future comparable study would be expected to fall. Because the included studies sampled heterogeneous populations rather than a single underlying global population, the random-effects mean was conceptualised as the average of the distribution of study-specific prevalence parameters represented in the available literature. It was not interpreted as the prevalence of CPTSD among all forcibly displaced adults globally.

  To this end, pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity using mixed-effects models (random effects within subgroups, fixed effects for the between-subgroup comparisons). The following categorical moderators were examined: (1) CPTSD measurement approach (ITQ vs. other ICD-11-mapped instruments); (2) recruitment context (treatment-seeking vs. community-based samples); (3) displacement subgroup (refugees vs. asylum seekers vs. IDPs vs. mixed samples); (4) income classification of the country of resettlement, based on the World Bank 2024/2025 classification (World Bank, 2025); and (5) instrument validation status in the language and population studied. Meta-regression was used to examine the associations between study-level characteristics and CPTSD prevalence, where sufficient data were available. Planned moderators included mean time since displacement, mean age, and proportion of female participants.

  Two pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimate. First, a leave-one-out analysis was performed, sequentially removing each study to identify disproportionate influence on the pooled estimate. Second, analyses were stratified by JBI quality rating to assess whether methodological quality influenced the pooled prevalence. Publication bias was assessed visually by funnel plot inspection of logit-transformed study proportions against their standard errors, and formally tested using Egger’s and Peters’ tests.


## Results

### Study selection
  A total of 4,700 articles were identified through database searches (MEDLINE = 884; Cochrane Library = 40; Embase = 850; CINAHL = 352; PsycINFO = 2,574). After removal of 1,472 duplicates, 3,228 records remained for screening. A total of 20 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review (Andisha et al., 2023; Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020). The systematic search and screening process is summarised in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Inter-rater agreement during title and abstract screening was high (observed agreement = 97.8%), with a Cohen’s κ of 0.45, indicating moderate agreement. The modest κ reflects the substantial class imbalance inherent in large-scale screening, where the majority of records are excluded. Three pairs of studies drew from overlapping participant samples: Hyland et al. (2018) and Vallières et al. (2018) reported on the same sample of treatment-seeking Syrian refugees in Lebanon. Karstoft et al. (2024, 2025) analysed the same cohort of Ukrainian refugees in Denmark, and Silove et al. (2017) and Tay et al. (2015) used the same sample of West Papuan refugees in Papua New Guinea. Vallières et al. (2018), Karstoft et al. (2024) and Tay et al. (2015) were retained for the meta-analysis on the basis of larger sample size.

  Total sample sizes across the 20 studies ranged from 94 (Hecker et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023) to 6,993 (Karstoft et al., 2024), with a combined total of 12,504 participants across all independent samples. The majority of studies sampled refugees (k = 13) (Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Hyland et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019; Rawers et al., 2024; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), with fewer studies focusing on asylum seekers (k = 3) (Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2023; Jowett et al., 2021), IDPs (k = 1) (McGinty et al., 2023), or mixed samples of refugees and asylum seekers (k = 3) (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018). Seven studies recruited treatment-seeking participants from clinical or specialist trauma services (Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Hyland et al., 2018; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020) or a mixture of treatment-seeking and community-based participants (Andisha et al., 2023; Liddell et al., 2019), while the remaining 13 recruited community-based or population-based samples (Bachem et al., 2024; Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018).


#### Participant demographics
  Where reported as a mean, participant average age ranged from 25.1 years (SD = 6.7) (Barbieri et al., 2019) to 50.9 years (SD = 17.1) (Frost et al., 2019). Three studies reported age as a median instead, ranging from 35.5 years (IQR = 24.7–44.0) (Benoni et al., 2025) to 40 years (IQR = 22) (Karstoft et al., 2025). The proportion of male participants varied considerably across studies, from 0% (Bachem et al., 2024), comprising exclusively female asylum-seeking mothers, to 86% (Barbieri et al., 2023). The majority of studies (k = 12) included both male and female participants, with most samples having a higher proportion of males (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Liddell et al., 2019; Rawers et al., 2024; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018; Vang et al., 2020). Notable exceptions included studies of Ukrainian displaced populations, which consistently reported a higher proportion of females (68–81%) (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; Karstoft et al., 2025; McGinty et al., 2023), while one study did not report sex distribution (Ipekci, 2023).


#### Geographic distribution
  Participants originated from a wide range of displacement contexts. The most frequently represented regions of origin were Ukraine (k = 4) (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023), Syria (k = 4) (Hyland et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), West Papua (k = 3) (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; Tay et al., 2018), and sub-Saharan Africa (k = 3) (Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023). Several studies drew from mixed-origin samples (Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Liddell et al., 2019; Vang et al., 2020). Resettlement countries included Denmark (k = 3) (Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Vang et al., 2020), Papua New Guinea (k = 3) (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018), Italy (k = 2) (Barbieri et al., 2019; Benoni et al., 2025), Lebanon (k = 2) (Hyland et al., 2018; Vallières et al., 2018), the US (k = 2) (Frost et al., 2019; Ipekci, 2023), and one study each in Australia (Liddell et al., 2019), Austria (Andisha et al., 2023), Israel (Bachem et al., 2024), Niger (Barbieri et al., 2023), Switzerland (Hecker et al., 2018), Turkey (Rawers et al., 2024), and the UK (Jowett et al., 2021). One study examined internally displaced persons within Ukraine (McGinty et al., 2023). Examining the country level income is one of the environmental factors that determine the psychological reactions of the participants.


#### Time since displacement
  Time since displacement was reported in 12 of the 20 studies, and varied substantially across samples. Durations ranged from approximately 10–22 months in recently displaced Ukrainian populations (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023) to over 27 years among West Papuan refugees in protracted displacement (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015).


#### CPTSD measurements
  A critical distinction emerged in how CPTSD was operationalised across studies. The majority of studies (k = 14) used the ITQ or a validated language adaptation thereof (Arabic, Dari, Tigrinya, Ukrainian, or Russian versions) (Andisha et al., 2023; Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020). The remaining six studies instead mapped items from other instruments onto ICD-11 CPTSD criteria: (Barbieri et al., 2019) mapped items from the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), (Frost et al., 2019) used the Alcohol Use Disorder and Associated Disabilities Interview Schedule-IV (AUDADIS-IV), (Liddell et al., 2019) combined the PTSD Symptom Scale -Interview (PSS-I) with a precursor version of the ITQ for DSO symptoms, and (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2016; Tay et al., 2018) used the Refugee Mental Health Assessment Package (R-MHAP).


#### CPTSD prevalence
  Reported CPTSD prevalence estimates varied widely across studies, ranging from 3.0% (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015) to 74.6% (Barbieri et al., 2023). This variability likely reflected differences in sample characteristics, recruitment context, and measurement approach. Among treatment-seeking samples, CPTSD prevalence was generally higher, ranging from 30.0% (Barbieri et al., 2019) to 74.6% (Barbieri et al., 2023). The highest rates (74.6%) were observed among treatment-seeking asylum seekers in Niger (Barbieri et al., 2023), 66.9% among treatment-seeking refugees in Denmark (Vang et al., 2020), and 66.2% among asylum seekers and survivors of trafficking referred to a specialist trauma service in the UK (Jowett et al., 2021). Among community-based samples, prevalence was generally lower, ranging from 3% (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015) to 23.6% (Bachem et al., 2024), with several studies reporting estimates between 7.8% and 13.5% (Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Ipekci, 2023; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Rawers et al., 2024; Tay et al., 2018). Two studies recruited mixed treatment-seeking and community-based samples with prevalence rates of 20.7% (Andisha et al., 2023) and 29.5% (Liddell et al., 2019). Prevalence also appeared to differ by measurement approach. Among the 14 studies using the ITQ directly, estimates ranged from 7.8% (McGinty et al., 2023) to 74.6% (Barbieri et al., 2023). Among the six studies that mapped other instruments onto ICD-11 criteria, estimates ranged from 3.0% (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015) to 30.0% (Barbieri et al., 2019).


#### Risk factors
  Risk factors examined across studies varied considerably in type and frequency. The most commonly examined variables included sex (k = 15) (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), age (k = 14) (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Frost et al., 2019; Hyland et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019; Rawers et al., 2024; Tay et al., 2015; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), trauma exposure or cumulative trauma load (k = 10) (Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019; Tay et al., 2015; 2018), and post-migration living difficulties (k = 4) (Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2025; Tay et al., 2018). Several studies examined pre-migration stressors alongside post-migration factors (Hecker et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Karstoft et al., 2025; Liddell et al., 2019). Other risk factors examined in individual studies included coping styles (McGinty et al., 2023), reception conditions (Barbieri et al., 2023), discrimination (Ipekci, 2023), perceived social support (Hecker et al., 2018; Karstoft et al., 2025), visa or legal status (Liddell et al., 2019), and socio-ecological stressors (Benoni et al., 2025). A recurring finding across multiple studies was that post-migration living difficulties were more strongly associated with DSO symptoms than with core PTSD symptoms, while trauma exposure was more consistently associated with PTSD symptoms (Hecker et al., 2018; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; Liddell et al., 2019).


### Risk of bias
  Pre-consensus inter-rater reliability on the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist was moderate (ICC[A,1] = 0.58, 95% CI [0.21, 0.81]). All disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion to produce final agreed scores. Final JBI quality scores ranged from 4 to 9 (out of a possible 9). Eight studies were rated as low risk of bias (scored 7–9) (Bachem et al., 2024; Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018), and 12 as moderate risk of bias (scores 4–6) (Andisha et al., 2023; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Ipekci, 2023; Jowett et al., 2021; Liddell et al., 2019; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020). No studies were rated as high risk of bias. The full quality appraisal ratings for each study are presented in Supplementary Table 2.


### Instrument validation
  Fourteen studies used the ITQ (Andisha et al., 2023; Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020) and six mapped items from other instruments onto the ICD-11 CPTSD criteria (Barbieri et al., 2019; Frost et al., 2019; Liddell et al., 2019; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018) (Supplementary Table 2B). Written instrument versions in 15 distinct languages were used across studies, and nine studies administered instruments in more than one language; three studies additionally relied on interpreters for a substantial proportion of participants (Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Vang et al., 2020). Arabic (Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020) and English (Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Frost et al., 2019; Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Liddell et al., 2019; Tay et al., 2018) were the most frequent, followed by Ukrainian (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023), French (Barbieri et al., 2019; 2023; Hecker et al., 2018), Russian (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023), and Bahasa Indonesian (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018). Bosnian (Vang et al., 2020), Danish (Vang et al., 2020), Dari (Andisha et al., 2023), German (Hecker et al., 2018), Spanish (Hecker et al., 2018), Tamil (Hecker et al., 2018), Tigrinya (Bachem et al., 2024), Tok Pisin (Tay et al., 2018), and Turkish (Hecker et al., 2018) were each used in a single study. Nine studies explicitly reported a translation and/or cultural adaptation process (Andisha et al., 2023; Bachem et al., 2024; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), four used original English-language versions requiring no translation (Barbieri et al., 2019; Frost et al., 2019; Jowett et al., 2021; Liddell et al., 2019), and the remaining seven provided no details regarding translation or cultural adaptation (Barbieri et al., 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Ipekci, 2023; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025; McGinty et al., 2023; Rawers et al., 2024).

  Psychometric evidence for the version administered was reported for the study sample in 15 of the 20 studies and was largely confined to internal consistency (Supplementary Table 2B): reliability was reported separately for the PTSD and DSO clusters in 13 studies [12 independent samples): across the ITQ-based and mapped self-report instruments Cronbach’s α (or composite reliability) ranged from .70 to .89 for PTSD and from .75 to .94 for DSO (Andisha et al., 2023; Bachem et al., 2024; Barbieri et al., 2023; Benoni et al., 2025; Hecker et al., 2018; Hyland et al., 2018; Liddell et al., 2019; Rawers et al., 2024; Vallières et al., 2018; Vang et al., 2020), and reached .88–.97 for the dichotomous, interviewer-administered R-MHAP (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; 2018). Two further studies reported reliability for total scores only (α = .81 for the mapped PCL-5 items [Barbieri et al., 2019]; α = .89 [McGinty et al., 2023]), and five reported no psychometric statistics for their sample (Frost et al., 2019; Ipekci, 2023; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2024; 2025). Reliability at the level of individual symptom clusters was reported in only one study and was weak for hypervigilance and affective dysregulation (α = .55 and .53; [Vang et al., 2020]). Test–retest stability was examined in a single sample (six-month follow-up, n = 101; [Tay et al., 2015]). Confirmatory factor analysis of the CPTSD instrument in the study sample was reported for five independent samples: the ICD-11 two-factor second-order structure of PTSD and DSO was supported for the Dari ITQ (CFI .965, TLI .950, RMSEA .056; [Andisha et al., 2023]) and the Arabic ITQ (Vallières et al., 2018), and a correlated six-factor structure fitted the mapped AUDADIS-IV items (Frost et al., 2019), whereas in the three West Papuan studies using the culturally adapted R-MHAP, a unitary or correlated six-factor solution was preferred, and higher-order PTSD/DSO models fitted poorly or failed to converge (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015; Tay et al., 2018). Population-specific validation among forcibly displaced populations was limited. The English, Arabic, Dari, Danish, and Bosnian versions of the ITQ, and the Bahasa Indonesian version of the R-MHAP, had been validated in forcibly displaced populations. Overall, 10 of the 20 studies (8 of the 17 independent samples in the meta-analysis) were rated as having used an instrument validated in the language and population studied.


### Meta-analysis: pooled prevalence of CPTSD
  Using a random-effects meta-analysis (k = 17, n = 12,504), the pooled prevalence of CPTSD among refugees, asylum seekers, and IDPs was 20.9% (95% CI [12.6%, 32.6%], see Figure 2). The 95% prediction interval was [1.7%, 80.3%], indicating that the true prevalence in a comparable future study could plausibly fall almost anywhere within this range. Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 98.3%, τ2 = 1.57, Q(16) = 936.97, p < .001). The subgroup analysis by measurement tool did not reveal a significant difference, Q(1) = 2.64, p = .104. Studies using the ITQ (k = 12) yielded a pooled prevalence of 26.3% (95% CI [15.1%, 41.7%], I2 = 98.7%, τ2 = 1.47, Q(11) = 843.62, p < .001) while studies using mapped instruments (k = 5) yielded a pooled prevalence of 11.3% (95% CI [4.3%, 26.4%], I2 = 95.6%, τ2 = 1.34, Q(4) = 90.67, p < .001; see Supplementary Figure 1). A subgroup analysis based on recruitment context indicated a significant difference in CPTSD prevalence between groups, Q(2) = 31.72, p < .001. The pooled prevalence in community-based samples (k = 10) was 10.6% (95% CI [7.4%, 15.0%], I2 = 91.5%, τ2 = 0.36, Q(9) = 105.72, p < .001). In treatment-seeking samples (k = 5), the pooled prevalence was substantially higher at 55.2% (95% CI [36.7%, 72.3%], I2 = 95.1%, τ2 = 0.7, Q(4) = 81.35, p < .001). Mixed samples (k = 2) yielded an intermediate pooled prevalence of 24.3% (95% CI [16.9%, 33.8%], I2 = 71.8%, τ2 = 0.08, Q(1) = 3.55, p = .059; see Supplementary Figure 2). Given that this subgroup comprised only two studies, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. The subgroup analysis of CPTSD prevalence based on displacement also revealed significant between-group differences, Q(3) = 64.0, p < .001. The pooled prevalence among refugees (k = 10) was 15.2% (95% CI [7.8%, 27.4%], I2 = 98.2%, τ2 = 1.4, Q(9) = 498.86, p < .001). Among asylum seekers (k = 3), the pooled prevalence was substantially higher at 54.8% (95% CI [23.8%, 82.5%], I2 = 97%, τ2 = 1.4, Q(2) = 66.77, p < .001). Mixed samples (k = 3; including both refugees and asylum seekers) yielded a pooled prevalence of 23.6% (95% CI [18.3%, 29.9%], I2 = 54.4%, τ2 = 0.04, Q(2) = 4.38, p = .111). For IDPs, only one study was available (k = 1), reporting a prevalence of 7.8% (95% CI [6.7%, 9.0%]); therefore, no pooled estimate or heterogeneity statistics could be calculated (see Supplementary Figure 3). A subgroup analysis based on the income classification of the country of resettlement (dichotomised as high-income versus low- and middle-income countries because too few studies were available for finer categories) was not statistically significant, Q(1) = 0.37, p = .544 (Supplementary Figure 4). The pooled prevalence among high-income countries (k = 11) was 23.6% (95% CI [13.8%, 37.3%], I2 = 98.3%, τ2 = 1.18, Q(10) = 571.51, p <.001  . Among low-income countries (k = 6) the pooled prevalence was 16.5% (95% CI [5.20%, 41.7%]), I2 = 98.5%, τ2 = 2.53, Q(5) = 333.09, p < .001). Subgroup analysis by instrument validation status (Supplementary Figure 5) was also not significant, Q(1) = 0.19, p = .661. Among studies using an instrument validated in the language and population studied (k = 8), the pooled prevalence was 18.6% (95% CI [8.70%, 35.3%], I2 = 98.1%, τ2 = 1.53, Q(7) = 373.66, p < .001). Similarly, among studies using an instrument without such validation (k = 9) the pooled prevalence was 23.1% (95% CI [11.1%, 42.0%]), I2 = 98.3%, τ2 = 1.77, Q(8) = 462.86, p < .001. Meta-regression analyses revealed that neither mean age, proportion of female participants, nor time since displacement significantly moderated CPTSD prevalence, though the limited number of studies available for time since displacement (k = 8) constrains interpretability. #### Sensitivity analysis
  Sequential leave-one-out sensitivity analyses revealed that exclusion of any single study did not materially alter the pooled prevalence (range: 19–23% (see Supplementary Figure 3)), indicating that the overall findings were robust. A sensitivity analysis restricting the meta-analysis to methodologically high-quality studies (JBI scores of 7–9) yielded a pooled CPTSD prevalence of 10.4% (95% CI [6.3%, 16.8%]). Substantial heterogeneity remained (I² = 94.0%, τ² = 0.44), indicating that between-study variability persisted even among studies with higher methodological quality.


#### Publication bias
  Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated substantial dispersion, consistent with the high between-study heterogeneity (see Figure 3). Egger's test did not indicate significant funnel plot asymmetry (z = −0.85, p = .397). A Peters’ test, which is more appropriate for proportion data and less prone to false-positive asymmetry than Egger’s test, yielded a nominally significant result (z = 2.02, p = .044), suggesting that smaller studies may have reported higher prevalence estimates. However, given the substantial between-study heterogeneity and known differences in study populations and recruitment contexts, funnel plot asymmetry in this meta-analysis likely reflects genuine between-study variability rather than publication bias per se. ## Discussion
  This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised evidence on the prevalence of ICD-11 CPTSD among forcibly displaced adults, including refugees, asylum seekers, and IDPs, and examined associated demographic, clinical, and trauma-related correlates. Across the included studies, prevalence ranged from 3.0% to 74.6%, and the very high between-study heterogeneity and broad prediction interval indicate that prevalence is strongly contingent on the population and context studied. Prevalence varied substantially by recruitment context and displacement subgroup, and a theoretically important pattern emerged from the narrative synthesis: post-migration living difficulties were more consistently associated with the DSO that distinguish CPTSD from PTSD than with core PTSD symptoms, suggesting that ongoing post-displacement adversity actively perpetuates complex trauma psychopathology. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and constitutes the primary constraint on the interpretability of the pooled estimate.

  The findings speak directly to the added value of the ICD-11 CPTSD construct in this population. First, CPTSD was identifiable in every setting studied and was the majority presentation in treatment-seeking samples, indicating that DSO is a common, rather than exceptional, feature of trauma-related presentations among displaced people. Second, the narrative synthesis showed that DSO symptoms were more consistently associated with post-migration living difficulties, whereas core PTSD symptoms tracked pre-migration trauma exposure. A PTSD-only framework would have missed this pattern entirely. It suggests that the ‘complex’ component of the disorder is not simply a residue of past trauma but is maintained by current legal, economic, and social conditions, which are potentially modifiable. Third, the DSO cluster carries implications for assessment and formulation, namely routine attention to shame, negative self-concept, and relational disturbance rather than re-experiencing and avoidance alone, and for treatment research: DSO symptoms have shown smaller responses to standard trauma-focused treatments in non-displaced samples (Karatzias et al., 2019a), but whether this holds for displaced populations, who have responded well to trauma-focused therapies for PTSD, remains untested.

  The pooled prevalence of 20.9% is consistent with the ICD-11 theoretical framework positioning prolonged, repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible, the defining exposure profile of forced displacement, as conferring heightened risk for DSO (Maercker et al., 2013). The sole prior systematic review examining CPTSD in refugees and asylum seekers identified 15 studies but reported only a narrative prevalence range of 16% to 38% in treatment-seeking samples and 2.2% to 9.3% in community-based samples for ICD-11 CPTSD, ICD-10 EPCACE, and DSM-IV DESNOS separately (de Silva et al., 2021). Notably, while de Silva et al. (2021) carefully disaggregated these estimates by diagnostic construct, the broad inclusion criteria across distinct diagnostic systems introduced conceptual diversity into the broader synthesis. EPCACE requires enduring personality change lasting at least two years (Heide et al., 2016), and DESNOS, which was never adopted as a standalone diagnosis (Mellor et al., 2021), encompasses additional domains including dissociation and somatisation absent from the ICD-11 framework. The present review builds upon this work by narrowing the focus exclusively to ICD-11-compatible instruments, providing the first pooled prevalence estimate specifically for ICD-11 CPTSD in forcibly displaced populations.

  Similar to the review by de Silva et al. (2021), the most clinically important finding in our analysis was recruitment context. Treatment-seeking samples yielded a pooled prevalence of 55.2%, nearly fivefold higher than the 10.6% observed in community-based samples. This pattern is consistent with Berkson bias: specialist trauma services are self-selected for the most severe and complex presentations, such that CPTSD is the majority diagnosis in clinical settings serving displaced people rather than an edge case. This has direct implications for service design. Recognising the distinct DSO symptom cluster provides substantive clinical utility across case formulation, assessment, and treatment planning. In complex trauma, the symptoms of DSO cannot be adequately addressed through a standard PTSD framework alone. DSO requires an expanded interpretive framework that accounts for systemic regulatory dysfunction, thereby capturing the pervasive self-concept distortions and emotional instability characteristic of ICD-11 CPTSD. Standard first-line PTSD interventions primarily target re-experiencing and avoidance. However, individuals with prominent DSO features often require phased or tailored approaches that address emotion dysregulation, pervasive shame or guilt, and repair disrupted relational functioning alongside trauma processing. Although there is strong empirical support for trauma-focused interventions in treating PTSD among forcibly displaced populations, the majority of existing treatment studies were conducted before the approval of ICD-11 criteria and did not evaluate CPTSD separately (Nosè et al., 2017). Currently, direct treatment research specifically involving forcibly displaced populations diagnosed with ICD-11 CPTSD remains very limited. Phase-based approaches addressing stabilisation, affect regulation, and interpersonal difficulties, consistent with International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) guidelines, are often discussed for complex presentations (Cloitre et al., 2012). However, whether tailored or phase-based strategies offer incremental benefit over standard trauma-focused protocols in this specific group remains an empirical question requiring dedicated trial evidence. Although the available community-based studies cannot support extrapolation to the global displaced population, the consistency with which CPTSD prevalence was identified across multiple settings indicates an important need for accessible assessment and care.

  A theoretically important pattern emerged from the narrative synthesis. Across four studies, post-migration living difficulties were more consistently associated with DSO than PTSD symptom clusters, whereas cumulative trauma exposure, particularly war-related events and interpersonal violence, demonstrated a more robust association with core PTSD symptoms (Hecker et al., 2018; Jowett et al., 2021; Karstoft et al., 2025; Tay et al., 2018). This dissociation suggests that the features distinguishing CPTSD from PTSD may be specifically driven by chronic post-displacement adversity context rather than pre-migration trauma alone. Factors such as legal insecurity, poverty, and systemic marginalisation appear to be potent drivers of this process (Andisha & Lueger-Schuster, 2024; Jannesari et al., 2020). The clinical implication is important: CPTSD among displaced people is not simply a function of past trauma that remits once safety is reached. Instead, ongoing post-migration living difficulties may actively perpetuate DSO symptoms (Schiess-Jokanovic et al., 2021), meaning effective treatment must address current structural stressors alongside trauma processing.

  Prevalence differed significantly by displacement subgroup, with asylum seekers exhibiting the highest pooled rate at 54.8%. This likely reflects the compounding burden of ongoing legal insecurity, restricted employment rights, detention conditions, and chronic inability to plan for the future, post-migration stressors constituting a form of secondary traumatisation distinct from pre-migration trauma (Gleeson et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Tay & Silove, 2017). However, this finding needs to be interpreted with caution: the asylum seeker subgroup comprised only three studies, two of which were treatment-seeking samples, meaning that displacement status and clinical selection effects are partially confounded. For IDPs, a single study precludes any reliable estimate (McGinty et al., 2023). Notably, IDPs may retain access to familiar social networks and cultural support by remaining within their country of origin, which could plausibly attenuate CPTSD risk relative to international refugees, though this remains speculative. Regardless, the near-absence of IDP-focused research represents a significant gap, particularly given that IDPs constitute the largest displaced subgroup globally, numbering 67.8 million as of mid-2025 (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2025).

  Although the difference between ITQ-based and mapped-instrument studies did not reach statistical significance, the approximately 15 percentage point difference in pooled prevalence is clinically meaningful. Instruments such as the PCL-5 (Barbieri et al., 2019), R-MHAP (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2018), AUDADIS-IV (Frost et al., 2019), and PSS-I (Liddell et al., 2019) were designed for different diagnostic frameworks and may systematically under-measure DSO symptoms, the defining clinical distinction between PTSD and CPTSD, when mapped onto ICD-11 criteria. The ITQ, purpose-built for the ICD-11 two-dimensional diagnostic structure, requires independent threshold attainment for both PTSD symptom clusters and DSO clusters (Cloitre et al., 2018), a structural requirement that mapped instruments may fail to replicate faithfully (Hyland et al., 2017). The availability of validated ITQ adaptations in Arabic (Hyland et al., 2021), Dari (Andisha et al., 2023) and Danish (Nielsen et al., 2023) supports its feasibility across the cultural contexts most relevant to this population.

  Meta-regression analyses indicated that none of the three pre-specified continuous moderators, i.e. time since displacement, mean age, or proportion of female participants, predicted CPTSD prevalence. The lack of an association with time since displacement does not imply that prolonged displacement is clinically irrelevant. At the study level, time since displacement conflates vastly different displacement trajectories, ranging from ∼10 months in recently displaced Ukrainian samples (Benoni et al., 2025; Karstoft et al., 2024; Karstoft et al., 2025; McGinty et al., 2023) to over 27 years among West Papuan refugees in protracted displacement (Silove et al., 2017; Tay et al., 2015), without accounting for resettlement stability, access to services, or specific trauma profiles. The null sex effect reflects a fundamental limitation of ecological inference: the proportion of female participants within a study is not equivalent to female sex as an individual-level predictor, and the absence of a study-level association should not be interpreted as evidence that sex does not moderate CPTSD risk. Individual-level studies consistently identify female sex as a risk factor for CPTSD (Frost et al., 2019; Liddell et al., 2019), and this finding is likely obscured at the aggregate level. Similarly, the null effects observed for age at the aggregate level likely mask significant individual-level variation.

  Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and represents the principal limitation on the interpretability of the pooled estimate. The 95% prediction interval, ranging from approximately 2% to 80%, reflects the degree to which true prevalence in any given study is contingent on recruitment context, displacement subgroup, geographic and cultural setting, measurement instrument, trauma type, cumulative trauma load, time since displacement, and host-country structural conditions. Heterogeneity of this magnitude is common in prevalence meta-analyses of complex psychopathologies across diverse populations (Steel et al., 2009; Turrini et al., 2017), and the pooled point estimate is best understood as a broad central tendency rather than a precise epidemiological constant. More fundamentally, the included studies do not constitute a probability sample of the global forcibly displaced population. Geographic coverage was uneven, internally displaced persons were represented by only one independent sample, and treatment-seeking samples were disproportionately represented among some displacement subgroups. Consequently, neither the overall random-effects mean nor the subgroup means can be interpreted as globally representative population prevalence estimates.

  Moreover, it is important to note that forced displacement is globally concentrated: at mid-2025, two-thirds of the world’s refugees originated from five countries, Afghanistan, Syria, Sudan, Ukraine, and Venezuela, and Sudan was the largest internal displacement situation (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2025). The studies included here map only partially onto this distribution. Syrian, Ukrainian, and Afghan samples were well represented, but Sudanese participants appeared in only one treatment-seeking sample in Niger, with Venezuelan participants were not represented at all. The Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan, Myanmar, and Somalia were absent, and the only internally displaced sample was Ukrainian. Conversely, displaced Ukrainians, who represent roughly one in 20 forcibly displaced people worldwide, contributed four of the 20 studies and approximately three-quarters of all participants. This imbalance reflects where ICD-11 CPTSD research has been feasible rather than where displacement is greatest: large registry-linked surveys have been possible for Ukrainians resettled in Europe, whereas populations displaced within or across sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and South and Southeast Asia are rarely studied with ICD-11 instruments and, when they are, in small treatment-seeking samples. As a consequence, no inference can be drawn about CPTSD prevalence in the largest displacement situations of the present decade, which are also the settings in which post-migration living difficulties, the factor most consistently linked to DSO symptoms in this review, are likely to be most severe. Extending ICD-11 CPTSD research to these populations is therefore urgently required for any estimate that could inform global service planning.

  Several additional limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, all included studies relied on self-report instruments, introducing the possibility of over- or under-reporting of symptom severity. Second, the predominantly cross-sectional design of included studies precludes causal inference regarding the temporal relationship between post-migration stressors and CPTSD. Third, restriction to English-language publications may have introduced geographic publication bias, potentially underrepresenting evidence from non-Western contexts. Fourth, translation procedures, cultural adaptation, psychometric evaluation, and population-specific validation of the CPTSD instruments were frequently under-reported or absent. Only 10 of the 20 studies used an instrument validated in the language and population studied; the remainder had no identifiable validation history in the relevant population. Psychometric evidence in the study sample was reported by 15 studies but was predominantly limited to internal consistency. Only one study reported reliability at the level of individual symptom clusters, and confirmatory factor analysis was reported for only five independent samples. Notably, the three studies that used an instrument developed and culturally adapted for the population studied (West Papuan refugees) did not support the ICD-11 hierarchical PTSD/DSO structure, so that even where validated translations exist, the conceptual equivalence of DSO constructs across cultural contexts cannot be assumed and warrants further psychometric investigation. These gaps introduce potential measurement bias that should be borne in mind when interpreting the pooled and subgroup estimates, although prevalence did not differ significantly between studies with and without validated instruments (18.6% vs. 23.1%).

  Our findings carry direct clinical and policy implications. Given that approximately one in ten forcibly displaced adults in community settings meets CPTSD criteria, rising to more than one in two in treatment-seeking settings, routine screening for CPTSD is warranted across health services for displaced people, with the ITQ as the recommended instrument. The high prevalence of CPTSD in forcibly displaced populations necessitates practical, scalable responses that extend beyond specialist clinical care: reducing bureaucratic delays in legal status determination, ensuring access to stable housing and employment, and strengthening community-based social support, the structural conditions that the evidence suggests directly drive DSO symptom burden. Task-sharing models, in which trained lay workers or community health workers deliver structured psychosocial support, offer a feasible pathway to addressing CPTSD at scale in resource-limited humanitarian settings (Bryant et al., 2023). The disproportionate burden among asylum seekers underscores the critical need to deliver mental health support before, not only after, refugee status determination. At the policy level, extrapolating the community prevalence to the current scale of global displacement implies tens of millions of people with unmet CPTSD treatment needs, requiring strategic investment in these models, stepped-care approaches, and culturally adapted interventions.

  Several priorities for future research emerge from this review. Individual participant data meta-analyses are required to examine individual-level predictors, particularly sex, age, trauma type, post-migration living difficulties, and their interaction, which cannot be adequately addressed at the aggregate study level. Longitudinal studies are essential to map the natural course of CPTSD in displaced populations, including rates of remission, the impact of resettlement conditions, and predictors of chronicity. Despite constituting the largest displaced subgroup globally, IDPs remain severely under-studied and represent an urgent research priority. Current evidence is also heavily concentrated on Middle Eastern, African, and Ukrainian or European contexts, with studies in under-represented regions such as Latin America, East and Southeast Asia, and the Pacific remaining scarce. Future prevalence studies should standardise the reporting of post-migration living difficulties, trauma load, and time since displacement to enhance the comparability and statistical power of subsequent meta-analyses. Finally, RCTs evaluating CPTSD-specific treatments within displaced populations are urgently needed to translate this epidemiological evidence into clinical practice (Hassija & Cloitre, 2013; Schäfer et al., 2022).

  This systematic review and meta-analysis provides the first quantitative synthesis of ICD-11 CPTSD prevalence rates among forcibly displaced adults. Across the included studies, roughly one in five participants met diagnostic criteria, but the evidence demonstrates pronounced variation across populations and contexts, and no single estimate can be generalised to the global forcibly displaced population. Recruitment setting accounted for an important component of this variation, with substantially higher prevalence in treatment-seeking samples and among asylum seekers, underscoring the compounding effect of ongoing legal and structural adversity on complex trauma psychopathology. A consistent pattern across studies links post-migration living difficulties specifically to DSO symptoms, suggesting that CPTSD in this population is actively maintained by post-displacement structural conditions rather than past trauma alone. These findings affirm the added value of the ICD-11 CPTSD construct in this population: DSO symptoms are common, are linked to modifiable post-migration conditions, and reinforce the need for ICD-11-informed screening, treatment research that evaluates CPTSD-specific outcomes, and socially informed care. Given that global displacement now exceeds 117 million individuals (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2025), addressing the CPTSD burden in this population is a public health imperative.
