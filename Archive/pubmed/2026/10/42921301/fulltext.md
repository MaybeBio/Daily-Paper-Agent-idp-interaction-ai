# N6-Methyladenosine
RNA Modifications Influence the
Stability of FUS-Dependent Biological Condensate Formation


## Abstract
  Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a process in which proteins and nucleic acids organize into dynamic, membrane-less structures within cells. These structures help regulate cellular processes such as gene expression, molecular storage, and stress responses through weak interactions between disordered protein regions and RNA. Emerging evidence highlights a regulatory role for epitranscriptomic modifications, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A), though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we investigated the relationship between m6A RNA nucleotide modification and LLPS by identifying proteins that selectively bind methylated RNA. An RNA pull-down assay enriched 106 proteins, including 53 known RNA-binding proteins and several proteins that support LLPS, including FUS (Fused in sarcoma). FUS, a well-characterized phase-separating RNA-binding protein, showed a 2-fold higher binding affinity for m6A-modified hairpin RNA than for its unmodified counterpart. Cloud-point analysis and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) microscopy revealed enhanced thermodynamic stability at higher m6A levels in total RNA extracts. Quantitative CARS imaging revealed that, although the overall RNA-to-protein ratio within condensates remains largely unchanged, larger droplets under high-m6A conditions exhibit increased RNA content, suggesting that m6A strengthens RNA–protein interactions without significantly altering the stoichiometry. Collectively, these findings indicate that while protein–protein interactions may initiate LLPS, m6A-modulated RNA–protein interactions govern condensate growth and stability. Furthermore, these results provide a mechanistic insight into how epitranscriptomic modifications regulate biomolecular condensation and influence cellular organization, gene regulation, and disease processes.


## Materials
and Methods

### RNA Pull-Down Assays
  The MDA-MB 231 cells were cultured in T75 flasks (Greiner Bio-One, Inc.), and lysates were obtained when the cells were fully confluent. Cell lysis was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Pierce IP Lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and SUPERase•In RNase Inhibitor (Invitrogen, Inc.). Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (New England Biolabs, Inc.) (40 μL) were equilibrated with IP Lysis buffer. The cell lysate was mixed with the beads and incubated at 4 °C for 20 min to preclear it of proteins that bind nonspecifically to the beads. The magnetic beads were pelleted, and the supernatant was saved. The m6A-modified target RNA (1 μM, 120 μL) was mixed with the supernatant and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The resultant mixture was added to a new batch of magnetic beads (60 μL) equilibrated with IP Lysis buffer, mixed, and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. The magnetic beads were pelleted, and the supernatant was saved. The respective unmodified RNA (2 μM, 120 μL) and m6A-modified control RNA (2 μM, 120 μL) were added to the supernatant and incubated at 4 °C for 20 min. The beads were pelleted, and the supernatant was discarded. The beads were washed (4 times) with TENT buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 250 mM NaCl; and 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100), followed by 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 (3 times). The bound proteins were eluted using 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, by heating at 95 °C for 10 min. The proteins in resultant samples were detected using LC-MS analysis (Proteomics and Metabolomics Core, Lerner Research Institute, Cleveland, OH, USA). RNA pulldown assays were performed in triplicate. The average and standard deviation of LFQ values from triplicates were calculated for each protein enriched in the pulldown assay. The percent coefficient of variation (%CV = S.D.×100%/Avg. LFQ) was plotted against Log2[Avg. LFQ]. Proteins with %CV ≤ 35 and Log2[Avg. LFQ] ≥ 15 were selected for further analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis to identify RNA-binding proteins was performed using open web resources (http://geneontology.org/).


### FUS Protein Expression and Purification
  The FUS_pET-24b­(+) (GenScript Biotech) overexpression plasmid was transformed into BL21­(DE3) cells (NEB) using the manufacturer’s suggested protocol. Bacterial cells containing the overexpression vector were grown in 5 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 50 mg/mL kanamycin at 37 °C overnight. This overnight culture was inoculated into 1 L of LB supplemented with kanamycin and grown at 37 °C. Protein production was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG after the OD600 reached 0.6, and the culture was incubated for an additional 3 h at 37 °C with constant shaking at 250 rpm. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and stored in a −80 °C freezer until further use. Cell pellets were resuspended in buffer A (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 6 M urea, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail, then sonicated. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min to remove particulates, and RNase A (0.1 mg/mL) was added immediately after centrifugation. The supernatant was then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min with gentle rotation. Proteins from the supernatant were separated by affinity chromatography (HisTrap FF column, Cytiva) under an imidazole gradient in Buffer A [Figure S9]. Fractions containing FUS protein in high purity were combined and dialyzed against Buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 30% glycerol) with a decreasing urea gradient (4–0 M). Three buffer changes were performed for the final dialysis step to the final storage buffer. Protein concentrations were determined by dividing the A280 by the molar extinction coefficient of the FUS protein. Purified FUS was then aliquoted, snap-frozen, and stored at −80 °C until use.


### RNA Preparation
  Chemically synthesized and HPLC-purified model RNAs used in this research (m6A-modified: 5′-GCAGG­GUCCGCm6ACUGA­UCCGC-3′; unmodified: 5′-GCAGG­GUCCG­CACUG­AUCCGC-3′) were purchased from Horizon Discovery Inc. in the 2′-ACE-protected form. RNAs used in EMSA were purchased with a Cyanine dye conjugated at the 5′-end. After deprotection, RNAs were dried and resuspended in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH = 7.5, and 1 mM Na2EDTA). RNA was then aliquoted and stored at −20 °C until further use. Before use in pulldown assays and EMSA, RNAs were folded in their respective reaction buffers by denaturation at 90 °C and slow cooling to ambient temperature.


### Electrophoretic Mobility
Shift Assays (EMSA)
  Cyanine-labeled RNAs (1 μM) were incubated with purified FUS protein (0–9 μM) in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 30% glycerol). After incubation at 37 °C for 20 min. RNA-protein samples were immediately placed on ice for 5 min. These samples were then loaded onto a nondenaturing 12% polyacrylamide-TBE gel and electrophoresed at −5 °C for 3.5 h. Gels were then imaged using a Typhoon FLA 9500 fluorescence scanner. Gel bands corresponding to RNA–protein complexes and free RNA were quantified using ImageJ. The fraction of protein-bound RNAs was calculated and plotted against FUS concentration. Binding curves were then fitted to the Hill equation using Origin software to calculate equilibrium dissociation constants for RNA-FUS complexes.


### Mammalian Cell Culture
  HEK 293 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 4.5 g/L of glucose (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin solution (Corning). They were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Cells were passaged upon reaching 70–80% confluency by trypsinization and split at a 1:5 dilution.


### siRNA Transfection and Total RNA Extraction
  The HEK cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density that would reach 50–80% confluency on the day of transfection. The siRNA against FTO (Dharmacon) was transfected into HEK293 cells using the transfection reagent, TransIT-X2 (Mirus). Total RNA was extracted from HEK293 cells 48 h post-transfection using the E.Z.N.A. HP Total RNA Isolation Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer before downstream analyses.


### m6A Quantification
  Overall N6-methyladenosine (m6A) levels in total RNA extracted from HEK293 cells were quantified using a commercially available semiquantitative m6A RNA methylation assay kit (Epigentek - fluorescence-based), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. m6A levels were calculated based on the provided standards and normalized to the total RNA input.


### 5-Ethynyl Uridine (5-EU) Labeling of RNA
  HEK cells were seeded in 6-well plates to achieve 60–80% confluency at the time of transfection. siRNA complexes were prepared in Opti-MEM according to the manufacturer’s protocol and added to the cells. Following a 48-h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 2.5 mM 5-ethynyl uridine (EU), and the cells were incubated for 1 h. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS, and 5-EU-labeled RNA was isolated for downstream analyses.


### Thermal Experiments
  Extracted FUS protein and total RNA from siFTO-transfected and untransfected HEK293 cells were mixed separately to achieve a final protein concentration of 36.4 μM and an RNA concentration of 4 ng/μL. The two samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and their turbidity was measured at 600 nm across a temperature range of 0 to 52 °C using a Cary 300 Bio UV–Vis spectrophotometer.


### Dynamic Light Scattering
Measurement of FUS-RNA Complexes
  The protein, dispersed in buffer B with 0 M urea, was studied using the DLS Particle Analysis System, Brookhaven ZetaPLUS, equipped with a HeNe laser of 35 mW power, which uses a 660 nm wavelength and the detection angle of the highly sensitive avalanche photo diode (APD) at 90° to measure particle size. Dispersions of 1 mL were placed in a quartz cuvette with an optical path length of 10 mm, and measurements were carried out at 25 °C with a data acquisition time of 300 s. Particle distributions were measured assuming all particles are solid, spherical protein particles and correcting for the viscosity and refractive index of the glycerol in buffer B with 0 M urea.


### In-vitro FUS Droplet Analysis
  FUS-RNA samples were prepared as described previously. Then each mixture was transferred onto a separate cavity glass slide and gently covered with a coverslip. The samples were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min to allow droplet formation. Droplet formation and morphology were then observed using a Leica Stellaris CARS microscopy system.

  An integrated laser system (picoEmerald S, Applied Physics & Electronics, Inc.) was used to generate three temporally and spatially overlapping ultrafast laser pulse trains at an 80 MHz repetition rate. The Stokes beam was fixed at 1031 nm with a pulse width of 2 ps and a spectral bandwidth of 10 cm–1, while a built-in optical parametric oscillator with identical pulse characteristics produced the tunable pump beam (700–980 nm).

  In vitro imaging was performed using a 40X water-immersion objective (HC PL IRAPO 40X, N.A. 1.10, water-immersion lens) with a refractive index of 1.33. A small volume of water was applied to the objective lens before positioning the sample, and Type F immersion liquid was added to the coverslip before lowering the condenser to close the sample chamber. The pump beam was tuned to 792 and 845 nm in order to excite the protein-specific (C–H, 2930 cm–1) and RNA-specific (CC, 2122 cm–1) vibrations. Environmental conditions were maintained at 24.8 °C and 48.3% relative humidity. Imaging was performed over a 512 × 512 frame with unidirectional scanning at 400 Hz and a pixel dwell time of 3.1625 μs. Image analyses were performed using home-built MATLAB codes.
