# SimHS-AFMfit-MD:
An Integrative Approach for Inferring
Alpha-Actinin Atomic Conformational Dynamics


## Abstract
  Many molecular systems, including intrinsically disordered proteins and flexible multidomain complexes, exhibit significant conformational heterogeneity and flexibility, making them difficult to study with conventional methods like X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM. To address this challenge, we introduce SimHS-AFMfit-MD, an integrative framework combining high-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM), molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and AFMfit-based structural modeling to infer dynamic protein conformations at atomic resolution. Using alpha-actinin, an actin cross-linking protein, as a model system, we demonstrate that nonlinear normal-mode analysis (AFMfit-NMA) enhances the accuracy of structural fitting. Additionally, guiding AFMfit with MD trajectories (AFMfit-MD) significantly improves fitting performance, aligning closely with unbiased all-atom MD simulations. This method converts thousands of 3D HS-AFM images into atomic-scale conformational ensembles, revealing key transitions between Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states of alpha-actinin. Our results showcase a hybrid computational–experimental approach that bridges simulation and imaging approaches, enabling real-time visualization of protein dynamics at the atomic scale.


## Overview of the Methodology for Integrating HS-AFM with AFMfit
and MD Simulations
  This study aimed to develop an integrated workflow for investigating the atomic conformation and dynamics of proteins. While understanding atomic structure is important, equally crucial is exploring protein dynamics, such as folding and interaction processes, as these directly influence their biological functions. HS-AFM can provide insights into the dynamics and surface structural changes in proteins during their functional processes in solution. ,,  While having high temporal resolution, HS-AFM lacks the atomic spatial resolution of other experimental techniques, such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Therefore, one of the principal challenges with AFM lies in interpreting its low-resolution, 3D surface images into detailed 3D atomic conformations.

  The key aspect of our approach lies in the combination of several techniques: HS-AFM, AFMfit (a method for fitting AFM images), nonlinear NMA, and MD simulations to establish the SimHS-AFMfit-MD pipeline (Figure  ). This workflow consists of four key steps: (i) acquisition of experimental AFM images and protein models (PDB files), (ii, iii) AFM fitting (i.e., AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD) and MD simulations to generate libraries of atomic conformations (PDB), and (iv) PCA analysis to classify the dynamic atomic conformations based on their principal components (PCs). By integrating these techniques, we can obtain a more comprehensive view of the 3D atomic structure of proteins, along with their dynamics and conformational changes during functional activities.

  We used our methodology to analyze the conformational changes in the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin over milliseconds to seconds and to determine the atomic conformational dynamics of this protein. Alpha-actinin is an antiparallel homodimer with a molecular weight exceeding 200 kDa. The structure of chicken gizzard smooth muscle alpha-actinin (PDB: 1SJJ) consists of the following domains: an N-terminal actin binding domain (ABD), in which the two calponin homology 1 and 2 (CH1, CH2) domains are in extensive contact; a central region containing four spectrin-like repeat (SR1-SR4) or rod domains; and a C-terminal calmodulin-like domain (CaMD) that includes two pairs of EF-hand motifs (EF12 and EF34), and the EF12 domain is responsible for Ca2+ binding. The two ABDs undergo a 90-degree rotation, enabling alpha-actinin to cross-link the actin filaments in nearly any orientation (Figure S1).

  We applied SimHS-AFMfit-MD to generate libraries of atomic models in different conformations for both Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin using a workflow shown in Figure  . This provided insights into their atomistic structures and collective motions over time. First, we gently immobilized alpha-actinin molecules on mica and HS-AFM collected data with a typical imaging rate of 100 ms per frame (Movie S1). We then processed the HS-AFM with AFMfit-NMA or AFMfit-MD, starting with a reference atomic model derived from the MD simulations (Movie S2 and Movie S3). Specifically, AFMfit-NMA fits this reference model to the experimental AFM data by generating pseudo-AFM images derived from this reference model over two fitting steps. The first step was rigid fitting, where the reference model was rigidly aligned with the AFM experimental data to obtain an optimal pose using an exhaustive search algorithm based on the FFT. In the second flexible fitting step, the aligned models were flexibly deformed using the NOLB technique to obtain the optimal elastic alignment of the atomic models to multiple AFM observations. By applying this iterative process to our alpha-actinin AFM data, we collected the corresponding conformational libraries for the protein (see the SI Methods). Simultaneously, the same initial protein model (PDB: 1SJJ) was used as the starting point for the MD simulations (see the SI Methods). The first set of simulations was conducted over a time scale of 1 μs, generating conformation libraries for alpha-actinin. These conformations were used to guide AFMfit in the AFMfit-MD pipeline. A second, independent set of simulations was performed for 2 μs and used for validation purposes. To illustrate the added value of long-time MD, we have also computed NMA conformational ensembles, starting from the average MD conformation. We then analyzed the atomic conformations obtained from AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, NMA, and MD simulations using principal component analysis (PCA). This analysis allowed us to infer the conformational ensembles as representing the dynamic behavior of the atomistic structure of the proteins.


## Ca2+ Binding Affects Smooth Muscle Alpha-Actinin
Flexibility
  First, we employed HS-AFM to obtain high spatiotemporal resolution images of alpha-actinin. Three-dimensional molecular surface images of the individual Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin molecules, weakly absorbed on mica via nonspecific electrostatic interaction, were continuously recorded under near-physiological conditions at an imaging rate of 100 ms per frame (Figure  , Table S1, Figure S1, Movie S1).

  From an electrostatic perspective, alpha-actinin carries a substantial net negative charge (−54e) at physiological pH, which weakens nonspecific electrostatic adsorption to the negatively charged mica surface, particularly under the ionic conditions used (Figure  B, C). The imaging buffer (20 mM KCl, 10 mM PIPES-KOH, pH 6.8, 0.25 mM EGTA, with or without 0.1–0.2 mM CaCl2 or 0.5 mM MgCl2) partially screens surface electrostatic interactions, allowing sufficient adsorption for stable imaging while preserving conformational freedom. As a result, the observed motions fall within a physically reasonable regime, where internal flexibility is maintained despite weak surface association.

  Indeed, we successfully captured the genuine homodimeric features of alpha-actinin molecules on mica (Figure  A–C), and their appearances are consistent with the homodimer structures of both Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin cross-linking actin filaments (Figure S2). These results indicate that the observed conformations reflect intrinsic structural features relevant to their biological function, such as understanding how alpha-actinin stabilizes the canonic actin helix to inhibit cofilin-induced twisting and cooperative binding.

  The conformational dynamics of thousands of alpha-actinin molecules, including bending and twisting motions, were successfully captured and visualized in our AFM images (red and yellow arrowheads, Figure  C). To distinguish the conformational dynamics between these two states, we semiautomatically tracked the positions (x, y, and z coordinates) of the two ABD spheres on opposite sides of multiple alpha-actinin molecules over sequential frames and calculated the distance between their peak heights. HS-AFM analysis demonstrated that while the ensemble lengths of alpha-actinin were similar in the presence and absence of Ca2+, there was a significant difference in the ensemble heights between the two states. From the HS-AFM data set, the mean ± SD heights and lengths were 5.7 ± 0.9 nm (N = 3040) and 25.1 ± 2.3 nm (N = 1941) for Ca2+-bound alpha-actinin and 4.8 ± 0.7 nm (N = 6080) and 25.0 ± 3.2 nm (N = 3885) for Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin. The results revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) in the height distributions, but no significant difference in length between the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin (Figure  D, Table S1).


## Investigating Alpha-Actinin Dynamics at the Atomic Level via
SimHS-AFMfit-MD
  Our HS-AFM–based analyses helped elucidate some of the characteristics of alpha-actinin, including its fluctuations in height and length over time. However, these conventional AFM processing techniques were unable to reveal critical insights into the atomic conformational dynamics of alpha-actinin, such as its bending and twisting motions, as they only provided its surface structure (Figure  C). But an understanding of these motions is essential to grasp the mechanical influence of the actin-cross-linking function of alpha-actinin on the structure, dynamics, and function of actin filaments. Thus, we developed the SimHS-AFMfit-MD pipeline by integrating HS-AFM with AFMfit-NMA or AFMfit-MD, and MD simulations. This pipeline includes four steps (Figure  ) and allows us to further investigate the dynamics and atomic structure of alpha-actinin. We utilized AFMfit-NMA and AFMfit-MD to transform the 3D molecular surfaces of alpha-actinin, obtained from HS-AFM, into 3D atomic conformations (Movie S2 and Movie S3). Through this method, we created libraries of atomic conformations for the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin states (Figure  A–C). These conformations were then aligned to compare the dynamics of the two states based on the AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, and the MD simulations. The distribution of Cα positions was broader in the AFMfit-NMA analysis compared to the MD simulations (Figure  A vs C), yet the distributions were quite similar between AFMfit-MD and the MD simulations (Figure  B vs C). Notably, integrating MD trajectory information into AFMfit (AFMfit-MD) resulted in a conformational library that was more closely aligned with the one generated by MD simulations (Figure  B vs C). NMA-based structural fits captured conformational changes between Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states that were comparable to those observed in the 2-microseconds unbiased MD simulations and AFMfit-MD.

  The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), which is a commonly used metric for evaluating the flexibility and mobility of atoms or residues in proteins, was employed to quantify the deviation of each Cα atom from its average position using the conformational libraries generated with the AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, and MD simulations (Figure  D). The results revealed the dynamic motions of CH1-CH2 within the ABD, neck, and EF12-EF34 domains. However, the rod (SR1–SR4) domains exhibited relatively high rigidity.

  We also analyzed the inter-domain distances (Figure S3, Table S2) and the bending and twisting angles of alpha-actinin’s ABD (Figure S4, Table S3) to characterize the dynamic conformations of alpha-actinin in both its Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states. These analyses are now achievable using our SimHS-AFMfit-MD framework.

  Collectively, the SimHS-AFM-MD analysis showed that Ca2+ binding slightly reduced the motion of the CH1-CH2 and EF12-EF34 domains near the neck in muscle alpha-actinin.


## Classifying the Dynamic Atomic Conformations of Alpha-Actinin
via PCA
  PCA is a statistical method used to reduce the dimensionality of a data set while retaining its most significant variations. It is highly effective in identifying and classifying the collective motions of individual protein domains using principal components (PCs).

  First, we determined the ensemble of the atomic motions of the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin by analyzing the libraries of the conformations obtained from the AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, NMA, and MD simulations and projected these motions onto a common PCA space (Figure  A, Figure S5, Movie S4, Movie S5, and Movie S6). The eigenvalues of principal components 1 (PC1), PC2, and PC3 were used to determine the percentage contribution of each motion. PC1 (28.7%) primarily captured the ensemble of the bending motions in the x-axis, PC2 (19.1%) represented the bending motions in the y-axis, and PC3 (9.8%) described the twisting motions.

  To compare the data obtained from the AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, NMA, and MD simulations with the static conformations (PDB: 1SJJ and 4D1E) obtained by Cryo-EM and X-ray diffraction, we projected the conformational libraries of the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin generated from the AFMfit-NMA, AFMfit-MD, NMA, and MD simulations onto a common PCA space and plotted the distributions of PC1 versus PC2 (Figure  ) and PC1 versus PC3 (Figure S5). For both the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states, PC1 and PC2 derived from the AFMfit-NMA conformational libraries exhibited a comparable distribution of ensemble bending motions along the x-y axes compared with those from the AFMfit-MD and MD simulation libraries. The distribution of the NMA ensemble alone, as expected, follows the harmonic law and is much more confined compared to the MD ensembles and AFMfit conformations. It still somewhat resembles the distribution of the Ca2+-unbound MD simulation, but shifted compared to the Ca2+-bound one. As a result, the difference between AFMfit-NMA and AFMfit-MD conformations is more pronounced for the Ca2+-bound state (see PC1).

  We observed a different trend for PC3, which captured the ensemble of twisting motions in the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states between the methods. However, PC3 accounts for only a small fraction of the overall conformational variability (9.8% of the total variance; Figure S5) compared with PC1 and PC2, which primarily described ensemble bending motions. The conformations derived from cryo-EM and X-ray diffraction are static and occupy distinct regions of the conformational space, highlighting methodological differences between HS-AFM, X-ray, and cryo-EM techniques rather than direct inconsistencies. We note that static conformations of Ca2+-unbound muscle alpha-actinin (PDB: 1SJJ and 4D1E), ,  resolved by Cryo-EM and X-ray diffraction, also differ. Although muscle alpha-actinin has been reported to be insensitive to Ca2+ binding, −      our HS-AFM and SimHS-AFMfit-MD analyses reveal previously inaccessible dynamic atomic conformations for both Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states. Ca2+ binding slightly reduced the motion of the CH1-CH2 and EF12-EF34 domains near the neck in muscle alpha-actinin.

  Collectively, AFMfit-NMA captures the dominant bending motions of alpha-actinin (PC1 and PC2) in close agreement with AFMfit-MD and MD simulations for both Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound states. Differences observed in PC3 reflect minor twisting motions that contribute only a small fraction of the total conformational variance.

  The resulting fluctuation profiles and dominant dynamic modes derived from the independent MD simulations are consistent with, yet statistically independent from, the conformational dynamics inferred from SimHS-AFMfit-MD. This agreement therefore provides an orthogonal validation of our approach, demonstrating that the AFM-derived fitting captures physically meaningful motions rather than merely reproducing the guiding MD information.

  Across all results, the eigenvector components or motions represented by the blue arrows in SR1 and SR4 were small in magnitude. However, those in SR2 and SR3 showed almost no motion (Figure  A). These results confirm the rigidity of the rod domain, consistent with previous studies. −      In contrast, prominent eigenvector components were observed in CH1–CH2 within the ABD, in EF12–EF34, and in the neck domain, indicating relatively high flexibility in these regions. In summary, our SimHS-AFMfit-MD approach demonstrates an ability to capture and categorize the atomic conformational dynamics of the Ca2+-bound and Ca2+-unbound alpha-actinin.

  Time-resolved cryo-EM is emerging as a powerful tool for investigating protein dynamics and atomic conformations simultaneously. −   Its potential can be further enhanced by integrating approaches such as cryo-EM flexible fitting or computational methods. Additionally, advanced NMR spectroscopy provides complementary insights and captures both structural and dynamic aspects of proteins during interactions. ,,,

  NMR spectroscopy is excellent for studying protein structure, dynamics, and interactions but has limitations. Traditional solution-state NMR is most effective for proteins under ∼30–50 kDa, with techniques like TROSY extending this to ∼100 kDa. Larger proteins face signal overlap and line broadening due to slow molecular tumbling and transverse relaxation. Compared to cryo-EM and X-ray crystallography, NMR has lower sensitivity, and peak overlap complicates resonance assignments in complex systems. ,−   In dynamic studies, NMR captures time scales from picoseconds to seconds but struggles with motions faster than 10 ps or slower than minutes. ,  Solid-state NMR is challenging for membrane proteins due to the need for isotopic labeling and specialized pulse sequences. Weak or transient interactions may produce faint signals, and long sample preparation and acquisition times increase the risk of protein degradation or aggregation.

  While cryo-EM surpasses NMR in resolving large protein complexes (>100 kDa), it is less effective for studying dynamics. However, these techniques can complement each other. Other single-molecule techniques, such as FRET, can complement NMR for fast dynamics, but no standalone method can perfectly capture the atomic structures, dynamics, and interactions simultaneously.

  By integrating HS-AFM with the AFMfit-NMA and AFMfit-MD and MD simulations through SimHS-AFMfit-MD, we generated comprehensive libraries of atomic conformations, validated against MD trajectories. This approach enabled the investigation of the physical motions and atomic structures of alpha-actinin through PCA, while also offering a powerful method for enhancing the interpretation of low-resolution AFM data, as demonstrated in a previous example. For instance, the twisting and bending motions of alpha-actinin were cross-validated using AFMfit guided by MD trajectories and unbiased all-atom MD simulations. This capability potentially contributes to better understanding of previous static structural studies using X-ray diffraction ,  and cryo-EM. ,

  The data from SimHS-AFMfit-MD can aid in developing force fields for simulating complex protein models, as shown in previous studies. −    Force fields are essential for accurately modeling interatomic forces and interactions, with multiple applications to simulating dynamics and energy minimization. ,  More broadly, the conformational libraries could also serve as training data for the deep learning models, −   helping predict protein dynamics across time scales and bridging the gap between experimental and computational methods. Recent advances in 3D-LAFM have also enabled the transformation of AFM data into 3D density maps, supporting large-scale atomic AFM data generation when integrated with MD simulations.
