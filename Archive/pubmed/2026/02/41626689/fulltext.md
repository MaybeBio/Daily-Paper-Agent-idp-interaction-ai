# Fibrillarin/Nop1 perturbs RNA folding and assembly independently of liquid–liquid phase separation


## Abstract
  The liquid–liquid phase-separated environment of the eukaryotic nucleolus is proposed to benefit ribosome assembly by chaperoning rRNA folding and spatially sorting ribosome assembly factors. Yet, how microscopic interactions within the condensed nucleolus affect rRNA folding and assembly is largely unknown. We used single-molecule fluorescence microscopy to monitor folding of the Tetrahymena ribozyme and an rRNA domain inside and outside droplets of Nop1/fibrillarin, a major constituent of the nucleolus. We found that Nop1 destabilizes tertiary docking of the ribozyme substrate helix equally in dilute and condensed phases, depending only on transient molecular interactions between Nop1 and the ribozyme. Nop1 binding also inhibits rRNA unwinding by a DEAD-box helicase and nonspecific binding of a ribosomal protein. Over time, nonspecific interactions with Nop1 are outcompeted by specific RNA-protein assembly. Our results illustrate how RNA-binding proteins residing in the nucleolus tune RNA folding stability while permitting assembly of native ribosomal complexes.


## Introduction
  Many RNA-binding proteins contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDR) that modulate their interactions with regulatory targets and other proteins [1]. Additionally, IDRs participate in a liquid mesh of nonspecific interactions driving condensation and phase separation of RNA granules [2, 3]. These dual functions have made the importance of specific and nonspecific interactions within RNA condensates difficult to disentangle [4]. A key example of this duality is the eukaryotic nucleolus that self-organizes around the sites of rRNA transcription [2, 5–7]. The condensed nucleolar environment is thought to aid ribosome synthesis by concentrating the ribosome assembly machinery [8, 9] and spatially sorting assembly factors specific for each subunit [6, 10, 11]. Some nucleolar proteins contain positively charged IDRs rich in glycine and arginine residues (GAR domains) [12, 13] that not only contribute to condensation [14–17] but also form nonspecific interactions with the pre-rRNA that may facilitate its folding and assembly with ribosomal proteins [18, 19]. Other nucleolar proteins contain mixed charged IDRs with a KKE/D motif that are proposed to assist the recruitment of assembly factors around the pre-rRNA [20, 21].

  The strength and impact of such nonspecific interactions on RNA folding and ribonucleoprotein (RNP) assembly are largely unknown. First, cellular imaging does not reveal the molecular interactions inside RNA condensates. Second, most proteins that contribute to phase separation also play specific roles in RNA processing or assembly [20, 22–26], making these specific and general functions difficult to separate. Here, we sidestep these limitations by using synthetic droplets and established model systems for RNA folding and ribosome assembly to study how an abundant nucleolar protein, the methyltransferase fibrillarin (human FBL/yeast Nop1), affects microscopic RNA and protein interactions.

  A core protein of box C/D snoRNPs, FBL/Nop1 is essential for ribosome assembly in eukaryotes [27, 28]. FBL/Nop1 localizes to the dense fibrillar component (DFC) in metazoans [29] and fibrillar strands in yeast [30], where it can interact with the newly transcribed pre-rRNA as well as other nucleolar components. In addition to its methyltransferase domain, FBL/Nop1 has a disordered GAR domain [13] that facilitates self-association and phase separation with a wide range of RNAs of different sequence and with heparin in vitro [6]. In human cells, FBL was reported to interact most frequently with the 5′ external transcribed spacer [10], suggesting that interactions between FBL and the nascent pre-rRNA are important for ribosome assembly.

  Previous in vitro studies indicated that the environment in protein–RNA droplets generally destabilizes nucleic acid structure. For example, dsDNA was shown to unwind in a droplet formed by the disordered N-terminus of a DEAD-box protein, because the nucleobases interact with arginine residues in the IDR [31]. RNA secondary structure was also disrupted by basic proteins in droplets in NMR experiments [32] and all-atom molecular simulations [33]. The hammerhead ribozyme was found to have lower activity in a bulk coacervate phase and in microdroplets than in buffer [34], correlating with reduced RNA–magnesium ion interactions in droplets [35]. Strong destabilization of RNA structure by disordered nucleolar proteins could delay pre-rRNA folding and binding of ribosomal proteins. However, moderate destabilization of rRNA structure could accelerate ribosome assembly by helping to resolve misfolded RNA conformations.

  To understand how nucleolar proteins affect RNP assembly, we used single-molecule total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy to monitor RNA folding inside or outside Nop1-RNA droplets in real time. Our method enabled a side-by-side comparison of RNA structure in the dilute and condensed phases, revealing that Nop1 destabilizes RNA tertiary structure regardless of phase separation. Using a model rRNA–protein complex, we further show how Nop1 impacts the delicate competition between ribosomal proteins and DEAD-box helicases for access to the rRNA.

Desired protein fractions were dialyzed against 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10% (v/v) glycerol and stored at 1.5 mg/ml in aliquots at −80°C. Nop1-FL and Nop1-BCO-MD were labeled with Cy3-NHS ester (SI Methods), repurified to remove free dye, and stored at 0.5 mg/ml in 50 mM Tris–HCl, 1 M NaCl, 2 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10% (v/v) glycerol in aliquots at −80°C. See SI Methods for full details.

  CsdA was purified as previously described [38], except final purification was with a HiTrap Heparin HP column (Cytiva) in 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4) and 200 mM NaCl. The protein was eluted with 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4) and 1 M NaCl. Ribosomal proteins uS4 and uS7 were purified as previously described [39].


### RNA preparation and labeling
  The Tetrahymena L-21 Sca ribozyme was extended by 5 nucleotides at the 5′ end [40] and 26 nucleotides at the 3′ end [41] to enable stable binding with the substrate and the DNA tether, respectively. The extensions were incorporated into pT7L-21 [42] by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using HIFI assembly (NEB) to derive pT7L-21-extended. A ScaI site was added to the 3′ end of the extended ribozyme sequence. pT7L-21-extended DNA was digested with ScaI prior to run-off in vitro transcription. The resulting L-21-extended ribozyme was purified using denaturing 4% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Escherichia coli 16S 5WJ rRNA [43] was prepared by in vitro transcription of PCR-amplified 5WJ DNA template (see Supplementary Table S2) as described previously [38].


### Single-molecule fluorescence microscopy
  Single-molecule fluorescence microscopy experiments were performed using a home-built TIRF microscope with an EMCCD camera [44], equipped with 532 and 640 nm lasers. Flow channels were prepared on quartz microscope slides passivated with dichlorodimethylsilane (DDS) as in a previous study [45], except that Tween 20 was omitted to attach droplets onto DDS-coated surfaces.


#### Ribozyme docking and colocalization
  L-21 extended ribozyme was renatured with L-21-tether-ATTO647N in 50 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 7.5), 20 mM (NH4)2SO4, and 10 mM MgCl2, mixed with L-21-substrate-ATTO532, and immobilized on a passivated quartz slide for TIRF microscopy in imaging buffer (50 mM HEPES–KOH, pH 7.5, 20 mM (NH4)2SO4, 4 or 10 mM MgCl2 as noted, 1 mM Trolox, 1% (w/v) glucose, and 165 U/ml glucose oxidase). Phase separation was initiated by adding 1 µl of 20 mg/ml E. coli MRE 600 tRNA (0.2 mg/ml final) to 0.75 mg/ml desalted Nop1, and the solution was immediately flowed into the TIRF chamber and incubated for 5 min. All experiments were finished within 30 min after initiating phase separation to prevent droplet gelation [6]. Nop1 co-localization was performed as above, except with unlabeled L-21-tether and L-21-substrate-ATTO647N. Cy3-Nop1 (10 or 200 nM in imaging buffer) was added to slide immediately after video recording was started. The stoichiometry of Nop1 binding was estimated using the total fluorescence intensity divided by 200 (see SI Methods). Unfolding and co-localization with P1 hairpin and DNA tethers were performed similarly (see SI Methods).


#### 5WJ rRNA unfolding and colocalization
  Folding of the 16S 5WJ RNA was monitored by TIRF microscopy as previously described [38], using T3-ATTO647N-biotin and SA5-ATTO532 oligonucleotides complementary to sequence extensions at the 5′ and 3′ ends of the rRNA. The RNA was heat renatured in 50 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 7.5) and 20 mM (NH4)2SO4, then equilibrated with 4 or 10 mM MgCl2 for 5 min at 37°C before immobilization. Proteins were diluted in imaging buffer to the desired concentrations before addition to slides. For CsdA unwinding and the respective controls, ATP was added to 1 mM. Co-transcriptional assembly was performed as previously described [38], with 10 nM Cy3-S4 and 0–200 nM Nop1. See SI Methods for details.


#### Movie recording
  Movies were recorded with nine frames of 640 nm excitation (100 ms per frame) to check acceptor fluorophore activity, 1000 frames (100 s) of 532 nm (ATTO 532) excitation, followed by nine frames of 640 nm excitation to monitor acceptor photobleaching. The angle of laser incidence was then slightly adjusted to make the laser pass the TIRF surface at a sharp angle and illuminate droplet edges to delineate the droplet.


### Confocal microscopy
  Phase separation of Nop1 and tRNA was initiated as described for TIRF microscopy, but with an additional 150 nM (final) Cy3-Nop1 to visualize the droplets. Coacervate solution (10 µl) was spread on a DDS-coated coverslip prepared as described above. After 3 min, 30 µl of 50 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 7.5), 20 mM (NH4)2SO4, and 10 mM MgCl2 was added to avoid evaporation. The droplets were bleached and imaged within 1 h using an LSM800 confocal microscope (Zeiss) equipped with a 561 nm laser and 40× water immersion objective. See SI Methods for analysis of fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and estimation of Nop1 concentration inside and outside droplets.


### Statistical Analyses

#### Single-molecule image analysis
  Recorded videos were analyzed using Imscroll [46] as described in SI Methods. Following corrections for background and crosstalk, FRET efficiencies were determined for all immobilized RNA molecules with active ATTO 532 and ATTO 647N fluorophores. For Nop1-RNA colocalization, a few frames of red excitation at the beginning of the movie were used to pick immobilized RNA molecules for analysis. Areas of interest containing more than one molecule were excluded from further analysis (<5%).


#### FRET state lifetimes
  Because the donor fluorophore intensity is affected by droplet autofluorescence, only the acceptor intensity (FRET) was used for extracting the lifetimes of the high and low FRET states in Fig. 1. Transitions between the high and low FRET states were defined by thresholds determined for each trace based on the 95th percentile of acceptor intensity for that trace (FIhigh). The quality of state extraction is shown in Fig. 1B–D. Cumulative distributions of the lifetimes were fitted using a single exponential function. Simulation of two-site Nop1 binding and the docking kinetics is described in SI Methods.


#### Nop1 binding
  Intervals of Cy3-Nop1 binding were defined by an increase in the sum of the Cy3 and ATTO 647N or Cy5 intensities to account for any FRET. The duration of Nop1 binding events was fitted by a triple exponential distribution by maximum likelihood [46] to obtain the probability of a binding event falling into binding mode with lifetime τ. Errors were estimated by bootstrap resampling [47]. Nop1 occupancy was defined as the proportion of time that immobilized RNAs were bound by Nop1, averaged across all molecules in each experiment (∼100 molecules per replicate). See Supplementary Table S3 for the number of replicates and molecules used for each experiment. One-way ANOVA was performed for each replicate to test significance.


#### Error analysis of fitted parameters
  The 95% confidence intervals of all fitted parameters were calculated using the Jacobian of the fitted value with respect to the coefficients and the mean squared error.


## Results

### Monitoring RNA folding dynamics inside coacervates by smFRET
  Because eukaryotic ribosome assembly has not yet been reconstituted in vitro, we used established model systems to understand the impact of nucleolar proteins on RNA folding and rRNA–protein assembly. The well-studied L-21 ribozyme derived from the Tetrahymena thermophila self-splicing group I intron was selected as a model for RNA folding [48]. This intron and its relatives are often found in the rDNA of unicellular eukaryotes, where folding of the intron and surrounding rRNA are likely coupled [49, 50]. In this system, the P1 helix containing the 5′ splice site docks with the catalytic center [51, 52] via tertiary interactions between the P1 helix and the core of the ribozyme [53–55]. To monitor P1 docking, we adopted an established single-molecule FRET (smFRET) assay (Fig. 1A), in which renatured L-21 ribozymes are immobilized on a quartz slide for TIRF microscopy [41]. For immobilization, a 3′ extension of the ribozyme was hybridized with a DNA tether labeled with the ATTO 647N acceptor fluorophore (see the “Materials and methods” section and Supplementary Tables S2). The ribozyme sequence was extended at its 5′ end [40] to enable stable annealing with a substrate RNA labeled with the ATTO 532 donor fluorophore. As previously observed [41], the P1 helix fluctuated between high FRET docked and low FRET undocked states (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table S3). The transition rates (kdock = 1.76 s−1, 95% CI [1.71, 1.80] and kundock = 0.42 s−1 [0.41, 0.42]; Supplementary Table S4) were comparable with previous results (kdock = 1.62 s−1 and kundock = 0.22 s−1) [41].

  Synthetic droplets were prepared using C. thermophilum (Cth) Nop1 [36] and E. coli tRNA. Cth Nop1 is a homolog of human FBL that shares its well-conserved composition and structure [56] and the ability of FBL to phase separate with a wide range of RNA [6]. We chose tRNA as the RNA component of our synthetic droplets because it has no expected specific interactions with the other molecules used in this study. Phase separation was initiated by adding tRNA to Cth Nop1 in 50 mM HEPES–KOH (pH 7.5), 20 mM (NH4)2SO4, and 10 mM MgCl2. To assess their quality, droplets doped with 150 nM Cy3-labeled Nop1 were imaged using confocal microscopy under the same conditions used for TIRF microscopy (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The results showed that the droplets were ∼2 µm in diameter and dome-shaped, indicating that the droplet contacts an area of the slide roughly equal to the area within its circumference (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Upon photobleaching, the fluorescence intensity recovered slowly over 5 min (Supplementary Fig. S1C), demonstrating that the droplets were stable yet retained liquid properties. These results were consistent with previous studies of Nop1–RNA droplets [6, 57].

  To image single L-21 ribozymes in droplets by TIRF microscopy, droplets were added to the DDS-coated flow chamber immediately after combining Nop1 and tRNA and allowed to settle onto the slide surface for 5 min. The settled droplets were visible by autofluorescence upon 532 nm laser excitation (Supplementary Fig. S1D) or from a ring-like reflection when the alignment of 640 nm laser was adjusted to illuminate the droplet edges (Supplementary Fig. S1D). We observed Cy3-labeled Nop1 diffusing into settled droplets (Supplementary Fig. S1E), confirming that Nop1 continued to exchange between the dilute and condensed phases.

  As the droplets settled, a fraction of ribozymes attached to the slide became submerged in a droplet, whereas other ribozymes remained in the dilute phase (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1D). This allowed us to directly compare P1 docking in the dilute and condensed phases. We observed that ribozymes both inside and outside the droplets were more likely to populate the low FRET state (undocked conformation) compared with control experiments without Nop1 (Fig. 1B–E). We considered whether the similar effect inside and outside droplets can be explained by ribozymes that are overshadowed by a droplet but not inside the dense phase. However, we found that proximity to the center of a droplet, where contact with the slide is greatest, made no difference to the observed P1 docking of ribozymes that we scored as being inside a droplet (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

  On average, the dwell times were longer for the low FRET state (Fig. 1F) and shorter for the high FRET state (Fig. 1G), indicating that the Nop1–tRNA coacervate changed the rate constants for docking and undocking, consistent with a shift in the docking equilibrium. Unexpectedly, the docking kinetics inside and outside droplets were indistinguishable from each other (Fig. 1E–G). These results indicated that Nop1 perturbs the folded structure of the ribozyme, but this perturbation does not depend on condensation of Nop1 into a droplet.


### Microscopic interactions between the Nop1 and the ribozyme perturb P1 docking
  Because Nop1 is present in both the dilute and condensed phases, we reasoned that the binding of Nop1, rather than its phase separation, perturbs P1 docking with the ribozyme core. To test this idea, the immobilized ribozymes were exposed to a solution of 2 nM–2 µM Nop1 without tRNA that does not phase-separate (Fig. 2A). Addition of Nop1 decreased the mean extent of P1 docking within 5 s (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2A–C). This effect depended on Nop1 concentration and was still noticeable at 2 nM (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S2C), indicating a shift in the RNA population from the high FRET to low FRET state. After measuring Nop1 concentration inside and outside the droplets in Fig. 1 (see the “Materials and methods” section), the relationship between Nop1 concentration and the partition into the high FRET state was readily fitted by a Hill function with n = 0.5 (Fig. 2D), suggesting that the effect only depends on Nop1 binding. The apparent anti-cooperativity may be due to self-association of free Nop1 at high concentration [6, 10]. The FRET efficiencies of the high and low FRET states shifted slightly following Nop1 addition (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2A–C), suggesting that Nop1 mildly alters the ensemble of conformations that represent the docked (high FRET) and undocked (low FRET) states.

  To test whether the destabilizing effect of Nop1 is reversible, immobilized L-21 ribozymes were incubated with 200 nM Nop1 for 1 min, then washed with buffer to remove excess free Nop1 (Fig. 2E). The average FRET efficiency partially recovered within a few seconds and remained constant for up to 5 min (Fig. 2E). The average FRET efficiency did not fully return to the pre-Nop1 level, however, either because not all Nop1 was washed away or because some RNA became trapped in a non-native structure.

  We considered whether tiny droplets of Nop1 form around the RNA and perturb its folding. To assess this possibility, we labeled Nop1 nonspecifically with Cy3 and observed its interaction with the immobilized L-21 ribozyme (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S2F). At 10 and 200 nM Nop1, single L-21 ribozymes were mainly occupied by less than one Nop1 at a time (Fig. 2G and H), as judged by the distribution of Cy3 intensities (Fig. 2I). These results confirmed that Nop1 directly binds the RNA under these conditions. Moreover, only a countable number of Nop1 proteins from the dilute phase interacted with L-21 ribozyme simultaneously, making it unlikely that the observed perturbation to P1 docking arises from condensation of Nop1 around the RNA.


### Nop1 disrupts P1 helix docking but not base pairing
  Since the P1 helix must base pair before docking with the ribozyme core [51], we wondered whether Nop1 perturbs P1 docking by disrupting its secondary structure. To test this, we performed smFRET experiments on an RNA hairpin with the same stem as the P1 helix. When folded, ATTO 532 on the 5′ end of the RNA comes near the ATTO 647N-labeled DNA tether (Fig. 3A), resulting in high FRET. A nonspecific RNA helicase, E. coli CsdA (200 nM), transiently unfolded the P1 helix, resulting in excursions to low FRET states (Fig. 3B). However, the addition of 200 nM Nop1 did not change the FRET distribution, suggesting that it does not detectably unfold stable RNA secondary structure under these conditions (Fig. 3C). Using fluorescence colocalization, we found that Cy3-Nop1 was slightly more likely to bind single-stranded RNA than double-stranded RNA or double-stranded DNA tether (Fig. 3D–G). Therefore, we cannot exclude that Nop1 can capture weak RNA duplexes that spontaneously unfold.

  If Nop1 does not unwind P1, it could directly block P1 docking or indirectly destabilize docking by stripping Mg2+ ions from the RNA, as has been suggested [35]. Several observations suggested that Nop1 does not destabilize the L-21 ribozyme tertiary structure merely by sequestering Mg2+ ions. First, we confirmed that lowering magnesium from 10 to 2 mM promoted undocking, whereas 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid completely destabilized the ribozyme and resulted in no FRET (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B), as previously shown [40, 41]. Because we never observed complete unfolding by Nop1 in 10 mM Mg2+, we concluded that it cannot displace all the Mg2+ ions when the ribozyme is stably folded. Adding 200 nM Nop1 with 2 mM Mg2+, however, abolished P1 docking within seconds, followed by a slower transition to an even lower FRET state (Supplementary Fig. S3A and C). The observed sequential unfolding in low Mg2+ was recapitulated by a quantitative two-binding-site model (Supplementary Fig. S3D–G), in which Nop1 binding to the undocked state triggered disruption of the entire ribozyme tertiary structure by a second Nop1 (see the “Materials and methods” section for details). This suggested that high levels of Nop1 may be able to progressively invade unstable RNA structures. Because RNA folding and counterion interactions are strongly coupled, even partial RNA unfolding by Nop1 is expected to release Mg2+ ions into the bulk solution [58–60], explaining why Nop1 is associated with a reduction in RNA-bound Mg2+ ions [35].


### Transient RNA binding of FBL/Nop1 requires the GAR domain
  To investigate which domain of Nop1 is responsible for interacting with the ribozyme, we prepared one Nop1 fragment containing the N-terminal GAR domain and BCO, a sequence without a defined function (Fig. 4A) [56], and a second fragment containing the BCO and methyltransferase (MD) domains (Fig. 4A). In smFRET docking experiments, 200 nM Nop1-GAR-BCO favored the undocked ribozyme half as much as full-length Nop1, whereas Nop1-BCO-MD had no observable effect on the RNA conformation (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). Single-molecule colocalization experiments showed that full-length Cy3-labeled Nop1 frequently interacted with the RNA (Fig. 4C), albeit briefly (≤ 5 s; Supplementary Fig. S4D). However, Nop1-BCO-MD did not appreciably bind to the ribozyme, yielding sparse co-localization events lasting <1s (Fig. 4D). Although the MD domain was reported to drive RNA binding of Arabidopsis thaliana fibrillarin [61], EMSA results confirmed that Cth Nop1-GAR-BCO but not Nop1-BCO-MD binds yeast U3 snoRNA (Supplementary Fig. S4E), consistent with our single-molecule experiments. Finally, we found that human FBL, which possesses a similar GAR domain as Cth Nop1, also perturbs the folding of the L-21 ribozyme (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4E and F). Thus, we concluded that FBL/Nop1 nonspecifically binds the ribozyme through its disordered, RGG-rich GAR domain, and this interaction hinders docking of the P1 helix.

  To test whether the destabilizing effect of the Nop1 GAR domain only depends on its amino acid composition, we added a synthetic (RGG)5 peptide to the immobilized ribozymes. Surprisingly, 2 µM (RGG)5 promoted rather than disfavored P1 docking in 2 or 10 mM Mg2+ (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4H–J). It is possible that the long (70 aa) RGG repeats in the Nop1 GAR domain form more stable or more extensive nonspecific interactions with the ribozyme that block P1 docking on the 0.1 s timescale of our TIRF experiment. In contrast, the peptide may effectively function as a multivalent cation to stabilize the RNA tertiary structure, highlighting the importance of IDR length and composition for RNA folding.


### Native rRNA–protein assembly outcompetes nonspecific Nop1 binding
  Fibrillarin interacts with immature ribosomal complexes but must disengage as the ribosomal complexes mature [56]. It was suggested that assembly of the pre-rRNA reduces its solubility in the nucleolus by covering the rRNA with ribosomal proteins and assembly factors that compete with nonspecific nucleolar protein binding [62, 63]. To test this idea, we asked whether E. coli ribosomal protein uS4 can compete with nonspecific Nop1 binding to a 5WJ in the 16S rRNA, thereby allowing assembly to proceed (Fig. 5A). Stable recruitment of uS4 to the 16S 5WJ is a well-characterized model for ribosome assembly [43, 64] that depends on proper folding of the 5WJ rRNA [65, 66].

  The 5WJ rRNA was labeled with Cy5 fluorophore and immobilized as previously described [38]. In colocalization experiments, 10 nM Cy3-Nop1 alone occupied the 5WJ rRNA ~6% of the time on average (Fig. 5B), forming mainly short-lived complexes with the rRNA as with the L-21 ribozyme (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Adding 10 nM uS4 reduced Nop1 occupancy to 2% (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5B). By contrast, ribosomal protein uS7 that recognizes the 16S 3′ domain rRNA did not significantly decrease Nop1 binding (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5C). Thus, specific but not nonspecific rRNA–protein interactions can exclude Nop1 from the rRNA. Conversely, Nop1 did not measurably affect specific uS4 binding to the 5WJ when uS4 occupancy was directly monitored by labeling uS4 with Cy3 (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S5D and E).

  We previously showed that the 5WJ rRNA tends to misfold during transcription, which hinders stable binding of uS4 and favors low-affinity, non-native uS4 complexes [38, 47]. Therefore, we tested the effect of Nop1 on co-transcriptional assembly of uS4–5WJ complexes. When Nop1 was added to rRNA newly transcribed on the slide, it decreased uS4 binding even further (Supplementary Fig. S5F–J). This inhibition of co-transcriptional assembly suggested that Nop1 either slows initial folding or inhibits rRNA refolding, possibly by destabilizing helix junctions. Thus, free Nop1 protein forms weak interactions with the rRNA. These weak, nonspecific interactions are excluded by specific binding of ribosomal proteins, allowing native RNP assembly to proceed.


### Nonspecific Nop1 binding shields rRNA from ribosome assembly factor CsdA
  DEAD-box RNA helicases use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to actively unfold RNA, such as variants of the Tetrahymena ribozyme, allowing the RNA a second chance to refold correctly [67]. In nature, DEAD-box proteins facilitate splicing of group I and group II introns [68–70] among other targets. Eukaryotic ribosome assembly involves many DEAD-box RNA helicases that restructure the pre-rRNA, promote pre-rRNA cleavage, and release snoRNPs [71–73]. We previously showed that the nonspecific E. coli DEAD-box protein CsdA [74, 75] unwinds misfolded rRNA structures that accumulate during transcription [38]. Here, we used CsdA and the E. coli 16S 5WJ rRNA to investigate how the function of DEAD-box proteins may be affected by phase separation and Nop1.

  To monitor unfolding, the 5′ and 3′ ends of the 5WJ rRNA were tagged with acceptor and donor fluorophores (Fig. 6A), resulting in high FRET efficiency (∼0.8) for the folded state. Unfolding by CsdA in the absence of Nop1 reduced the FRET efficiency to ∼0.1 in a few seconds (Fig. 6B), as previously observed [38]. To test the effect of phase separation on unwinding by CsdA, Nop1–tRNA droplets were formed as before and allowed to settle over some of the immobilized 5WJ rRNA molecules (Fig. 6A). Two hundred nanomolar of CsdA plus 1 mM ATP was either pre-mixed with Nop1 before phase separation (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S6A and B) or added into the flow chamber after Nop1–tRNA droplets were settled (Supplementary Fig. S6C–E). Remarkably, Nop1–tRNA protected the 5WJ rRNA from CsdA unwinding both inside and outside the droplets (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S6A–E). This was true whether CsdA was added before or after the droplets were formed.

  We next asked which component of the droplets protected the 5WJ rRNA from unwinding. We found that 0.2 mg/ml tRNA (the same amount used in droplet formation) partially reduced CsdA activity (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6F), presumably by acting as a competitive inhibitor. However, 2 µM Nop1 alone in the dilute phase was sufficient to completely protect the 5WJ rRNA from CsdA unwinding (Fig. 6D–G). The level of protection was equal to that of droplets formed from 20 µM Nop1 + tRNA. We observed that Nop1 also lowered the average FRET efficiency of the folded 5WJ rRNA, indicating some perturbation to its structure. This effect was distinct from transitions to a low FRET state due to rRNA unfolding.

  When CsdA was pre-mixed with only 200 nM Nop1, the 5WJ rRNA initially unfolded within the first 10 s, then slowly refolded over 50 s (Fig. 6H and Supplementary Fig. S6G). This recovery showed that CsdA was not inactivated by Nop1. Indeed, the RNA continued to fluctuate between folded and unfolded states throughout the 30 s video (Supplementary Fig. S6G). Instead, recovery was likely due to nonspecific Nop1 binding that shielded the 5WJ rRNA from CsdA, allowing its secondary structure to reform. To test this possibility more directly, we pre-incubated the 5WJ rRNA with CsdA so that all the rRNA was unfolded at the start of the experiment. We then added a mixture of 200 nM Nop1 and CsdA to the sample on the slide. The population of rRNA molecules was able to refold over 20 s, showing that Nop1 successfully prevented CsdA from acting on them (Fig. 6I and Supplementary Fig. S6H).


## Discussion
  In this study, we used synthetic FBL/Nop1 droplets and model RNAs to investigate how nucleolar proteins affect the formation of RNA and protein interactions during ribosome assembly. Our results show that microscopic interactions with the arginine-rich FBL/Nop1 GAR domain destabilize RNA folding independently of liquid–liquid phase separation. Since Nop1 interacts similarly with different RNAs, these perturbations are likely to be nonspecific and general. By contrast, site-specific assembly factors such as the Kre33 module hinder compaction of particular rRNA domains until they are released by a processing event [63, 76]. FBL/Nop1 assembly with a box C/D snoRNA is expected to direct the GAR domain to rRNA residues near the snoRNA target site, conferring regional specificity to its perturbations. As an abundant protein in the DFC, free FBL/Nop1 may also interact nonspecifically with other sites in the pre-rRNA. For instance, the association of human FBL and the pre-rRNA 5′ETS [10] raises the possibility that FBL directly facilitates the earliest steps of co-transcriptional folding.

  Interestingly, our results on the L-21 ribozyme and 16S 5WJ show that Nop1 primarily acts by reducing the strength of rRNA tertiary interactions, rather than by unpairing RNA helices. The Nop1 GAR domain could inhibit helix packing by masking the double helices or by preventing the folding of helix junctions that typically have some unpaired residues. In general, more open tertiary structures permit faster reorganization of RNA secondary structures, thereby lessening the impact of kinetic folding traps during assembly [77–79]. Thus, nucleolar proteins such as FBL/Nop1 may ameliorate ribosome assembly by transiently engaging the pre-rRNA during transcription and delaying its compaction into higher-order structures, thereby widening the time window for structural reorganization.

  Our study also reveals that Nop1 modulates the intricate competition between specific and nonspecific protein interactions during ribosome assembly. Although we have used model bacterial and yeast components to evaluate this competition, the results inform on the principles of ribosome assembly because each protein acts on the RNA rather than on each other. First, Nop1 is displaced from the 16S 5WJ rRNA by specific assembly with its cognate ribosomal protein uS4, but not by low-affinity binding of uS4 to the nascent RNA or by a non-cognate ribosomal protein uS7 (Fig. 5). Although this intricate competition may be tuned differently in different organisms, our result is consistent with the proposal that specific interactions between ribosomal proteins and rRNA decrease the valency for nonspecific interactions with nucleolar proteins, thereby thermodynamically excluding mature ribosomes from the nucleolus [62, 63]. Second, we observe that Nop1 shields the rRNA from unwinding by the DEAD-box helicase CsdA (Fig. 6), possibly by preferentially interacting with helix junctions and internal bulges. This competition operates in both dilute and condensed phases. An interesting possibility is that passive interactions with nucleolar proteins limit excessive rRNA unfolding by ATP-dependent helicases and remodeling enzymes.

  The side-by-side comparison of dilute and condensed Nop1 enabled by our single-molecule experiments demonstrates that Nop1 perturbs RNA folding and assembly regardless of phase separation. Evidence that specific RNA-protein recognition is not altered by phase separation [80] further supports the idea that the microscopic interactions important for ribosome assembly do not depend on the macroscopic organization of the molecules. Instead of directly affecting ribosome assembly, phase separation of the nucleolus may concentrate assembly factors and enhance assembly efficiency [8]. Additionally, phase separation may limit unwanted RNA binding by nucleolar proteins elsewhere in the cell, including other nucleolar sub-compartments. Finally, phase separation of the nucleolus may be important for other aspects of ribosome assembly not investigated here.

  Like Nop1, many nucleolar proteins contain RGG motifs that are important for both RNA binding and phase separation. The intrinsically disordered Nop1 GAR domain is more variable across species than the BCO and MD domains [56] and may function independently of methyltransferase in ribosome assembly. Single-molecule experiments have shown that the phase-separating protein fused in sarcoma (FUS) dynamically engages single-stranded RNA and reduces its contour length [81, 82]. Modulation of the RNA structure by FUS also depends on interspaced disordered RGG motifs and RNA recognition motifs in its RNA binding domain [82]. Our results highlight how nonspecific and transient interactions with RGG proteins can modulate the assembly of stable RNPs like the ribosome.


### Limitations of this study
  The synthetic systems used in this study offer complete control over the reaction composition, allowing precise dissection of molecular function. A limitation is that our simplified reactions lack the many RNA-binding proteins and assembly factors present in cells and nucleoli. In the future it will be interesting to investigate how RNA folding and RNP assembly are affected by more complex mixtures of RNA-binding proteins and RNAs. Another limitation is that our single-molecule study used well-structured RNAs that fold reliably. Natural pre-rRNA transcripts may be more susceptible to disruption by nonspecific interactions with RNA-binding proteins. Lastly, it will be interesting to study the impact of RNA condensates on free ribosomal proteins that also contain partially disordered regions.
