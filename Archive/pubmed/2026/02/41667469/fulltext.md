# Oxygen depletion in biomolecular condensates is dominated by macromolecular density


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates are dynamic cellular compartments formed by the self-assembly of proteins and nucleic acids. Many metabolites partition into condensates based on their interactions with the macromolecular constituents; yet, whether gases behave similarly remains unknown. Here, we show that oxygen partitions into protein-based condensates formed by intrinsically disordered repeat proteins with systematically varied sequences. Using microelectrodes, phosphorescence lifetime imaging microscopy, and molecular dynamics simulations, we find that oxygen is partially excluded from the condensate, and its partitioning does not correlate with the condensate hydrophobicity. Instead, oxygen concentration is inversely related to condensate protein density. These results suggest that the prevailing theory of small-molecule partitioning into condensates has to be augmented to consider the concentration of macromolecules as a dominant factor in the absence of interactions between the metabolite and the condensate. Our results suggest that biomolecular condensates can generate a nanoscale oxygen gradient, potentially modulating the local availability of oxygen for biochemical reactions within the cell.


## Introduction
  Oxygen gradients form throughout biology when the rate of consumption exceeds the rate at which it can be replenished by diffusion. Oxygen gradients are well documented on meter scales in the ocean due to the respiration of organic matter, or on centimeter scales in e.g., lake bottoms1–3. In our blood circulation, oxygen gradients occur on sub-millimeter scales across alveoli and arteries, where oxygenated blood releases oxygen to tissues4. Diffusion works more efficiently at short length scales, and therefore, it is increasingly difficult for oxygen gradients to arise. However, it has been speculated that oxygen gradients could also arise on a sub-cellular length scale, but this has been difficult to test experimentally. Nonetheless, oxygen gradients have been reported in giant umbrella cells, where deoxygenated regions formed around mitochondria due to aerobic respiration5. Here, we investigate an additional mechanism by which oxygen gradients might arise on a subcellular length scale: passive partitioning into membrane-less organelles.

  Cells are compartmentalized into several compartments. In addition to the traditional organelles, a new type of compartments has recently been identified that form by condensation of biomolecules, so-called biomolecular condensates, or membrane-less organelles6–8. Like traditional organelles, membrane-less organelles can regulate biochemical reactions by spatial confinement – some components are concentrated, and others are excluded9. Unlike traditional organelles, membrane-less ones can form and dissolve and thus serve as a trigger for changes in cellular metabolism or signaling10–12. Oxygen is the ultimate oxidizing agent in most of our metabolism and thus takes part in many biochemical reactions, and in undesired side reactions for others. To understand how membrane-less organelles affect oxygen-dependent cellular processes, it is thus necessary to understand how oxygen partitions into biomolecular condensates.

  Biomolecular condensates concentrate or exclude molecules through chemical partition equilibria. Condensates have a different interior milieu compared to the bulk cytosol that forms solvation-like interactions with other molecules and thus acts as a distinct solvent phase13,14. Macromolecules like proteins and RNA can be concentrated by thousands of folds or excluded because the condensate forms a mesh with pores too small to accommodate molecules beyond a certain size. Comparatively little is known about the partitioning of small molecules into condensates, as these cannot easily be tagged with a fluorophore. Using fluorescence microscopy to quantify partitioning, it has been shown that condensates up-concentrate a range of small molecules in vitro and in cells15–17, and that the protein scaffold creates distinct chemical environments that concentrate specific molecules. These studies were, however, limited to the study of fluorophores and thus mostly aromatic compounds. Mass spectrometry and NMR approaches developed for metabolomics allow investigation of a broader range of metabolites and drug-like compounds18–20. This led to the conclusion that small molecule partitioning did not depend on high-affinity binding with scaffold protein or pore size, but rather depended on solvent properties of the condensates, principally hydrophobicity. The investigation of condensate metabolomes in cells further revealed that phospholipids are particularly enriched in condensates20.

  A key challenge for the study of the subcellular oxygen gradients has been a scarcity of methods to detect such gradients. Phosphorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (PLIM) is currently the oxygen imaging technique with the highest spatial resolution. Some transition metal dye molecules display oxygen-sensitive phosphorescence lifetimes, which have been developed into a series of probe molecules used for PLIM. The presence of heavy metals in these phosphorescent probes allows the efficient intersystem crossing from the singlet excited state to the triplet excited state, leading to phosphorescence21,22. The phosphorescence of these metal complex dyes, called indicators, gets quenched by molecular oxygen by the process of energy transfer from the indicator to the oxygen. We speculated that these dyes could be used to measure oxygen gradients inside biomolecular condensates as well.

  In the present work, we measured oxygen partitioning in the biomolecular condensates using PLIM and microelectrodes. We found that oxygen was partially excluded from condensates, a finding corroborated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Instead, we found a strong anti-correlation between the concentrations of oxygen and protein inside the condensate. This suggests that the model for partitioning of small molecules has to be augmented to consider the effect of the macromolecular density—especially in the absence of attractive interactions between the metabolite and condensate.


## Results

### Synthetic IDP as a model system for determining oxygen concentration in condensates
  We aimed to study how oxygen partitions into biomolecular condensates (Fig. 1a) and thus needed a model system that is both representative of natural condensates and experimentally convenient. Many intrinsically disordered protein-based models phase separate in the presence of crowding agents like PEG. However, these crowders also affect oxygen solubility23, which is undesirable in this study. Therefore, we used a synthetic intrinsically disordered protein model system consisting of octapeptide repeats that form condensates at low saturation concentrations (Csat) without a crowding agent (Fig. 1b)24. The synthetic IDP system also permits systematic variation of the number of repeats and the properties of the sequence by changing the amino acid composition. The repeat protein, i.e., GRGDSPYS, originally contained serine in positions 5 and 8, which we replaced with glutamine, i.e., GRGDQPYQ, for compatibility with other experiments. The repeat protein is charge neutral but has a slight positive charge coming from the termini.   Initially, we expressed and purified [Q5,8]−30 and reconstituted in PBS buffer. The protein formed micrometer-sized spherical assemblies that fuse upon encounter and increase in size with time (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The droplets were polydisperse with diameter in the range 2–12 µm (Supplementary Fig. 1b). These peptide repeats exhibit upper critical solution temperature (UCST), meaning they form a two-phase system at lower temperature and turn into a single homogeneous phase above a critical temperature (Supplementary Fig. 1c), consistent with previous characterization24, This suggested that [Q5,8]−30 exhibits prototypical UCST liquid-liquid phase separation and is thus a good model for biomolecular condensates formed by many IDPs.


### Estimation of oxygen partitioning using a microelectrode
  To measure oxygen concentrations inside biomolecular condensates, we initially used an electrochemical Clark-type microsensor with a tip diameter of 10 μm. We created a macroscopically phase separated sample by centrifugation, which resulted in a dilute phase on top of a protein rich dense phase (Fig. 1c). The oxygen profile was measured as the sensor was inserted across the phase boundary (Fig. 1d). The oxygen concentration was constant in the dilute phase, but gradually dropped with increasing depth, whereas no change was seen for buffer. The slope of the oxygen concentration dropped with increasing depth, suggesting an approach to a plateau. The electrochemical measurements indicated that the homogeneous dense phase partially excluded oxygen. The gradient observed by the microelectrode spanned more than two millimeters, which is a much broader transition than expected from partitioning across a phase boundary. To test whether this represents a true gradient, we measured the oxygen concentration at a static depth over half an hour and observed a gradual approach to steady state (Fig. 1e). The oxygen-consumption of the electrode is minuscule and is too low to cause a gradual depletion of oxygen, so instead we hypothesized that the gradual transition represented the interaction between the electrode and the condensates, driving a deformation of the condensate. To test this, we measured oxygen concentrations in a phase-separated sample with dispersed droplets, while observing the tip of the electrode under a microscope (Fig. 1f). We observed that the droplets collapse onto the electrode, merging into film-like condensate on the tip of the electrode. This is similar to the wetting of the surface observed for many condensates. Simultaneous measurement of oxygen concentrations showed an oxygen concentration between the dilute solution and the steady-state value inside the condensate. The electrochemical measurements show that the oxygen concentrations can be measured inside macroscopically phase-separated samples, and the oxygen is depleted inside the [Q5,8]−30 condensates. However, the sensor tip perturbs the phase-separated sample, and it is not likely to be able to probe oxygen distributions in dispersed condensates. Therefore, we sought to develop a non-perturbing technique that measures oxygen concentration in co-existing phases.


### Measurement of oxygen inside the condensates using phosphorescence lifetime imaging
  PLIM has been used to quantify the oxygen concentration in, for example, mammalian cells and organoids25–29. The phosphorescent dye is quenched by the triplet state of molecular oxygen, and the lifetime thus decreases with increasing oxygen concentration (Fig. 2a). We speculated that PLIM would also be useful for measuring oxygen partitioning in phase-separated systems. As a sensor, we used dichlorotris (1,10-phenanthroline) ruthenium (II) hydrate (Fig. 2a), which is a water-soluble, positively charged dye, has excitation and emission in the visible range and has been used for PLIM-based oxygen imaging. In the following, we will refer to it as the ruthenium dye. We incubated the ruthenium dye with a phase-separated sample of 20 μM [Q5,8]−30, and we imaged the condensates at 40X magnification using PLIM to record the phosphorescence intensity. Initially, we compared total dye concentrations from 100 μM to 1 mM (Supplementary Fig. 2), aiming to find the highest concentration where lifetimes are not affected by self-quenching. Phosphorescence lifetimes are similar for 100 and 500 μM, whereas 1 mM dye results in shorter lifetimes, suggesting undesirable self-quenching.   Condensates can be distinguished as round objects of elevated intensity (Fig. 2b) and changed lifetimes (Fig. 2c), which allows the determination of the lifetime in the dense and dilute phases simultaneously. Cross-sections of droplets reveal a steep gradient in the phosphorescence lifetime between a high value in the dilute phase and a lower value in the dense phase (Fig. 2c). In principle, the gradient could represent a boundary layer zone between the phases. However, the spatial resolution of the imaging system is likely not sufficient to resolve the boundary layer, so we focus on the lifetime in the center of the condensate. To quantify oxygen concentrations, we measured the phosphorescence lifetime in the absence of oxygen by adding sodium sulfite, a chemical oxygen scavenger (Fig. 2d, e). Neither sodium sulfite nor the dye affects the phase behavior of the condensates at the concentrations used, as assessed by the critical concentration and temperature of phase separation (Supplementary Fig. 3). Removal of oxygen increased phosphorescence lifetimes in both the dense and the dilute phases, but the lifetime remained lower inside the condensates than outside (Fig. 2e). This suggested that the dye experienced a different environment inside the condensate, which changes its phosphorescence emission. It is necessary to account for this change in basal lifetime when quantifying oxygen concentrations.

  The change in phosphorescence lifetime could either be due to an interaction between the dye and the IDP or an emergent property of the dense phase. To distinguish these possibilities, we used an octapeptide which has the same sequence repeat as the condensate-forming IDPs but did not undergo phase separation. We titrated the dye with the octapeptide while recording phosphorescence lifetime and intensity (Fig. 2f, g). The addition of the peptide did not affect the phosphorescence lifetime but led to a ~20% reduction of phosphorescence intensity. The curve shows clear saturation in the high-micromolar range, indicating a weak, but specific interaction with the peptide resulting in slight static quenching. These interactions also lead to preferential partitioning of the dye into the condensates, shown by a partition coefficient of 80 ± 12 as measured by comparing intensity by confocal fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2h), which is similar to the partitioning of other hydrophobic small molecules into condensates17,19. In total, this suggests that the change in lifetime is not due to a simple collisional quenching by the condensate protein but is an emergent property of the different physical environment inside the condensate that has to be accounted for.

### Experimental determination of oxygen partitioning coefficients
  To quantify the oxygen concentration, we measured phosphorescence lifetimes in [Q5,8]−30 condensates at different sodium sulfite concentrations that only partially scavenge the dissolved oxygen (Fig. 3a). Oxygen concentrations were estimated by a titration in a buffer sample, which was observed over time following the addition of sodium sulfite (Supplementary Fig. 4). 10 mM Na2SO3 resulted in complete removal of oxygen after 3 min, whereas lower concentrations gradually decreased to a near steady-state plateau. We performed PLIM imaging 25 min after the addition of Na2SO3, which allowed us to estimate the oxygen concentration from the standard curve.   In the absence of Na2SO3, the oxygen concentration determined using PLIM data was 254 µM ± 6.0 µM in the dilute phase and 218 µM ± 8.6 µM in the dense phase (Fig. 3d). Comparison of [O2]dense/[O2]dilute across different Na2SO3 concentrations resulted in a partitioning coefficient of 0.86 ± 0.027. In agreement with microelectrode measurements, this indicated that protein condensates partially exclude oxygen and created low oxygen zones inside them. While it is possible to measure oxygen lifetimes at intermediate oxygen concentrations using chemical oxygen scavengers, the oxygen concentration is only stable at atmospheric and completely anoxic conditions. In the remaining experiments, we thus focus on these two conditions, which still allowed us to determine oxygen concentrations as described above.


### Contributions of phase behavior on Kp
  Next, we set out to investigate the determinants of oxygen partitioning by varying the condensate properties. Condensates exhibit a polydisperse nature with a broad size distribution. Therefore, we first examined how droplet size affects phosphorescence lifetime. We observed a slight decrease in phosphorescence lifetime with increasing droplet size (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, condensates under atmospheric oxygen and anoxic conditions were affected similarly, so the size effect largely canceled out when estimating oxygen concentrations.

  To explore the determinants of oxygen partitioning further, we sought a way to change the phase diagram without changing the chemical environment in the condensates. Repeat proteins offer this possibility of altering the phase behavior without modifying the chemical properties of the constituent chains, as the phase diagram is highly sensitive to the number of identical repeats. We thus expressed and purified [Q5,8]-20, [Q5,8]−30, [Q5,8]-40, and [Q5,8]-60 and characterized them (Supplementary Fig. 6), which showed that all the variants formed condensates with condensate size decreasing with chain length. For PLIM measurements, we used a ruthenium dye that partitions uniformly into all repeat condensates (Supplementary Fig. 7). We measured the phosphorescence lifetime of the similar sized [Q5,8]-20, [Q5,8]−30, [Q5,8]-40 and [Q5,8]-60 condensates at atmospheric oxygen concentrations and anoxic conditions to determine the oxygen concentrations in the dense phase (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 8). The difference in the phosphorescence lifetime from oxic-to-anoxic condition increased with an increasing number of repeats for similar size of the droplets (Fig. 4a, b). The average phosphorescence lifetime values for dyes inside the droplets were analysed to calculate Kp and the oxygen concentration inside the condensates (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 8). In agreement with measurements for described for [Q5,8]−30 above, the oxygen concentration was lower in the dense phase than the corresponding dilute phase, demonstrating a partial exclusion from the condensate. The dense phase oxygen concentration increased monotonously towards one with increased chain length (Fig. 4c). To rule out a technical cause for this effect, we repeated the measurements using continuous dense phases obtained by centrifugation and by inserting an electrochemical sensor as in Fig. 1.   The length of the repeat protein affects the critical concentration, ranging from 26 µM (0.543 mg/mL for [Q5,8]-20 to 0.6 µM (0.03 mg/mL) for [Q5,8]-60. In homogeneous solutions, the concentration of dissolved macromolecules affects the solubility of oxygen23, which led us to measure the oxygen concentration in the dilute phase with an optical oxygen sensor. The oxygen concentration measured in the dilute phase of all variants is similar around 265 µM ± 10 µM, unlike the oxygen concentration in the dense phase which decayed with Csat (Fig. 4d). This suggested that the partitioning of oxygen was dominated by the properties of the dense phase with minimal contributions from the dilute phase.

  Chain length generally lowers the critical concentration of repeating polymers, but its effect on the dense-phase concentration is less understood due to the challenges in obtaining accurate measurements. We measured the dense phase concentration for [Q5,8]-X using two different methods: I) By determining the volume of the dense phase from confocal microscopy, which allows [P]dense to be calculated from [P]dilute and [P]total. II) By separating the two phases by sedimentation and extracting a controlled volume of dense phase using a positive displacement pipette, followed by dilution and concentration determination by UV-Visible spectroscopy. Both methods show a monotonous decrease in [P]dense with chain length (Fig. 4e) from around 220 + /− 50 mg/mL for [Q5,8]−20 to (~70 mg/mL) for [Q5,8]−60. The variation in [P]dense with repeat length was unexpected, but the range is broadly consistent with values found in other condensates31–35. Comparison of the [O2]dense and [P]dense suggests an anti-correlation (Fig. 4f) with Pearson correlation coefficients of −0.9440 (PLIM) and −0.8517 (electrode).

  To test whether the exclusion from the dense phase is specific to oxygen, we examined partitioning of three fluorophores in [Q5,8]−20 and [Q5,8]−60 using fluorescence microscopy. All three fluorophores partition favorably into both condensates, although to different extents, with partition coefficients ranging from ~4 to 60 (Supplementary Fig. 9). While the differences between [Q5,8]−20 and [Q5,8]−60 were minor, the denser [Q5,8]−20 condensate consistently contained more of the small molecule client, in contrast to the behavior observed for oxygen. Together, these findings indicate that oxygen partitioning is distinct from that of other small hydrophobic molecules, whose distribution is primarily governed by local hydrophobic environments.


### Sequence contributions to oxygen partitioning
  Previous studies of small molecule partitioning into condensates emphasized the role of hydrophobicity in the concentration of solutes in the dense phase17,19. Oxygen is non-polar with an octanol-water partition coefficient of 4.436, which suggested that it is more prone to partition into hydrophobic condensates in line with other small molecules. Previous studies have demonstrated that RLPs can accommodate hydrophobic substitutions at position 5 of the octapeptide repeat, with the replacement of glutamine (Q) by valine (V) not abolishing condensate formation but only moderately altering the phase behavior24. To systematically investigate the impact of hydrophobicity, valine substitutions were introduced at defined intervals within a 60-repeat protein: every fourth repeat, every second repeat, and every repeat. However, the most hydrophobic variant produced only small dense-phase fractions, complicating accurate assessment of condensate properties (Supplementary Fig. 10). To address this, shorter 25-repeat constructs were generated, preserving the sequence pattern and spanning GRAVY scores from approximately −2.3 to −1.3. The reduced repeat size facilitated precise measurement of protein concentrations in these constructs. The sequence design was guided by hydrophobicity values calculated using the GRAVY score, which predicts the theoretical hydrophobicity of the IDP sequence. To experimentally assess the hydrophobicity of the dense-phase condensates, we employed the solvatochromic dye PRODAN, whose emission spectrum is blue-shifted in hydrophobic environments14,37,38.   Next, we measured oxygen dense phase concentrations in this series using PLIM (Supplementary Fig. 11). [O2]dense showed a systematic decrease from ~200 to 100 µM with increasing hydrophobicity and a positive correlation with condensate polarity as measured by PRODAN (Fig. 5b) and an anti-correlation with scaffold sequence hydrophobicity measured GRAVY score (r = −0.95, Supplementary Fig. 12a). The anti-correlation was unexpected and contrary to the finding for partitioning of other hydrophobic solutes and possibly suggests another change in condensates correlated to the hydrophobicity. Inspired by the anti-correlation previously observed with [P]dense, we measured the dense phase protein concentration ([P]dense) derived from the dense phase volume fraction quantified via confocal microscopy (Supplementary Table 2). The dense phase protein concentration decreased with increasing dense phase hydrophobicity (Supplementary Fig. 12b), which is one possible explanation for the anticorrelation seen between [O2]dense and [P]dense (r = −0.93) (Fig. 5c).

  Building on the unexpected observation that oxygen partitioning decreases with increasing condensate hydrophobicity, we also measured the partitioning of small molecule hydrophobic dyes in the [Q5,8]−25 and [V5,Q8]−25 constructs (Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast to oxygen, these dyes showed slightly preferential partitioning into the more hydrophobic condensates, consistent with previous studies.

  Next, we sought to investigate oxygen partitioning in condensates showing a broader variation in chemistry. Rekhi et al. generated a broader series of RLP-like condensate-forming IDPs, including variations in charge, hydrogen bonding potential, chain flexibility, and type of aromatic sticker39. We produced these 15 variants with 25 repeats and characterized them as above. In agreement with previous studies, we observed round micrometer-sized condensates for all variants except for two highly hydrophobic constructs, i.e., GRGDLPYS and GRGDVPYV, which did not form condensates but instead produced irreversible aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 14). This observation suggests that condensate formation is restricted to a defined hydrophobicity window, beyond which increasing hydrophobicity drives the system toward irreversible aggregation. The anti-correlation between sequence hydrophobicity and PRODAN emission wavelength is weaker for this series (Fig. 5d), but still detectable (r = −0.35). A notable outlier in emission wavelength is the variant with tryptophan instead of tyrosine as the aromatic “sticker” residues. Tryptophan is notable for being an efficient quencher of many fluorophores and has previously been shown to quench PRODAN40, and this variant likely perturbs fluorescence emission through other mechanisms than condensate hydrophobicity. Accordingly, excluding the tryptophan-containing residue improves the anti-correlation between sequence-based and PRODAN-reported hydrophobicity to (r = −0.47).

  We measured dense phase oxygen concentrations in the RLP series and observed variation from approximately 50 to 250 µM. However, we did not find any apparent correlation with hydrophobicity as assessed by both PRODAN measurements (Fig. 5e) and the sequence GRAVY score (Supplementary Fig. 15a). Next, we measured the dense phase protein concentration in the RLP series using confocal volume reconstitution (Supplementary Table 2). [P]dense varies by a factor of ~3 in the series and is anti-correlated with both the [O2]dense (r = −0.76) (Fig. 5f) and the hydrophobicity (Supplementary Fig. 15b).

  Across three different series of condensates, we have thus seen dense phase protein concentration emerge as a strong predictor of oxygen partitioning; however, the correlation decreases in the last series with more sequence diversity. Notably, the two condensates with the lowest [O2]dense both involve the removal of the sole negatively charged residue in the sequence. While this is not sufficient to conclude on, it suggests that there are additional contributors to oxygen partitioning. And in turn, the condensate environment has complex sequence-function properties with emergent properties that are not easily rationalized.


### Molecular dynamics simulations of oxygen partitioning
  To investigate how oxygen interacts with the condensates in atomistic detail, we thus undertook an MD simulation of oxygen partitioning. MD methods range from all-atom models that can represent any type of chemistry to highly coarse-grained models where each residue is represented by a single bead. Although it is possible to simulate at an all-atom resolution, these force fields are generally too computationally expensive to handle the system size needed to achieve phase separation41. At a much coarser resolution, although one-bead-per-residue methods are efficient at capturing the phase behavior of IDPs, they are inherently unable to describe the interactions of small molecule clients such as oxygen42–45. Between these options is the Martini force field, which uses a transferable building block approach representing 2-4 heavy atoms by a single bead, combining beads to build entire molecules, parameterized based on underlying chemical properties46–48. Martini has already shown promise in simulating several condensate systems49–52. Building on this, we showed that Martini resolution simulations can reproduce the phase separation of [Q5,8]-20 IDPs as expected (Supplementary Fig. 16).

  We added models of the dye and oxygen to condensate simulations to assess their interaction with condensates. There is no Martini model for the dye used in this study, so instead we used HEME, which is also a large poly-aromatic ring system. HEME partitioned into the condensate as observed for the dye, but its presence did not affect the dynamics of the condensate as indicated by measurements of the protein diffusion coefficients through incoherent scattering functions (Supplementary Fig. 18)48. Qualitatively, the simulations agree with the experimental results in predicting a 50% lower oxygen concentration inside the condensate relative to the outside (Fig. 6a). Moreover, the visits of oxygen to the condensate region are typically very short lived. As Fig. 6b shows, single visits to condensate are predominantly less than 10 ns in total before the molecule leaves to the dilute region of the system again, with longer occupations becoming exponentially rare. This suggests that oxygen mostly remains highly dynamic inside the condensate.Fig. 6Coarse-grained simulations of oxygen molecules in condensates.a Normalized density profiles of the simulated system components across a slab condensate system. b Lengths of visits of oxygen molecules to the bulk region of the condensate. c Radial distribution functions plotted for the distribution of oxygen around different residues of the octapeptide repeat unit.

  To understand the potential interaction between oxygen and the condensate forming proteins, we looked at the distribution of oxygen around each of the residues in the octapeptide repeat unit of the [Q5,8]−20 protein using radial distribution functions (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 19). While there is relatively little change in the residue-specific radial distribution functions, there is a slight indication that oxygen is more prevalent around tyrosine compared to other residues in the octapeptide repeat. The same sequence of preference around the repeat unit residues is also observed for a simulation of a single protein (representing the dilute phase of a condensate system), further indicating that the overall interaction of oxygen with disordered proteins is weak and the condensate does not change the preference for interactions compared to the monomeric state (Supplementary Fig. 20). Biomolecular condensates can concentrate or deplete ions in their interior including the repeat proteins studied here53. High ionic strength is known to reduce oxygen solubility54 and could contribute to the exclusion of oxygen from the dense phase. To assess this effect, we analyzed the ion and oxygen concentrations in the absence of NaCl as well as at two different salt concentrations. In agreement with previous studies, we find that NaCl is concentrated in the dense phase. Oxygen is still partially excluded from the condensate in the absence of NaCl, and increasing concentrations lead to a slight decrease in oxygen partitioning. The absolute change is much smaller than the variation observed experimentally (Supplementary Fig. 21). This suggests that salt partitioning is not the main mechanism of the exclusion of oxygen from condensates.

  Finally, we investigated the hydrophobic sequence effect, also determined experimentally by titration valine residues at position 5 in the octa-repeat. In simulations, the oxygen partitioning into the condensate increases monotonously with increased hydrophobicity (Supplementary Fig. 22). The oxygen model is mainly parameterized based on, e.g., water-octanol partitioning data, so that is the expected behavior of a hydrophobic solute. The experimental data show a non-monotonous trend with an overall larger magnitude of variation. The discrepancy suggests that the underlying cause is not well captured by the current simulation setup yet.


## Discussion
  Recently, theoretical models for partitioning of small molecules into condensates have been established based on experimental measurement of libraries of metabolites and drug-like compounds partitioning into a handful of model condensates16,17,19,20. These datasets allow statistical analysis to identify features that predict partitioning and support the training of machine learning models17,19. These studies disagree on whether the small molecule partitioning is primarily determined by general features of condensates19,20 or by a distinct environment in different condensates17—likely reflecting the modest number of condensates that have been studied.

  Despite these differing perspectives, prior literature converged on hydrophobicity as the strongest predictor of small molecule partitioning. These findings align with a model in which the condensate forms an “effective solvent” of higher hydrophobicity, which is also supported by, e.g., solvatochromic dyes that suggest that the dense phase has a polarity like that of methanol14. The effective solvent model sets a clear expectation for oxygen partitioning: oxygen should preferentially partition into the dense phase, analogous to its behavior in organic solvents36, and the effect should be stronger for more hydrophobic condensates. Instead, we observe the opposite: Oxygen is partially excluded from condensates, with the exclusion being more pronounced in more hydrophobic condensates.

  These results call for an alternative model explaining oxygen partitioning into condensates - and possibly an addendum to the general effective solvent model of metabolite partitioning. First, we excluded a technical origin of our observations, as [O2]dense, [protein]dense, and dense phase polarity are each supported by orthogonal lines of evidence. The observed anti-correlation between [O2]dense and [protein]dense presents a simple explanation: The density of macromolecules within the condensate affects the amount of solvent accessible for small molecule partitioning, suggesting that a denser condensate can accommodate fewer soluble metabolites. However, when the metabolite interacts favorably with the condensate-forming proteins, this effect can be overruled by hydrophobic interactions. In the absence of such strong interactions, simulations suggest that only minimal interactions between oxygen and the scaffold occur, and as a result, the concentration of macromolecules in the dense phase becomes the dominant factor. Recently, imaging methods for measuring the concentration of macromolecules in dense phase concentrations have become available and have revealed considerable variation in dense phase concentrations between different condensates and during condensate aging35. Our results for oxygen suggest a mechanism whereby such changes in concentration can affect biochemical reactions involving metabolites that do not interact strongly with the dense phase.

  The study of oxygen partitioning was only possible due to the development of procedures for measuring oxygen in condensates, principally PLIM. While the PLIM technique has several limitations - such as the assumption of an unchanged quenching constant kq—its findings were consistent with direct electrode measurements. A key limitation of the current approach is the need to measure the fluorescence lifetime (τ) in the condensate both in the presence and absence of oxygen, which precludes the technique from being considered fully robust at this stage. To avoid the effects of long incubation in oxygen free atmosphere, we removed oxygen rapidly with a chemical scavenger, which is convenient but precludes measurement in living cells. In the future, this limitation could be addressed by developing dyes that are more shielded from their chemical environment and thus do not require correction for τ0.

  In conclusion, we have developed tools for studying oxygen partitioning in condensates and discovered a so far unappreciated role for the concentration of macromolecules in the dense phase in oxygen partitioning. This supports the emerging view that condensates are not analogous to simple solvents but require us to account for their constituent macromolecules explicitly.


## Methods

### Protein expression and purification
  Genes encoding octapeptide repeat protein [Q5,8]−20, [Q5,8]−30, [Q5,8]−40, [Q5,8]−60, [(3Q5:V5),Q8]−60, [(Q5:V5),Q8]−60 and [V5,Q8]−60, as well as [S5,8]−25 and its 14 sequence variants, were synthesized and cloned into the pET-15b vector between NdeI and BamHI restriction sites by Genscript (Supplementary Table 1). Plasmids were transformed into chemical competent Escherichia coli BL21(DE3), which were cultured at 37 °C in Terrific Broth supplemented with 100 µg/mL ampicillin and shaken at 180 rpm. Cultures were grown to the stationary phase, after which protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, and cells were incubated for the next 14–16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 g for 30 min.

  Protein purification was performed with minor modifications to a previously reported protocol24. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in Milli-Q water, lysed by probe sonication, and centrifuged (13,000 g, 30 min). The resulting pellet was solubilized in a denaturation buffer (pH 7.4) containing 8 M urea, 20 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4, and 150 mM NaCl, followed by centrifugation and saving the supernatant. Proteins were then purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography under denaturing conditions. For most constructs—including [Q5,8]−X repeats, [S5,8]−25, and its variants, standard Ni-NTA purification was sufficient. However, two positively charged [S5,8]−25 variants, i.e., [GRGNSPYS]−25 and [GRGNSPWS]−25, required additional high-salt washes (3 M NaCl followed by 500 mM NaCl instead of 150 mM) during the Ni-NTA step to reduce non-specific electrostatic interactions with nucleic acids. After Ni-NTA purification, elution fractions containing protein were dialyzed against water with at least six changes of reservoir solution at 6-h intervals. After 3 days of dialysis with repeated exchanges of Milli-Q water, protein condensates formed a film along the dialysis membrane, which was scratched off and recovered. The sticky protein material obtained from each construct was then lyophilized and stored as a powder until use.

  Lyophilized proteins were reconstituted in 20 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4, 150 mM NaCl buffer (pH 7.4) and heated at 80 °C for 10 min to yield a clear solution, which become turbid upon cooling. These clear, heated solutions were briefly centrifuged to remove irreversible aggregates, if present. Protein concentrations were determined by quantified by diluting the samples in urea buffer until optically clear. Absorbance at 280 nm was measured, and concentrations were calculated using molar extinction coefficients obtained from ProtParam, accounting for the respective dilution factors. To minimize interference from nucleic acids, only fractions with a 260/280 ratio between 0.6 and 0.8 were used.


### Characterization of condensates
  Condensates were visualized using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 inverted microscope by drop-casting 40 μL of the turbid sample onto a 10 mm microwell of a 35 mm petri dish (P35G-1.5-10-C from MatTek). To determine the upper critical saturation temperature (UCST), optical density at 600 nm was recorded during programmed cooling from 90 to 15 °C in 5 °C increments using a Labbot UV–visible spectrophotometer (Probation Labs, Sweden AB). This approach exploits the fact that the proteins remain homogeneous at high temperature but associate and become turbid upon cooling, allowing precise UCST measurement. Saturation concentrations (Csat) were quantified by pelleting the dense phase through centrifugation and measuring absorbance at 280 nm in the dilute phase with the same instrument.


### Dense phase concentration determination
  The dense phase concentration of the droplets was quantified using two complementary approaches: sedimentation assay and confocal imaging. In the sedimentation assay, phase-separated samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at the desired temperature, yielding continuous dense and dilute phases. A defined volume of dense phase was carefully extracted using a positive displacement pipette, diluted into denaturation buffer (20 mM K2HPO4/KH2PO4,150 mM NaCl, 8 M urea) to obtain a homogeneous solution, and analyzed by UV-Visible spectroscopy at 280 nm to determine concentration.

  For confocal imaging, protein samples were prepared at the indicated concentrations and drop-cast (50 μL) into the inner wells of PVA-coated MatTek glass-bottom dishes to prevent surface wetting. To visualize droplets, 0.5 μL of a 1 mM fluorescent dye was added, and samples were equilibrated for 15 min at room temperature before imaging. Droplets were imaged using a Zeiss LSM confocal fluorescence microscope equipped with a 60× oil-immersion objective. Z-stacks were acquired from the bottom of the droplets to the last detectable plane, with identical imaging parameters applied across all samples. The total imaged volume was determined from the x–y dimensions of the images and the fixed z-axis depth of the well. Three independent fields of view were acquired per sample. Image stacks were analyzed in Zeiss ZEN software using 3D segmentation. A fluorescence intensity threshold and minimum size cutoff were applied to exclude artifacts. The summed volume of segmented droplets was divided by the total imaging volume to calculate the droplet volume fraction (φ). ### Electrochemical sensing of oxygen
  Oxygen concentrations in the dense and dilute phases were measured using in-house built oxygen microsensor of diameter 10 µm, also available at UNISENSE (Aarhus, Denmark)55. These sensors measure oxygen reduction at a gold cathode and have a very low oxygen consumption between 0.4 and 5 nmol O2/h. The calibrated (2 points in O2-free and air-saturated water) sensor was mounted on a micromanipulator (MM33, UNISENSE) controlled by SensorTrace Suite via a motor controller (MOTCON). A macroscopically phase-separated sample was prepared by centrifuging 100 µM of [Q5,8]−30 solution using a swinging bucket centrifuge at 4000 g, and 20 °C temperature for 10 min. The sensor was inserted into the sample and moved across the phase boundary using the micromanipulator, resulting in measurements of the oxygen concentration at different depths of the solution starting from several micrometers above the phase boundary. Time-dependent measurements were performed at a fixed depth within the dense phase, with the sensor held stationary and oxygen concentrations recorded every 0.5 min for 30 min. These measurements were carried out for all peptide repeats from [Q5,8]−20 to [Q5,8]−60. In addition to the macroscopic phase-separated samples, we also probed individual droplets in the co-existing phases of [Q5,8]−30 under a light microscope. For these experiments, the sample was drop-cast onto a glass depression slide, and the sensor was initially positioned in the dilute phase before being gradually moved toward the settled droplets, i.e., the dense phase of the condensates.


### Phosphorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (PLIM)
  Samples for PLIM experiments were prepared by adding 500 μM of the polar, positively charged phosphorescent dye 500 μM dichlorotris (1,10-phenanthroline) ruthenium (II) hydrate (Sigma Aldrich), which will be referred to as ‘ruthenium dye’ in the later text, to the condensate solutions56. The total protein concentration in the condensate sample used was just above the critical concentration to avoid spinodal decomposition and allow the formation of droplets in the nucleation metastable region. Freshly made condensate samples were used to avoid sample variation due to ageing of the condensates.

  PLIM was performed using a Zeiss Observer Z1 inverted microscope equipped with pco. FLIM lasers and frequency domain based luminescence lifetime camera system57. The frequency modulation was set at 200 kHz based on the known unquenched lifetime of around 1 μs for the ruthenium dye. Starna fluorescent red (UMM/SFR/200X) has a similar excitation wavelength as the ruthenium dye used and was used as a reference with a known lifetime of 5.25 ns. The laser exposure time was adjusted to 1 s to get good intensity signals. The phosphorescent intensity of the ruthenium dye used as an indicator in the sample should be less than the intensity of the reference dye sample used to avoid background noise in the experiment. Oxygen concentrations were measured using a firestring needle-type oxygen sensor with a tip diameter of 430 µm (Pyroscience, GmbH), mentioned as the reference oxygen sensor in the text, in the dilute phase of the samples. The oxygen sensor was calibrated using two known oxygen concentrations, air-saturated water and an oxygen-free solution (alkaline ascorbate solution).

  We used a chemical oxygen scavenger, i.e., sodium sulfite, to create different oxygen concentrations in the samples. We incubated the sample with different concentrations of sodium sulfite and measured the oxygen concentration in the dilute phase of the same sample using the reference oxygen sensor, where 10 mM sodium sulfite resulted in an anoxic environment in the dilute phase. Further, we recorded the phosphorescence lifetime values in the dense phase of the same sample using PLIM to determine the partitioning coefficient from the Stern-Volmer plot. Further, the oxygen concentration in the dense phase (i.e., inside the condensates) was extrapolated from the partitioning coefficient values obtained. PLIM images were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji software): Background noise was reduced using a Gaussian filter (sigma radius of 70), and the filtered image was subtracted from the original raw intensity image to reduce the background noise. The resulting intensity image obtained was redirected to the lifetime channel to measure the phosphorescence lifetime of the dye inside the droplets. We analyzed the droplets by filtering for circularity and diameter and obtained the average lifetime and size for individual droplets. The mean phosphorescence lifetime of the dye in the droplets was plotted against the area of the droplets for both the anoxic and the oxic conditions. Finally, we averaged the phosphorescence lifetime values for different sizes of the droplets and plotted the average phosphorescence lifetime of the dye inside the condensates against the known oxygen concentration in the dilute phase.


### Partitioning of fluorescent dyes into condensates
  Partitioning of the ruthenium dye (used in PLIM experiments) was monitored for all constructs using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. Similarly, three other small-molecule fluorescent dyes—Sypro Orange, Fluorescein, and Rhodamine B—were tested for partitioning into [Q5,8]−20 and [Q5,8]−60 condensates, as well as [Q5,8]−25 and [V5,Q8]−25 condensates. Fluorescein and Rhodamine B were used at 20 µM, while Sypro Orange was applied at a 10× concentration. Samples were prepared on PVA-coated slides to prevent surface wetting effects. Images were captured with a 63× objective at 16-bit depth and a resolution of 1024  × 1024 pixels for enhanced image quality.

  Fluorescence intensities were measured using Fiji software in 10 distinct regions inside the droplets and 10 regions in the dilute phase. Both DIC and fluorescence channels were carefully examined to exclude any underlying condensate signal that could falsely elevate background intensity. Partition coefficients were determined from these ratios, with errors calculated by standard propagation of uncertainty using the respective standard deviations.


### Determination of the polarity of condensates using PRODAN dye
  We measured the polarity of protein-rich droplets by recording the emission spectrum of the solvatochromatic dye PRODAN, a well-established reporter of environmental polarity14,37,38. Condensate samples were drop-cast onto PVA-coated MatTek glass-bottom dishes, and PRODAN was added at a final concentration of 5 μM to facilitate its recruitment into the condensates. Imaging was performed on a confocal fluorescence microscope equipped with a 60× objective lens. Spectral acquisition was carried out in Zeiss λ-scanning mode using excitation at 405 nm, and emission spectra were collected between 400 and 600 nm with a 3 nm spectral window. In some instances, protein aggregates formed in the presence of PRODAN, complicating automated analysis; in such cases, manual analysis was performed by measuring fluorescence intensity within droplets across the emission wavelength range.


### MD simulations
  Topologies and initial conformations for Martini 3 models of the IDPs were generated using Polyply, including updated bonded parameters for disordered proteins46,58. Additionally, non-bonded protein-water interactions were increased by a factor of 1.04 to both improve the global dimensions of disordered proteins and reduce protein-protein interactions to avoid aggregates50,59. A factor of 1.04 was used instead of the generally recommended 1.10, as this degree of increase was found to produce homogeneous systems with no protein phase separation. As described elsewhere, the increase in the interaction strength was achieved using a virtual site built directly on top of the protein backbone bead with a specific interaction to water60. Molecular oxygen was represented by a single TC3 bead, based on water-octanol partitioning along with preliminary studies of membrane partitioning and binding to folded proteins46,61. The parameters for HEME were taken from the work of Chiariello et al.62.

  To simulate a system representative of a protein in the dilute region of a condensate system, a single protein was placed in a 20 nm cubic box, solvated, neutralized, and salt was added to a concentration of 150 mM NaCl. Oxygen beads were further added to the system at a concentration of 150 mM. For simulations of [Q5,8]−20 condensates, a slab configuration was prepared with a condensate of 50 molecules contained within the central 20 nm of a 12 × 12 × 60 nm simulation box. As mentioned before, the system was similarly solvated, neutralized, and ions and oxygen beads were both added to concentrations of 150 mM. Full system compositions are described in supplementary Table 3.

  All simulations were performed using Gromacs 2021.763. Systems were energy minimized, followed by equilibration and production simulations. Systems were equilibrated for 100 ns using a timestep of 10 fs and using the Berendsen barostat and thermostat64. For production runs, a timestep of 20 fs was used for simulations of length 10 μs, using a velocity-rescaling thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat65,66. For single-molecule simulations, the pressure coupling was maintained isotropically at 1 bar, while for slab condensate simulations, semi-isotropic pressure coupling was applied. All simulations were maintained at a temperature of 300 K. Non-bonded simulation parameters otherwise followed the new-rf Martini parameter set, except for the new advised treatment of neighbor lists for the correct calculation of pressure, such that parameters of rlist = 1.35 nm and verlet-buffer-tolerance = −1 were used67,68. All simulations were performed with three replicates with different initial configurations to ensure convergence and consistency in results.

  Simulations were analyzed using a combination of Gromacs tools (gmx density) and custom analysis using Python scripts utilizing MD Analysis69,70. Systems were visualized using the MartiniGlass and VMD71,72. Analysis was performed across the final 2 μs of the simulation time, ensuring that the density profiles of the system were well converged (Supplementary Fig. 17). Radial distribution functions were measured between protein backbone beads and oxygen molecules. To measure contacts between oxygen beads and proteins inside the condensate, a contact was defined as an oxygen being within 6 Å of any protein bead. The length of an individual contact event was defined as the number of consecutive simulation frames where an individual oxygen molecule was in contact with a protein. Similarly, the length of a single condensate visit was defined as the length of time a single oxygen was inside the bulk region of the condensate before leaving again.
