# Genome-wide identification and analysis of YTH gene family and its response to MeJA and salt treatment in Panax ginseng C. A. Meyer


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-08015-2.


## Introduction
  Ginseng (Panax ginseng C. A. Meyer) is one of the most valuable traditional Chinese medicinal materials in China, often referred to as the “King of Herbs,” and it possesses significant medicinal value. Similar to changes in DNA epigenetic modifications, typical components of RNA, such as adenine (A), uracil (U), guanine (G), and cytosine (C), also undergo a series of chemical modifications. These modifications can occur either during transcription or after transcription has been completed, and they persist throughout the entire lifespan of most RNA molecules [1]. RNA modifications are crucial for the characteristics and functions of RNA, affecting stability, translation, splicing, and transport, which in turn increase the functional versatility and diversity of RNA molecules [2]. To date, over 170 different RNA modifications have been identified [3].

  In RNA molecules, adenosine (A) is methylated by methyltransferases, where the hydrogen atom at the sixth nitrogen position (N6) is replaced by a methyl group (CH3). The changes in m6A methylation are primarily regulated by various factors, including m6A methyltransferases, demethylases, and m6A recognition proteins. m6A modifications have been detected in several plants, including Arabidopsis and rice [4]. Additionally, proteins associated with m6A modifications have been progressively identified in plants. In Arabidopsis, the m6A methyltransferase complex consists of mRNA adenosine methyltransferase A (MTA) [5], MTB, FKBP12 interacting protein 37 (FIP37) [6], VIRILIZER (VIR) [7], and HAKAI [8], while m6A demethylation is regulated by ALKBH9B and ALKBH10B [9]. Research indicates that m6A modifications play significant regulatory roles in processes such as fruit ripening, abiotic stress responses, and secondary metabolite synthesis in plants. m6A methylation plays an essential regulatory role in the processes of fruit ripening and quality metabolism in kiwifruit. Overexpression of AcALKBH10 greatly elevates the soluble sugar content in the fruit while markedly reducing the accumulation of acids, whereas silencing AcALKBH10 exhibits the opposite trend [10]. In apples, sorbitol significantly affects the m6A methylation of mRNAs by controlling the expression of two m6A methyltransferases, MdVIR1 and MdVIR2. This RNA modification is essential for the resistance against Alternaria alternata, a pathogen controlled by sorbitol [11]. Additionally, m6A-mediated regulatory mechanisms coordinate the accumulation of terpenoid metabolites and the formation of tea aroma during the sunlight wilting stage of tea leaves [12].

  m6A exerts its regulatory functions primarily by recruiting specific m6A-binding proteins known as “readers”. In mammals, various m6A-binding proteins have been identified, with the most extensively studied being the family of proteins containing the YT521-B homology (YTH) domain [13], which is categorized into YTHDC1, YTHDC2, and YTHDF1-3 subfamilies. These proteins recognize the m6A modification through a conserved YTH domain characterized by a “hydrophobic pocket” formed by two or three aromatic residues necessary for binding to the m6A site [14]. YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, and YTHDC2 are all cytoplasmic m6A readers that function by binding to mature mRNAs containing m6A. In contrast, YTHDC1 is localized in the nucleus and selectively binds pre-mRNA containing m6A [15]. The m6A readers play a broad regulatory role in RNA transcription-related processes, including alternative splicing, mRNA stability, and translation. Thirteen YTH proteins have been identified in Arabidopsis, which are classified into two evolutionary clades: YTHDF and YTHDC [16]. Eleven genes (AtECT1-11) belong to the YTHDF clade, while AtCPSF30 and AtECT12 (AtDC1) belong to the YTHDC clade [17]. In recent years, members of the YTH gene family have been successively identified in plants such as rice [18], tomato [19], and wheat [20] through bioinformatics approaches. However, The YTH proteins in ginseng have not yet been identified. The functional role of m6A reader YTH proteins in response to hormone and abiotic stress in P. ginseng has not yet been reported.

  In this study, a total of 18 PgYTH genes were identified in ginseng and classified into four subgroups, with detailed analyses of their chromosomal distribution and collinearity, gene structures, cis-acting elements, and protein motifs. We also examined the expression profiles of these PgYTH genes. Conserved domain analysis, multiple sequence alignment, and phase separation prediction of PgYTH proteins were subsequently performed. We also investigated the responses of the PgYTH genes to MeJA (Methyl jasmonate) and salt treatments using qRT-PCR. These findings provide a foundation for further investigation into the roles of YTH genes in response to hormone and abiotic stress.


## Methods

### Identification and characterization of physiochemical properties of YTH genes in ginseng
  The gene annotation and telomere-to-telomere reference genome files of ginseng from Jilin Province, China, were retrieved based on the data availability in the previous study [21]. Protein sequences of YTH genes from Arabidopsis and rice were sourced from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) [22] and Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/index.shtml), respectively. Candidate YTH gene sequences were aligned against the protein sequences of ginseng using the blastp algorithm. The presence of the YTH domain in the candidate genes was verified using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/, accessed on January 6th, 2025) [23]. Confirmation of the identified YTH genes was further conducted using the Hmmsearch function [24]. The physicochemical properties, including relative molecular mass (MW) and theoretical isoelectric point (PI), were calculated utilizing the ExPASy ProtParam tool (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/, accessed on Febrary 19th, 2025) [25]. Additionally, the chromosomal locations of the YTH genes were extracted from the annotation files and visualized using TBtools v2.357 (a Toolkit for Biologists integrating various biological data-handling tools) [26].


### Evolutionary, gene structure, and conserved motif analysis of PgYTHs
  The identified YTH protein sequences from Jilin ginseng, Arabidopsis, and rice were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA-X. Phylogenetic tree construction was performed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA-X, employing the Poisson model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and enhanced using Evolview v2 (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview-v2/, accessed on March 7th, 2025) [27]. Collinearity analysis of YTH genes in ginseng was conducted using TBtools. Conserved motifs within the ginseng YTH protein sequences were identified and analyzed using the MEME program Version 5.5.8 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/, accessed on March 15th, 2025) [28]. The gene structures were visually displayed using TBtools v2.357.


### Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis of PgYTHs
  Ginseng reference genome annotation files from Jilin Province, China, were used to determine the chromosomal distribution of YTH genes [21]. The chromosomal distribution of ginseng YTH genes was drafted from top to bottom by TBtools v2.357 according to gene positions in the genome annotation.


### Regulatory elements analysis of PgYTHs
  The 2000 bp region upstream of the coding sequence (CDS) of YTH genes was extracted with TBtools v2.357 and designated as the promoter region. Putative cis-regulatory elements within these promoter regions were identified using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/, accessed on April 6th, 2025) [29]. The distribution and organization of these elements were then visualized with TBtools v2.357.


### Structure construction and liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) prediction
  ESPrint3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi-bin/ESPript.cgi, accessed on May 1st, 2025) was used to analyze the protein domain [30]. The 3D structure of PgYTH proteins was constructed using AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.com/, accessed on May 6th, 2025) with the default parameters [31]. The prion-like domain (PrLD) and intrinsically disordered region (IDR) associated with LLPS in PgYTH proteins were predicted using the Prion-like Amino Acid Composition (PLAAC) tool (http://plaac.wi.mit.edu/, accessed on May 10th, 2025) [32], which employs a hidden Markov model (HMM) algorithm.


### Subcellular localization of PgYTH12
  The full-length coding sequence of PgYTH12 was amplified using specific primers PgYTH12-GFP-F (5’-ACTATTTACAATTACGGATCatggctaccgttgctcctcgg-3’) and PgYTH12-GFP-R (5’-TCCTCGCCCTTGCTCACCATagatcctcctccagatcctcctc-3’), and subsequently cloned into the vector pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-3xflag-ccdB-egfp. The SPER gene was fused to mKate to construct the SPER-mKate fusion protein. The resulting recombinant plasmid, pBWA(V)H2STMVΩ-PgYTH12-GFP and SPER-mKate were co-injected into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, which was then used to infiltrate fully expanded leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. Expression of GFP in the infiltrated cells was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2-ER, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with argon laser excitation at 488 nm.


### Expression profiles analysis of PgYTHs
  To investigate the expression patterns of YTH genes in ginseng, RNA-Seq datasets were retrieved from NCBI (accession number PRJNA302556 and PRJNA266907). These datasets include samples from 14 different tissues of 4-year-old ginseng, ginseng roots at four different growth stages, roots of 42 different cultivated varieties of 4-year-old ginseng (Table S1), and four time points of MeJA treatment were collected from Jilin, China [33]. RNA-seq data under various stress treatments were acquired from the P. ginseng Genome Database (http://ginsengdb.snu.ac.kr/, accessed on October 12th, 2025) [34]. Transcript assembly and expression quantification were conducted through a pipeline involving Hisat2 v2.2.1, StringTie v2.2.3 [35], Kallisto v0.28.2 [36]. We explored the correlations between the PgYTH genes and ginsenoside biosynthesis-related genes in ginseng through correlation analysis. The correlation analysis was visualized using Origin2021, with the significance level judged by the P-value (P < 0.05).


### The PgYTH genes response under MeJA treatment in ginseng
  To explore the potential involvement of the PgYTH gene family in hormone response, ginseng adventitious roots were treated with MeJA. The adventitious roots (1 g) of ginseng were inoculated in 250 mL triangular flasks containing 150 mL of liquid B5 medium and incubated in a shaker at 22℃, 110 rpm for 21 days. On the 22nd day, 200 mM MeJA was added to the culture flasks, and this time point was designated as 0 h of treatment. Samples were collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after treatment, with three biological replicates for each time point. The 0 h samples served as the untreated control group. All collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for further analysis. Adventitious roots subjected to MeJA treatment were induced from Jilin ginseng plants (variety named “Fuxing No. 2”) originating in Jilin Province, China. All ginseng plant materials were stored in Medical Laboratory Testing Technology and Analytical Laboratory, Beihua University.


### The PgYTH genes response under salt treatment in ginseng
  To investigate the potential role of the PgYTH gene family in salt stress response, ginseng adventitious roots were subjected to NaCl treatment. Adventitious roots measuring 1 cm in length were cultured on B5 medium supplemented with varying concentrations of NaCl (0 mM, 70 mM, 80 mM, 90 mM, and 100 mM) at 25 °C for 30 days. After treatment, the samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at -80 °C for subsequent gene expression analysis. The adventitious roots used for salt treatment were induced from Jilin ginseng plants (variety named “Fuxing No. 2”) sourced from Jilin Province, China. All ginseng plant materials were stored in Medical Laboratory Testing Technology and Analytical Laboratory, Beihua University.


### Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis
  Total RNA from Jilin ginseng (variety named “Fuxing No. 2”) was extracted using the TRIzol method. Subsequently, the Super RT III Kit (Biosharp Biotech, Labgic Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to reverse-transcribe the RNA into cDNA. Primers for qPCR were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), with their sequences provided in Supplementary Table S2. The β-actin gene served as the internal reference. qPCR assays were conducted using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) kit (Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Each experiment was performed in triplicate for every sample group, and the relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [37].


## Result

### Genome-wide identification of ginseng YTH genes
  In total, we identified 18 YTH genes in P. ginseng (Table 1). The complete information for these genes, including the gene ID, number of amino acids (aa), molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), location and subcellular localization were analyzed. The lengths of the corresponding YTH proteins ranged from 436 aa (PgYTH7) to 720 aa (PgYTH11). The pI exhibited variability, spanning from 5.13 (PgYTH6) to 9.23 (PgYTH5). Additionally, the MW of these proteins was found to range approximately from 49.4 kDa (PgYTH7) to 79.8 kDa (PgYTH1). The subcellular localization prediction results indicated that 4 (PgYTH1, PgYTH2, PgYTH7, and PgYTH16) out of the 18 proteins were localized in the nucleus, while the remaining 14 were exclusively localized in the cytoplasm.

### Phylogenetic analysis of YTH genes
  A phylogenetic tree was constructed with YTH protein sequences from ginseng, Arabidopsis, and rice to analyze the evolutionary relationships among YTH genes (Fig. 1). In our study, the YTH proteins of ginseng were classified into two families, DC and DF, based on previous studies in other plant species. The DC group contains 4 members: PgYTH1, PgYTH2, PgYTH7, and PgYTH16. The DF group was further divided into 3 subfamilies (DFA, DFB, and DFC). PgYTH9-12 were classified into subfamily DFA. PgYTH3/6/8/13 were classified into subfamily DFB. PgYTH4/5/14/15/17/18 were classified into subfamily DFC (Fig. 1).

### Motifs, conserved domain and gene structure analysis
  An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed to investigate the evolutionary patterns and classification of YTH genes in ginseng. We examined the conserved motifs of the YTH proteins and the gene structures of the identified YTH genes. The MEME program was employed to analyze the organization of conserved motifs in ginseng YTH proteins, resulting in the detection of a total of 10 distinct motifs. These genes contained 4 to 9 motifs. Notably, motifs 1, 2, 3, and 5 were present in YTH proteins. PgYTH9-12 contain all motifs except motif10. PgYTH3/6/8/13 contain all motifs except motif8. YTH gene family members that cluster within a clade share similar motif compositions (Fig. 2A).

  Fig. 2Phylogenetic tree, protein motifs, conserved domain, and gene structure of YTH genes in ginseng. A Phylogenetic tree and motif distribution of YTH proteins in ginseng. Each colored box represents a specific motif. B Conserved domain in ginseng YTH proteins. C Exon-intron structure of ginseng YTH genes. Green boxes denote untranslated regions (UTRs), yellow boxes indicate exons, and black lines represent introns

  The YTH domains in the candidate genes were verified through analysis in the NCBI CDD. The YTH domains of most YTH proteins are located near the 3’ end. The YTH domains of PgYTH2 and PgYTH7 are located near the 5’ end. The YTH domains are in the middle of PgYTH1 and PgYTH16 (Fig. 2B).

  To evaluate the consistency of exon-intron patterns among YTH genes, we performed a comprehensive gene structure analysis. The results revealed that the number of exons in the YTH gene family of ginseng ranged from 7 to 10. The analysis of exon and intron arrangements offered valuable insights into the evolutionary relationships among different members of this gene family. Notably, genes that are closely related displayed a higher degree of structural similarity, with variations primarily observed in the lengths of their introns and exons (Fig. 2C).


### Chromosome distribution and collinearity analysis of YTH genes
  Considering that ginseng is an allotetraploid plant, the previous study categorized the ginseng chromosomes into two subgenomes (subgenome A and B) [21]. The BLASTN search was conducted to determine the chromosomal distribution of YTH genes in ginseng. The PgYTH genes were approximately evenly distributed across both subgenomes of ginseng. Nine PgYTH genes were located on subgenome A (Chr03A-07 A, Chr10A, and Chr12A). Nine PgYTH genes were located on subgenome B (Chr03B-07B, and Chr10B). Three YTH genes (PgYTH11-13) were anchored on chromosome Chr04B. With the exception of four genes (PgYTH9-12), the chromosomal locations of the remaining genes were largely conserved between subgenome A and subgenome B (Fig. 3A). These results implied that divergent evolutionary pressures have likely shaped the evolution of ginseng.

  Fig. 3Chromosomal locations and collinearity analysis of the PgYTH genes. A Chromosomal distribution of PgYTH genes across both A and B subgenomes. B Collinearity analysis of ginseng YTH gene family members in ginseng chromosomes

  To further investigate the phylogenetic mechanisms of the ginseng YTH gene family, we performed an intraspecific collinearity analysis of PgYTH genes. A total of 19 pairs of colinear PgYTH genes were identified (Fig. 3B). To explore the potential selective pressure on PgYTHs, the Ka (non-synonymous) and Ks (synonymous) substitution rates, along with the Ka/Ks ratios, were analyzed. The Ka values for the PgYTH family genes ranged from 0.005 to 0.150, the Ks values for the PgYTH family genes ranged from 0.015 to 0.401, and the Ka/Ks ratios varied between 0.206 and 0.716 (Table S3). The results revealed that PgYTH has mainly undergone purifying selection throughout its evolution, as all PgYTH gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks values below 1.


### Cis-regulatory element analysis of YTH genes
  Understanding the cis-regulatory elements that control YTH gene expression is crucial for elucidating their regulatory mechanisms and potential functions in ginseng. To this end, we extracted the 2000 bp region upstream of the start codon of each ginseng YTH gene as its promoter region for further analysis. A total of 469 cis-regulatory elements, representing 17 different types, were identified in the promoter regions of PgYTH genes (Fig. 4A). Among them, light-responsive elements were the most abundant, accounting for nearly half of the total number (224) (Table S4). Stress-responsive regulatory elements were also identified, including those associated with anaerobic environments, MBS (MYB-binding site involved in drought-inducibility), low temperature-responsive elements (LTREs), wound response, mixed stress conditions, and anoxia (Fig. 4B). A total of 85 environmental stress-related cis-acting elements were identified. Over 70% of these elements were anaerobic induction elements and drought-responsive MBS, highlighting their dominant roles in stress response (Fig. 4B). In addition, several hormone regulatory sites were identified, including those responsive to MeJA, abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellin (GA), auxin (IAA), and salicylic acid (SA). The distribution of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions suggests that PgYTH genes may be primarily regulated by MeJA and ABA (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, cis-acting elements related to zein, meristem, endosperm, palisade mesophyll cells and circadian were also detected.

### The structure of PgYTH proteins
  The m6A mononucleotide is positioned within an aromatic cage formed by three aromatic amino acids (Trp486, Trp432, and Trp491) in HsYTHDF2. The structure is highly conserved across different YTHDF members and across species, serving as the core structural basis for m6A recognition [38]. To identify the core m⁶A recognition structure of PgYTH protein members, a multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega. Two of the three residues forming the aromatic cage in HsYTHDF2, Trp432 and Trp486, are conserved in PgYTH proteins. The third residue of the aromatic cage (Trp491 in HsYTHDF2) is substituted by Tyr in PgYTH1 and PgYTH16 (Fig. 5A). The results showed that PgYTH proteins share conserved tryptophan residues with human YTHDF proteins.

The conserved amino acids are highlighted as follows: similar residues appear as red letters, while identical residues are shown within red boxes. Positions indicated by red triangles represent conserved aromatic cages (WWW/WWY) in the PgYTH proteins. B Predicted three-dimensional structures of representative PgYTH proteins (PgYTH3, PgYTH8, PgYTH9, PgYTH10, PgYTH11, PgYTH12). The β-sheet and α-helix in the PgYTH protein’s 3D structure are showed by yellow and blue colors, respectively

  Three-dimensional structures of representative PgYTH proteins were also predicted. Ginseng PgYTH proteins exhibited the canonical YTH domain architecture. The three-dimensional (3D) structure is predominantly composed of six β-strands that assemble into a β-barrel, constituting the central hydrophobic core of the protein, and multiple α-helices (typically more than three) are packed against this β-barrel, further stabilizing the overall fold (Fig. 5B).


### Liquid–liquid separation of PgYTH proteins
  The PrLD domain has been demonstrated to facilitate the liquid–liquid phase separation of proteins. The online tool PLAAC has proven to be an effective method for identifying PrLDs in proteins. In this study, we utilized PLAAC to predict prion domains within PgYTH proteins to assess their potential for phase transition. PgYTH proteins from different subfamilies exhibited varying numbers of highly disordered PrLDs. The DFA subfamily proteins (PgYTH9-12) included three PrLDs, indicating a strong propensity for phase separation. The DFB subfamily proteins contained one to two PrLDs. Within the DFC subfamily, four PgYTH proteins (PgYTH5, PgYTH14, PgYTH17, and PgYTH18) had one PrLD each, while two proteins (PgYTH14 and PgYTH15) lacked PrLDs. In the DC subfamily, two PgYTH proteins (PgYTH1 and PgYTH16) contained three PrLDs, and two proteins (PgYTH2 and PgYTH7) had none (Fig. 6). This indicated that PgYTH proteins, especially those belonging to the DFA subfamily, might undergo phase separation similarly to YTHDF1–YTHDF3 proteins of human.

### Expression patterns of ginseng YTH genes
  Gene expression patterns are strongly associated with gene functions. To better understand the expression patterns of PgYTH genes in ginseng, we obtained expression data from publicly available databasefor PgYTH transcripts across 42 farm cultivars (S1–S42) (Table S1), 14 different tissues, ginseng roots of four varying ages (5, 12, 18, and 25 years), under abiotic stress treatments (salt, drought, and cold), and under MeJA treatment at four time points (0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h). The expression levels of PgYTH9-12 in ginseng roots of four different ages were significantly higher than that of other PgYTH genes, with PgYTH12 exhibiting the highest expression level (Fig. 7A).

B Expression patterns of PgYTHs in 14 different tissues of 4-year-old ginseng. C Expression of PgYTH genes in different stress conditions (salt, drought, cold). D Expression patterns of PgYTHs in in the 42 farm cultivars of 4-year-old ginseng roots. E Expression of PgYTH genes at four time points with MeJA treatment (0, 6, 12, 24 h). F Correlation analysis of PgYTH genes and ginsenoside biosynthesis pathway genes. High correlation is indicated by red, while low correlation is showed by blue. “*” indicates P ≤ 0.05

  The PgYTH genes exhibited dynamic and tissue-specific expression patterns throughout these organs, reflecting their diverse functional roles in ginseng development and metabolism. PgYTH1-8 and PgYTH13-18 exhibited generally low expression levels across all ginseng tissues. Compared to other PgYTH genes, PgYTH12 exhibited higher expression levels across various tissues, indicating its potentially broader or more significant functional role in ginseng. Moreover, PgYTH12 showed the highest expression level in the main root cortex (Fig. 7B). Under different abiotic stress treatments (salt, drought, and cold), the expression levels of most PgYTH genes (PgYTH1–9 and PgYTH13–18) remained nearly unchanged. PgYTH10 and PgYTH11 exhibited increased transcript levels under cold or drought treatment. The expression PgYTH12 was downregulated under drought treatment but upregulated under cold or salt treatment (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, PgYTH12 exhibited the highest expression level across 42 farm cultivars (Fig. 7D), indicating that this gene may play an important role in the root of ginseng.

  The expression level of PgYTH9-12 continuously decreased with prolonged MeJA treatment duration (6 h, 12 h, and 24 h), while expression levels of the other PgYTH genes remained very low at all time points (Fig. 7E). Co-expression analysis was conducted between PgYTH family genes and genes involved in the ginsenoside biosynthesis pathway to explore their potential functional associations. The results showed that the expression levels of some PgYTH genes were significantly negatively correlated with the expression levels of ginsenoside biosynthesis pathway genes in ginseng, implying that PgYTH genes may modulate the biosynthetic process via inhibitory mechanisms (Fig. 7F).


### Expression analysis of PgYTH genes under MeJA treatment in ginseng
  MeJA was found to be involved in the regulation of ginsenoside biosynthesis. qRT-PCR was conducted to determine the expression profiles of the PgYTH genes under MeJA treatment. The qRT-PCR analysis showed a predominant downregulation trend across most members of this gene family. Among the 18 PgYTH genes examined, the expression levels of PgYTH2, PgYTH14, PgYTH15, and PgYTH16 showed no significant difference compared with the control after MeJA treatment (Fig. 8), indicating that these genes may be governed by different regulatory mechanisms or have functions that are less affected by MeJA signaling. The expression levels of PgYTH3, PgYTH4, PgYTH10, PgYTH12, PgYTH13, PgYTH17, and PgYTH18 were significantly decreased compared to the control at all time points post-treatment, suggesting that they may serve as key components in the MeJA signaling pathway.

### Expression analysis of PgYTH genes under salt treatment in ginseng
  Ginseng growing in natural environments is influenced by multiple stress factors, and gene expression patterns in these specific conditions are commonly utilized to study gene functions. qRT-PCR was used to verify the response of PgYTH genes to salt stress. Ginseng adventitious roots were treated with different concentrations of salt (0, 70, 80, 90, and 100 mM NaCl) in B5 medium for 30 days. As the salt concentration increased, the growth of ginseng adventitious roots was increasingly inhibited (Fig. 9A). Except for the expression levels of PgYTH4 and PgYTH8–12, the expression levels of the other PgYTH genes showed no significant differences compared with the control after salt treatment (Fig. 9B). All PgYTH genes within the DFA subfamily (PgYTH9-12) demonstrated a significant upregulation trend under salt treatment, suggesting that these genes may have crucial roles in the molecular mechanisms governing salt stress tolerance. In addition, the PgYTH8 gene, which belongs to the DFB subfamily, also exhibited a significant upregulation trend under salt treatment. These results suggested that while many PgYTH genes remain stable under salt stress, a targeted subset was actively engaged, highlighting functional differentiation within the PgYTH gene family in adapting to salt stress.

## Discussion
  m6A RNA methylation is the most prevalent reversible modification found on eukaryotic RNAs and is one of the earliest reported chemical modifications, with its abundance varying across developmental stages and different tissues [39]. However, the roles of m6A and YTH reader proteins in medical plants remain largely unknown. The recognition of m6A-modified RNAs is largely mediated by reader proteins containing the YTH domain, which features an aromatic cage that specifically binds to the GG(m6A)C sequence [40]. In this study, we identified 18 PgYTH genes were discovered across two subgenomes of ginseng, within a total genome size of 3.45 Gb. This tally contrasts with prior findings in other species: Arabidopsis harboring 13 YTH genes in approximately 135–155 Mb [41], rice with 12 in about 385.7 Mb [18], apple containing 26 in around 631.76 Mb [42], Camellia chekiangoleosa possessing 10 in roughly 2.73 Gb [43], alfalfa having 53 in ~ 816 Mb [44], and Ginkgo biloba encoding 10 in ~ 10.6 Gb [45]. The observed PgYTH genes in ginseng in our study add to the growing body of evidence that YTH family size is highly lineage-specific and not solely dictated by genome size. The species-specific differences in YTH gene numbers imply that functional diversification of YTH readers may follow distinct evolutionary trajectories.

  Previous studies have shown that the YTH gene family can be classified into four subfamilies: YTHDC, YTHDFA, YTHDFB, and YTHDFC [46]. In this study, the ginseng YTH gene family was almost evenly distributed among the four categories, with 4 genes each in YTHDC, YTHDFA, and YTHDFB, and 6 genes in YTHDFC. It has been demonstrated that YTH domain proteins recognize m6A through a conserved mechanism involving an aromatic cage that interacts specifically with the methyl group of m6A. The YTHDF proteins possess a characteristic “WWW cage”, whereas YTHDC proteins feature a “WWL cage” in humans [47]. In plant YTHDC proteins, a “WWY cage” has been identified [20]. Among the 19 PgYTH proteins, only PgYTH1 and PgYTH16, which belong to the YTHDC subfamily, contain the WWY-type aromatic cage. All the other PgYTH proteins possess the conserved WWW-type aromatic cage necessary for m6A recognition. Besides the YTH domain, YTH proteins also contain PrLDs, which serve as the primary driver for initiating LLPS in RNA-binding proteins [48, 49]. Among the 18 PgYTH proteins, only four were predicted to lack PrLDs as determined by the online software PLAAC. Of these, two belong to the YTHDC subfamily (PgYTH2 and PgYTH7), while the other two are members of the DFC subfamily (PgYTH4 and PgYTH15). All members of the YTHDFA subfamily (PgYTH9-12) contain three PrLD domains, which are clustered in the central region near the N-terminus of the protein, suggesting that this subfamily may form more stable or functionally specific phase-separated condensates within cells. Two members of the YTHDC subfamily (PgYTH1 and PgYTH16) also contain three PrLDs. However, these three PrLDs are located separately at the N-terminal, middle, and C-terminal regions of the proteins. The remaining PgYTH proteins contain one to two PrLDs. Human and many plant YTH proteins localize predominantly to the cytoplasm or nucleus. Some m6A readers have been implicated in the organization of phase-separated compartments associated with intracellular membranes. In this study, PgYTH12-GFP localizes to the ER, points to a potentially unique role for this ginseng YTHDF-class member in ER-linked mRNA regulation, possibly related to translation control, mRNA quality surveillance, or stress adaptation pathways. Such ER localization has not been widely reported among YTHDF proteins in plants, indicating that PgYTH12 could participate in novel m6A-mediated regulatory processes integral to the ginseng stress response system. Further biochemical and functional assays, particularly focused on PrLD-driven phase separation and subcellular compartmentalization, will be essential to clarify how individual PgYTH proteins in regulating developmental plasticity and stress adaptation in ginseng.

  In Arabidopsis, AtECT2, AtECT3, and AtECT4 exhibit significant redundant regulatory functions in leaf growth, organogenesis, and trichome branching. Additionally, AtECT2/ECT3/ECT4 collaborate by directly recruiting poly(A) binding proteins, which enhances the stability of target genes and regulates ABA responses [50]. In recent years, bioinformatics approaches have identified members of the YTH family in rice, tomato, apple, and other plants. OsYTH3, OsYTH5, and OsYTH10 are m6A readers in rice that are highly expressed across various tissues, showing significant protein homology with AtECT2. The three genes OsYTH3/5/10 regulate rice plant height through functional redundancy. Further transcriptomic analysis indicates that the reduced height of yth03/05/10 triple mutant plants may result from disrupted pathways for terpenoid and brassinosteroid synthesis [18]. This highlights a shared mechanism by which YTH proteins regulate growth-related processes via m6A-mediated RNA recognition. In tomato, SlYTH2 is highly homologous to Arabidopsis AtECT2, AtECT3, and AtECT4. SlYTH2 possesses the ability to recognize m6A and promotes the formation of protein-RNA condensates through liquid-liquid phase separation, thus enabling precise regulation of molecules related to fruit aroma quality [51]. In our study, PgYTH9-12 were classified into subfamily DFA together with AtECT2/3/4 and OsYTH3/5/10. The PgYTH9-12 proteins possess three PrLDs, suggesting a pronounced tendency toward phase separation. This conserved classification indicates that PgYTH9-12 could contribute to key developmental and physiological processes, possibly including growth regulation and secondary metabolite production. Further genetic and biochemical analyses of PgYTH9–12, particularly loss-of-function and RNA target profiling, will be crucial to establish whether these YTHDFA-type readers execute conserved roles in growth regulation of ginseng. Additionally, whether PgYTH9-12 belonging to the YTHDFA subfamily exhibit functional redundancy requires further investigation.

  Gene expression patterns are closely related to gene functions. In C. chekiangoleosa, Solanum lycopersicum, and strawberry, the YTH genes, belonging to the YTHDFA subfamily, show significantly higher expression compared to other genes in different tissues [43, 52, 53]. In Salvia miltiorrhiza, the SmYTH3 gene, which is classified into the YTHDFA subfamily, exhibited significantly higher expression levels in roots compared to other tissues [54]. In this study, PgYTH9–12 showed significantly higher expression in roots of varying ages. Among the PgYTH family, PgYTH12, stood out due to its consistently elevated expression across diverse tissues, the main root cortex, and a wide range of farm cultivars. In response to abiotic stresses, PgYTH12 expression decreased under drought but increased under cold and salt treatments. Our findings emphasize the conserved and critical roles of YTHDFA subfamily genes in plant roots, especially PgYTH12, are key players in orchestrating transcriptomic responses necessary for resource allocation between growth and defense. Future functional characterization to identify specific mRNA targets and interacting proteins will be necessary to unravel the precise molecular pathways governed by PgYTH proteins in ginseng.

  Ginsenoside, a secondary metabolite of ginseng, is a triterpenoid saponin exhibit significant therapeutic effects in areas such as anti-tumor activity, anti-aging, immune enhancement, and neuroprotection [55]. Recent studies have shown that saponin synthesis is not only directly regulated by transcription factors such as MYB and WRKY [56, 57], but also influenced by epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation and histone methylation, ) that dynamically regulate chromatin structure and gene transcription activity. These modifications may serve as critical regulatory nodes in the synthesis of secondary metabolites [58]. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms underlying ginsenoside biosynthesis is of great significance for improving the medicinal value of ginseng. MeJA is widely used as an inducer in research on the ginsenoside synthesis pathway because of its effective and consistent induction properties [59–61]. The transcriptome data demonstrating that PgYTH9–12 genes are gradually downregulated under MeJA treatment, combined with their significant negative correlation with ginsenoside biosynthesis pathway genes, strongly suggests a fine-tuned hormonal regulatory mechanism in ginseng mediated by jasmonate signaling. Cis-acting element analysis revealed that the promoters of PgYTH10-12 contain MeJA-responsiveness elements (CGTCA and TGACG). However, no MeJA-responsive elements were predicted in the promoter region of PgYTH9 (Table S4), indicating​ differential modes of regulation within this gene family. Correspondingly, qPCR validation of PgYTH9–12 downregulation reinforces their responsiveness to MeJA. This finding strongly indicates that members of the PgYTH gene family are subject to fine hormonal regulation by MeJA. The pattern of downregulation of PgYTH genes in response to MeJA mirrors observations in S. miltiorrhiza, where most SmYTH1–SmYTH6 genes exhibited high sensitivity to MeJA, with predominantly decreased expression. In S. miltiorrhiza, this downregulation correlated with increased accumulation of phenolic acids, bioactive compounds of medicinal significance [54]. MeJA-mediated repression of certain PgYTH genes might release post-transcriptional control over ginsenoside biosynthesis genes, facilitating increased metabolite accumulation as part of the plant’s adaptive defense mechanism. Further studies are needed to investigate the molecular links between PgYTH proteins, MeJA signaling, and ginsenoside biosynthesis.

  The YTH domain-containing gene family has emerged as a key regulator in plant responses to various abiotic stresses, particularly salt and drought stress, across a diverse range of plant species, including Arabidopsis, apple, Setaria italica, cotton, and C. chekiangoleosa. AtECT8 functions as a sensor for salt stress, promoting mRNA degradation that is essential for preserving transcriptome balance and improving salt stress tolerance in Arabidopsis [62]. The absence of AtECT8 leads to impaired sequestration of m6A -modified PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE 1-LIKE 7 (PYL7) within stress granules, allowing increased translation of PYL7 transcripts. This results in the overactivation of ABA-responsive genes and the development of ABA-hypersensitive traits, such as enhanced drought tolerance [63]. In Arabidopsis, AtECT12 was identified to modulate the stability of m6A-marked RNA transcripts, thereby promoting Arabidopsis tolerance to salt or drought stress [64]. The m6A reader SiYTH1 improves drought tolerance in S. italica by modulating the stability of messenger RNAs associated with stomatal closure and reactive oxygen species scavenging [65]. Functional validation demonstrated that silencing GhYTH8 decreased drought tolerance in the upland cotton TM-1 line [66]. C. chekiangoleosa CchYTH genes expression levels vary in response to drought stress [43]. Consistent with these findings, our qPCR analysis verified that salt stress significantly induces the expression of PgYTH9-12 in ginseng, correlating well with transcriptomic data and reinforcing the essential role of YTH genes in stress tolerance mechanisms in plants. Cis-acting element analysis revealed that the promoters of PgYTH9-12 contain identical salt stress response elements (CCATCTTTTT) (Table S4), suggesting conserved regulatory control under salt stress. These results suggest that PgYTH9-12 are key components of the m6A-mediated regulatory network that modulates gene expression in response to environmental stresses, potentially enhancing ginseng’s ability to cope with salt stress. PgYTH8 also exhibited significant transcriptional induction under salt treatment, despite lacking identifiable salt-responsive cis-elements in its promoter. This inconsistency suggests that PgYTH8 may be regulated through alternative mechanisms, such as trans-acting factors, post-transcriptional regulation, or epigenetic modifications. However, it remains uncertain whether PgYTH8–12 directly contribute to salt tolerance or simply respond secondarily to salt-induced signaling. The specific functions and regulatory mechanisms of PgYTH genes during ginseng’s response to salt stress require further investigation.


## Conclusion
  In this study, a comprehensive analysis of the ginseng YTH gene family was conducted, encompassing identification, phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, chromosomal distributions, expression patterns, protein structures and PrLDs. Expression profiling revealed diverse spatiotemporal and stress-responsive patterns among PgYTH genes, with PgYTH12 showing notably high and tissue-specific expression. The downregulation of most PgYTH genes under MeJA treatment and the upregulation of PgYTH8–12 under salt stress suggest their potential involvement in ginsenoside biosynthesis and abiotic stress adaptation. This research offers a foundation for future metabolic regulation of the ginsenoside biosynthesis pathway. Furthermore, this research sheds new light on the intricate mechanism of m6A reader-mediated salt tolerance and lays a foundation for enhancing salt tolerance in ginseng breeding.
