# Advantages and Limitations of AlphaFold in Structural Biology: Insights from Recent Studies

## Introduction

### Cryo-electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM)
  Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a powerful technique for determining the three-dimensional (3D) structures of purified biological macromolecules, including proteins and viral particles [1]. In this method, samples are rapidly frozen to preserve their native conformation and are then imaged by a transmission electron microscope using an electron beam. Multiple two-dimensional (2D) projection images are collected and computationally reconstructed into a high-resolution 3D model [2].

  Unlike X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM does not require crystallization, making it particularly advantageous for studying large, dynamic, or flexible proteins that are difficult to analyze using other structural techniques [3–5]. While cryo-EM can achieve near-atomic resolution under favorable conditions, it requires highly purified samples and is limited in capturing interactions within the native cellular environment.


### Cryo-electron Tomography (Cryo-ET)
  Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) extends cryo-EM to enable 3D visualization of biological specimens—from purified macromolecules to intact cells—in a near-native frozen-hydrated state. Samples are vitrified to preserve native architecture, and a series of 2D projections is acquired at incremental tilt angles. These projections are reconstructed into a 3D tomogram that reveals the spatial organization of cellular components at nanometer resolution.

  Unlike single-particle cryo-EM, which requires purified samples, cryo-ET can visualize macromolecules in situ, providing insights into molecular interactions within their physiological context. Nevertheless, cryo-ET faces challenges such as radiation damage, sample thickness, and the “missing wedge problem” (loss of information from limited tilt angles). Advanced computational techniques such as subtomogram averaging—where multiple repeating structures are aligned and averaged—help overcome these issues, achieving near-atomic resolution and bridging cellular and molecular scales [6, 7]. This capability makes cryo-ET indispensable for exploring viral infection mechanisms and organelle dynamics within native environments.


### AlphaFold
  AlphaFold, developed by DeepMind, is a deep learning framework that predicts protein structures directly from amino acid sequences [8]. By leveraging large databases of known structures, multi-sequence alignments, and transformer-based attention networks, AlphaFold achieves near-experimental accuracy and has transformed structural biology. Its iterative refinement process integrates geometric and physical constraints, as demonstrated in CASP14, where it reached sub-ångström precision for many targets.

  Recent studies have shown AlphaFold’s value across biological systems—for example, modeling SARS-CoV-2 spike and nucleocapsid proteins, aiding cryo-EM fitting of bacterial ribosomal complexes, and supporting mutational analysis of human GPCRs [9–11]. These applications underscore how AlphaFold accelerates hypothesis generation and interpretation of experimental data.

  However, AlphaFold also exhibits intrinsic limitations that are central to this review. It primarily predicts static conformations and struggles with intrinsically disordered regions, ligand-bound states, post-translational modifications, and large multi-component assemblies [12]. As acknowledged in the AlphaFold Database publication, approximately one-third of residues do not achieve atomistic precision. These shortcomings highlight the ongoing need for integration with experimental validation, particularly through high-resolution imaging techniques such as cryo-EM and cryo-ET.


### Transitional Summary and Study Rationale
  Together, cryo-EM, cryo-ET, and AlphaFold represent complementary approaches that have collectively redefined structural biology. Cryo-EM offers near-atomic resolution for purified macromolecules, cryo-ET extends structural insight to the cellular level, and AlphaFold introduces data-driven prediction that bridges experimental gaps. Importantly, the convergence of AI-based prediction and high-resolution imaging now defines a new frontier in integrative structural biology.

  Recent improvements in cryo-EM resolution (approaching 1.5–2.0 Å) and AlphaFold’s confidence metrics (pLDDT, pTM) have made cross-validation and model integration increasingly feasible. Indeed, a growing body of work demonstrates automated fitting and flexible refinement of AlphaFold2-predicted models into cryo-EM maps — as illustrated by DeepTracer and AlphaFold2, DomainFit and elastic-network-based flexible fitting methods [13].

  In this review, we critically examine recent studies (2022–2025) that integrate AlphaFold with cryo-EM and cryo-ET workflows, emphasizing both the advances achieved and the methodological limitations that remain. We frame cryo-EM and cryo-ET as contextual tools that validate, refine, or challenge AlphaFold predictions rather than as co-equal foci. This analytical perspective aligns with the review’s title—“Insights from Recent Studies”—and aims to provide a literature-driven synthesis of how AI-based modeling and experimental imaging together are reshaping modern structural biology.

### Statistical Analysis of AlphaFold Usage by Resolution
  Datasets were binned into high-resolution (≤ 4.0 Å) versus lower-resolution (> 4.0 Å) groups. Statistical tests—including chi-square (χ2 = 0.20, p = 0.656), Fisher’s exact (p = 0.640), and Spearman’s ρ (ρ = –0.163, p = 0.373)—revealed no significant association between nominal map resolution and AlphaFold usage (Supplementary Fig. S1A–B).

  Interpretation. The absence of correlation likely reflects broad adoption across resolution regimes: even at medium-to-low resolutions, AlphaFold2 domain models are routinely used for docking/annotation, whereas at near-atomic resolutions experimental density already enables confident building. In other words, decision to use AlphaFold2 is driven more by local interpretability and compositional complexity than by global nominal resolution. We note that power is limited by the sample size (n = 32), and future expansions may refine these trends.


### Result 2 — AlphaFold2 in Protein–Protein Interaction Studies
  All case studies presented in this section involve the explicit use of AlphaFold2 as part of the structural determination workflow. Across diverse biological contexts—from chromatin remodeling complexes and viral core proteins to nuclear pore components and RNA virus assemblies—AlphaFold2 has been employed to generate high-confidence structural predictions that complement experimental cryo-EM and cryo-ET data, thereby resolving binding interfaces, oligomeric states, and dynamic architectures that were previously inaccessible.


### Fundamental Principles and Advances in Protein–Protein Interaction Studies
  Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are fundamental to virtually all biological processes, including cellular signaling, metabolic regulation, immune defense, and structural organization. These interactions frequently occur within large and dynamic macromolecular assemblies, where subtle conformational changes can determine functional outcomes. Traditional experimental methods—such as X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy, and cryo-EM—have provided invaluable insights but are often constrained by resolution, sample requirements, or their inability to capture transient states.

  The advent of deep learning–based prediction tools, particularly AlphaFold2 [10], has transformed computational structural biology by enabling high-accuracy modeling of tertiary and quaternary structures. In its “Complex Prediction” mode, AlphaFold2 can model binding interfaces and oligomeric states de novo [10].

### RNA Virus Assembly Mechanisms
  AlphaFold2 has also provided critical insights into RNA virus organization. In EMD-46612 [19], the rabies virus matrix protein (M) was reconstructed by cryo-EM at 18.7 Å (Fig. 3B, cyan). AlphaFold2 predicted an elongated structure with a conserved hydrophobic groove, and when fitted into the cryo-EM density, the model revealed a 1:1 stoichiometry with nucleoprotein (N), organizing the ribonucleoprotein complex during budding [20].

  Similarly, the Thogoto virus nucleoprotein (NP) was reconstructed by cryo-ET at 18.0 Å (EMD-19599; Fig. 3C, pink) [21]. AlphaFold2 modeling revealed NP as a hexameric ring stacking into left-handed double helices, suggesting a conserved genome-packaging mechanism across orthomyxoviruses.

## Summary
  Together, these case studies highlight how AlphaFold2 complements cryo-EM and cryo-ET by providing accurate de novo models that can be docked into medium-resolution density maps. This synergy enhances model completeness, accelerates structural interpretation, and guides experimental validation. From E3 ligase–substrate interactions to viral capsid assembly, nuclear pore plasticity, and RNA virus genome packaging, AlphaFold2 has resolved previously intractable structural questions and revealed novel biological mechanisms.


### Result 3. AlphaFold’s Applications, Limitations, and Synergy with Cryo-EM/ET
  Despite its transformative impact on single-protein modeling, AlphaFold2 remains constrained when biological insight depends on native context, mesoscale organization, or time-dependent conformational change. Reading across our dataset, the cases in this section demonstrate that cryo-EM and cryo-ET frequently provide information beyond the predictive scope of current AI models, while also showing how AlphaFold2 can still contribute as a source of high-confidence starting models that are refined against experimental density. Together, these studies delineate a practical division of labor: AlphaFold2 excels at stable domain geometry and interface hypotheses, whereas cryo-EM/ET resolves membrane association, long-range packing, and state switching in the cellular environment.


### In situ macromolecular assemblies: contextual complexity beyond prediction
  In situ assemblies underscore why contextual complexity exceeds sequence-to-structure prediction. In the CRP–C1 system (EMD-18830), cryo-ET visualized a four-pentamer C-reactive protein platform engaging four C1 head groups on damaged membranes, revealing an arrangement driven by surface electrostatics and curvature that cannot be inferred from isolated subunits (Fig. 4A) [22]. A similar point emerges from the LRRK2–microtubule complex (EMD-40525), where only cellular cryo-ET captured the filamentous registry and domain disposition that stabilize native filaments (Fig. 4B) [23]. Nucleosome-scale chromatin organization likewise illustrates limits of prediction: Volta phase-plate cryo-ET of RPE-1 cells (EMD-37002) showed irregular chromatin domains containing canonical mono- and dinucleosomes in G1 and densely packed, substructure-poor chromatids in metaphase; higher-order ordered fibers were absent (Fig. 4C) [24]. During mitotic condensation (EMD-19459), subtomogram averages revealed short-range nucleosome stacking rather than long-range fibers, with rearrangements linked to condensins and histone modifications (Fig. 4D) [25]. ## Summary
  By reading across these studies, we can see that while AlphaFold has demonstrated significant advantages in predicting the structures of individual proteins or simpler complexes, its utility is inherently linked to its design. As a tool primarily for proteins, it cannot predict the structures of nucleic acids, lipids, or other non-protein macromolecules, nor simulate the interactions of these molecules within dynamic cellular environments.

  These limitations are evident in studies of large in situ assemblies like CRP–C1 (Fig. 4A) and LRRK2–microtubules (Fig. 4B), higher-order chromatin organization (Fig. 4C–D), dynamic myosin head transitions (Fig. 5A), and complex functional systems such as protein engineering and immune evasion (Fig. 7).

  Despite these boundaries, AlphaFold is widely and effectively used as a complementary tool: it verifies experimental results, supplements structural information (e.g., ZBTB20, Fig. 6A–E), predicts flexible or unresolved domains (e.g., MYPT3 ankyrin repeats, Fig. 6F), and offers initial models where data are incomplete (e.g., SNO-GSTO1). The benefits include completing structural models, guiding mutational analyses, and bridging gaps where experimental methods face challenges with dynamic or flexible regions. In these contexts, AlphaFold enhances and complements experimental workflows, but does not replace them.


## Discussion

### Understanding the Frontiers of AlphaFold’s Predictive Prowess
  While AlphaFold has indisputably transformed structural biology by providing near-experimental accuracy for a wide range of proteins, a complete understanding of its predictive power requires acknowledging its boundaries. These limitations arise not only from the scope of its training data but also from the complexity and dynamism of real biological systems, where structure and function are rarely static.

  A key limitation is AlphaFold’s reliance on large datasets of previously determined, predominantly soluble and well-folded proteins. This introduces a structural bias that limits performance for novel folds, membrane proteins, and multi-subunit assemblies lacking homologous templates. For example, chromatin and sarcomere assemblies (Figs. 4C–D and 5A) involve extended, flexible architectures whose modular interfaces are incompletely represented in AlphaFold’s training distribution. These cases highlight how the method’s data-driven priors constrain generalization beyond canonical globular folds.

  AlphaFold’s difficulties with intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) further illustrate the limits of its design. Proteins such as α-synuclein, Tau, and the p53 transactivation domain adopt heterogeneous, dynamic conformations in vivo but are returned by AlphaFold as static, fragmented, low-confidence predictions (pLDDT < 50). Experimental evidence from NMR, SAXS, and single-molecule fluorescence confirms that these regions exist as ensembles of interconverting structures, emphasizing that static predictions cannot capture their biological relevance.

  Challenges also arise in protein–protein interaction (PPI) modeling. Although AlphaFold-Multimer extends AlphaFold’s capacity to predict quaternary structures, it struggles with weak or transient interactions where interface geometries vary across conditions. For instance, in complexes such as Hsp90–cochaperone and ubiquitin–E2 enzyme assemblies, AlphaFold-Multimer often misassigns partner orientation or fails to model the flexible linker regions that mediate binding. These issues are particularly common in low-affinity or regulatory assemblies, where small energetic perturbations yield large conformational variability. Likewise, ligand-induced transitions and ternary PROTAC complexes (Fig. 5C) remain beyond the system’s resolution because such rearrangements involve context-dependent energetics that cannot be inferred from sequence alone.

  Another major challenge lies in transmembrane protein prediction. Unlike soluble proteins, membrane proteins are stabilized by lipid interactions and asymmetric electrostatic environments that are absent from AlphaFold’s training data. As a result, helical bundle orientation, loop topology, and dynamic coupling to lipid bilayers are often mispredicted. Comparisons with cryo-EM reconstructions of GPCRs and ABC transporters show that AlphaFold2 correctly captures the general fold but mispositions helices by up to 3–5 Å in low-homology regions. Recent efforts such as MembraneFold and AF2Complex have begun to address these issues by embedding physical membrane constraints or coevolutionary interface priors into the prediction pipeline.

  The modeling of large or in situ macromolecular assemblies also exposes AlphaFold’s limitations. Complexes like CRP–C1 and LRRK2–microtubule filaments (Fig. 4A–B) exhibit compositional heterogeneity and contextual flexibility that are only discernible under native cellular conditions. AlphaFold cannot capture such mesoscale organization, which depends on crowding, cofactors, and post-translational modifications. Similarly, multi-chain docking in AlphaFold-Multimer remains rigid; dynamic rearrangements and conformational gating are not yet captured, limiting its use for modeling transient assemblies and viral capsids.

  To overcome these barriers, integrative workflows combining AI-based prediction with experimental validation are increasingly crucial. In the ZBTB20 and MYPT3 studies, AlphaFold2 models were refined using cryo-EM maps, SAXS profiles, and crystallographic restraints via tools such as Phenix real-space refinement and ISOLDE. This integration provided domain-level accuracy while correcting misoriented linkers and flexible loops. Similar strategies can be expanded to include cross-linking mass spectrometry, chemical labeling, and HDX–MS, which offer empirical distance or dynamics constraints to guide refinement. However, we also note that aligning predicted coordinates with experimental density can be non-trivial; discrepancies in scaling, overfitting, or partial density matching can propagate bias if not carefully validated.

  Despite its computational efficiency relative to traditional physics-based simulations, AlphaFold remains resource-intensive for proteome-scale or multi-chain systems. GPU-based inference for large complexes still represent a bottleneck for smaller research groups, limiting widespread accessibility and benchmarking diversity.

  Finally, the dynamic nature of proteins underscores the conceptual gap between AlphaFold’s static predictions and biological reality. Proteins continuously sample conformational ensembles, undergo modifications, and participate in transient networks. Future development must integrate molecular dynamics, ensemble refinement, and machine-learning models trained on time-resolved data to move toward a dynamic, context-aware representation of protein structure.

  We also acknowledge that our dataset of 32 cryo-EM and cryo-ET studies, while systematically selected, captures only a fraction of the literature available by 2024. Nevertheless, this focused corpus allowed us to extract quantitative insights into AlphaFold2 adoption patterns, identify recurring experimental contexts where it excels or fails, and highlight emerging integrative workflows—including its application to in situ cryo-ET, membrane assemblies, and plant-specific transcriptional complexes (Fig. 5A). These observations extend beyond previously published general reviews by linking specific structural contexts to predictive performance trends, thereby providing a comparative framework for the community to assess AlphaFold’s evolving role in structural biology.


### Limitations
  This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our dataset is restricted to 32 cryo-EM and cryo-ET studies deposited in the EMDB in 2024, which provides a focused but non-exhaustive snapshot of current AlphaFold usage; therefore, trends identified here may not fully represent the broader structural biology community. Second, the classification of “AlphaFold contribution” relies on descriptions provided by the original articles, which vary in detail and may introduce reporting bias. Third, we did not include non-EM structural techniques such as NMR, X-ray crystallography, or XL-MS workflows, which may influence how AlphaFold is adopted across disciplines. Finally, our synthesis emphasizes qualitative interpretation rather than benchmarking predictive accuracy, and future meta-analyses incorporating quantitative metrics could further refine these observations.


### Future Perspectives
  The extraordinary success of AlphaFold2 has set a new standard in structural prediction, but significant opportunities remain to expand its scope, improve its accuracy, and enhance its integration with experimental data. Examples throughout this study (Figs. 4, 5, 6, 7) illuminate key frontiers for future development.

  One major direction is modeling protein dynamics. AlphaFold’s static predictions do not reflect the reality of conformational landscapes. Integrating molecular dynamics simulations—such as those based on Markov state models—could enable ensemble-based predictions that capture intermediate and allosteric states. This would be especially beneficial in large, flexible systems like those observed in Fig. 5A, where transient domain movements are central to function.

  Improving interaction prediction is another urgent goal. AlphaFold-Multimer has limitations in modeling weak, transient, or flexible interfaces, as demonstrated in the PROTAC ternary complex case (Fig. 5C). Here, enhanced interface-specific modeling that combines deep learning with co-evolutionary coupling and physics-based scoring could improve structural accuracy for drug discovery applications.

  Extending predictive capabilities to intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) also represent a crucial challenge. Experimental techniques like NMR and single-molecule FRET could provide valuable restraints for machine learning models to generate conformational ensembles, especially for signaling proteins or plant-specific IDPs (Fig. 5A). This would move beyond AlphaFold’s current tendency to return collapsed or uncertain outputs for disordered segments.

  Integrative modeling is likely to become the norm, rather than the exception. Combining AlphaFold predictions with experimental density maps, chemical crosslinking, or SAXS profiles—as in Fig. 6A–F—can help disambiguate flexible domains, validate domain orientation, and complete partial reconstructions. For example, cryo-EM data in the ZBTB20 complex (Fig. 6A–E) and the ankyrin repeat domains of MYPT3 (Fig. 6F) highlight how predictive models can serve as robust starting points, but require experimental anchoring to reach atomic precision.

  In parallel, the ability to integrate ligand and small-molecule docking into predictive frameworks could greatly enhance AlphaFold’s utility in pharmacological design. This is particularly pressing in contexts such as immune evasion (Fig. 7B), where small differences in binding geometry or glycosylation can profoundly affect antigen presentation.

  Future structure prediction frameworks are expected to integrate generative diffusion models and multimodal datasets, combining cryo-EM density, NMR restraints, and sequence co-evolution to bridge the gap between static prediction and dynamic function.

  Finally, accessibility and interpretability must improve. Future tools should offer intuitive interfaces, clear uncertainty metrics, and easy export to modeling pipelines. Visual aids that link structural predictions to biological function would empower experimentalists to generate more targeted hypotheses, and in turn, produce better training data for future AI models.


### Toward an Integrative, Context-Aware Structural Biology
  AlphaFold is not the endpoint—it is the launchpad. Its greatest impact will emerge not from replacing traditional methods, but from elevating them into a more predictive, integrative paradigm. From resolving membrane-bound CRP platforms (Fig. 4A) to refining conformationally heterogeneous chromatin structures (Fig. 4C–D), and from stabilizing dynamic sarcomere assemblies (Fig. 5A) to accelerating structure-based mutagenesis (Fig. 6), the synergy between AI and experimentation is unmistakable.

  Moving forward, structural biology will depend increasingly on hybrid workflows that combine high-accuracy AI predictions with rich experimental context. Together, these tools promise not only to solve structures—but to understand how they move, interact, and function inside living systems.


## Conclusion
  This study presents a systematic and comparative analysis of 32 cryo-EM and cryo-ET studies published in 2024, providing quantitative evidence for the growing integration of AlphaFold into structural biology. Among these, 25 studies (78.1%) employed AlphaFold2 in some capacity, while 7 did not. In our analysis, “contribution” was operationally defined as AlphaFold being used for at least one of the following: (i) model completion in unresolved or low-density regions, (ii) domain or subunit fitting within cryo-EM maps, or (iii) generation of structure-based functional hypotheses (e.g., mutagenesis targets or conformational interpretation). Under this definition, AlphaFold enabled atomic-level modeling (pLDDT > 90) in the majority of AlphaFold-utilizing cases, including complex systems such as viral core assemblies (EMD-17704) [13], chromatin remodelers (EMD-37966) [12], and E3 ligase–substrate complexes (EMD-38163) [11].

  AlphaFold’s utility was most evident where experimental resolution or local map quality limited de novo building, enabling structure-based hypotheses, mutagenesis guidance, and subunit assignment. Its synergy with cryo-EM supported structural elucidation even in challenging contexts, exemplified by rabies virus matrix proteins (EMD-46612) [19] and host–pathogen interfaces such as the SARS-CoV-2 ORF6 nuclear pore blockade (EMD-19137) [17]. At the same time, our dataset also exposes clear boundaries. In CRP–C1 complexes (EMD-18830) [32] and LRRK2–microtubule assemblies (EMD-40525) [23], AlphaFold failed to recapitulate domain movements, flexible linkers, and compositional heterogeneity evident in cryo-ET maps—illustrating that high local confidence does not guarantee biological completeness in native, heterogeneous environments. Likewise, mitotic chromatin compaction (EMD-37002; EMD-19459) [24, 25] underscores mesoscale packing and cell-state transitions that remain experiment-resolved rather than prediction-resolved.

  Beyond restating known limitations (e.g., flexible or disordered regions), this review adds new, comparative insights from the 2024 corpus: (1) adoption patterns—AlphaFold2 was applied most frequently to viral and bacterial assemblies typically mapped at ~ 7–20 Å, while higher-resolution eukaryotic complexes showed reduced dependence; and (2) mode of use—across AlphaFold-utilizing studies, most instances fell into domain-level completion/validation rather than full complex determination, aligning with the method’s current strengths. These meta-level observations help contextualize when AlphaFold materially improves outcomes versus when orthogonal data are decisive.

  Looking forward, integrative workflows will define the next phase of discovery. In practical terms, this means iterative fitting and refinement that couples AlphaFold starting models with density-guided rebuilding and validation (e.g., real-space refinement, interactive flexible refinement, cross-validation with biochemical restraints), alongside MD-based ensemble sampling to probe conformational variability. Such pipelines are already enabling reweighting by density correlation, confidence-aware editing of low-pLDDT segments, and targeted experimental design, thereby moving from static predictions toward dynamic, context-aware structural hypotheses. In this view, the convergence of computation and experiment is not replacing bench validation but redefining its scope—accelerating the path from structure determination to functional interpretation and rational design.

  Finally, we explicitly acknowledge that our dataset—32 EMDB-linked studies from a single year—is a focused, illustrative snapshot rather than an exhaustive survey. Nonetheless, to our knowledge, this is the first systematic, quantitative synthesis of AlphaFold utilization specifically within EMDB-deposited cryo-EM and cryo-ET structures in 2024. By coupling case-level evidence with cross-study statistics, the review delineates where predictive models align—or diverge—from biological reality and highlights priority areas where integrative modeling can most effectively advance structural biology.


### EMDB-Based Dataset Collection and Literature Screening
  We performed a time-bounded survey of the Electron Microscopy Data Bank (EMDB), which archives 3D cryo-EM and cryo-ET maps/tomograms and associated metadata (formal resource citation provided in the References). To capture current practice, we restricted the search to entries deposited between 1 January and 31 December 2024. The EMDB query returned all 2024 deposits (N =  < N_total_2024 > ; see the legend of Table 1), from which we curated a study set of 32 entries with peer-reviewed primary articles suitable for AlphaFold usage assessment (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. S1).

  Topic filters and rationale. To ensure biological and methodological diversity while keeping the review tractable, we used stratified purposive sampling across organismal contexts and resolution regimes. Specifically, we defined topic bins—Homo sapiens, Sus scrofa, vaccinia virus/viral assemblies, other viral vaccines, and a resolution-focused bin—to (i) represent both eukaryotic and viral systems that dominate 2024 depositions, (ii) balance single-particle cryo-EM and in situ cryo-ET exemplars, and (iii) include a near-atomic subset for contrastive analysis. Counts per bin (e.g., Sus scrofa, n = 6; vaccinia virus, n = 5; etc.) are reported in Table 1. Following community convention, we classify high resolution as < 3.0 Å, and 3.0–4.0 Å as near-atomic/medium–high; the latter category (9 entries) is labeled accordingly in the tables to avoid ambiguity.

  Inclusion/exclusion criteria. We included EMDB entries that (i) had a peer-reviewed primary publication in 2024 linked to the deposition; (ii) reported experimental cryo-EM or cryo-ET data as the basis for structural interpretation; and (iii) provided sufficient methodological detail to verify whether AlphaFold/AlphaFold2 (monomer) and/or AlphaFold-Multimer were used. We excluded purely computational papers without experimental maps, preprints lacking peer review for the primary structure report, and entries missing sufficient methodological detail. The screening flow and counts at each stage (initial retrieval, screened, included/excluded with reasons) are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S1.

  Ascertainment of AlphaFold usage. For each included study, two reviewers independently performed full-text manual screening supplemented by keyword searches (“AlphaFold”, “AlphaFold2”, “AF2”, “Multimer”, “ColabFold”, “predicted model”, “fitted AF2 domain”). Disagreements were resolved by consensus; Cohen’s κ for the binary label “AF used vs not used” is reported in Supplementary Table S1. Where AlphaFold was used, we coded mode of use into three non-exclusive categories: initial model completion, domain/subunit fitting/validation, and functional inference (e.g., guiding mutagenesis). These category counts are reported in Table 1.


### Data Extraction, Coding, and Quantitative Synthesis
  For each study we extracted: organism/system, technique (cryo-EM/cryo-ET), nominal and local resolution (Å), EMDB/PDB accessions, AlphaFold version (monomer vs Multimer), presence of pLDDT/PAE confidence reporting, and the use-mode categories above. Data were tabulated in a version-controlled spreadsheet (Supplementary Data File 1) and analyzed in R/Python (scripts provided as Supplementary Code). Summary statistics (proportions and 95% CIs) and association tests (χ2/Fisher’s; Spearman’s ρ for resolution vs usage) are presented in the Results and Supplementary Fig. S1.


### Structural Visualization and Fit Assessment
  EMDB maps (.map/.mrc) were downloaded via the EMDB web interface and programmatically using the EMDB REST API for batch retrieval (accession list in Supplementary Data File 1). Visualizations were prepared primarily in UCSF ChimeraX (v < ChimeraX_version >) with confirmation in UCSF Chimera (v < Chimera_version >). Maps were displayed with Volume Viewer, and atomic models (PDB or deposited models) were fitted using Fit in Map. Where AlphaFold predictions were integrated by the original authors, we overlaid the corresponding AlphaFold models and annotated pLDDT/PAE where available.

  Contour-level policy. We adopted the EMDB-recommended contour as the starting level and adjusted within ± 10–20% to optimize visualization of contiguous signal while avoiding noise inflation; the chosen contour (absolute value or multiplier of the recommended level) is reported in figure legends. To assess agreement between model and density we used map–model correlation (ChimeraX Map–Model Fit, reporting CC_mask/CC_box) when models were available; qualitative overlays are used otherwise, with representative views captured from multiple angles. Where relevant, we additionally inspected local resolution maps to ensure that fitting claims did not rely on sub-threshold regions.


### Definitions and Versioning
  Throughout, “AlphaFold2” refers to the monomeric prediction framework; “AlphaFold-Multimer” denotes the multimeric complex mode; and “ColabFold” refers to community deployments of AF2/Multimer. When a paper stated “AlphaFold” generically, we resolved the version from the text, methods, or repository metadata; unresolved cases are labeled “AF (unspecified)” in Table 1.


### Transparency, Reproducibility, and Limitations
  All screening criteria, coded variables, accession lists, and analysis scripts are provided in Supplementary Data File 1 and Supplementary Code. The curated dataset size is 32 studies (25 with AlphaFold usage; 7 without), as summarized in Table 1 with system-level breakdowns. We note that restricting to one calendar year yields a focused but non-exhaustive corpus; accordingly, our statistical syntheses are descriptive, and inferences are framed with this scope in mind.
