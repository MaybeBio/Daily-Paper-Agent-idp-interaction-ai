# Proteome-wide computational analyses reveal links between protein condensate formation and RNA biology


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates mediate dynamic compartmentalization of cellular processes. The multivalent interactions that underlie biomolecular condensation are often promoted by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within proteins. Although the role of IDRs in biomolecular condensates is well appreciated, predicting whether an IDR forms condensates in cells remains challenging. Here, we developed a machine learning model to accurately predict the condensation behavior of IDRs, analyzing 215 IDRs from fusion oncoproteins in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells. We identified distinct sequence-derived physicochemical features associated with condensation. Leveraging these data, our model predicts that ~12% of the ~13,000 IDRs in the human proteome are likely to form cellular condensates, establishing a robust framework for proteome-wide analysis of IDR-mediated biomolecular condensation. Notably, proteins with condensate-forming IDRs are significantly enriched in RNA processing and splicing functions and are predominantly localized to membraneless organelles, highlighting a central role of IDR-mediated biomolecular condensation in cellular organization and RNA biology.


## INTRODUCTION
  Biomolecular condensates, also termed membraneless organelles (MLOs), mediate dynamic compartmentalization of biomolecules associated with diverse biological processes (1). Found in both the nucleus (nucleoli, paraspeckles, Cajal bodies, and PML bodies) and the cytoplasm (P-bodies, stress granules, and germ granules), MLOs are essential for myriad cellular functions, and their dysfunction has been implicated in numerous diseases (2–4). The assembly of biomolecules into condensates is often driven by phase separation (PS), which is facilitated by weak, multivalent interactions between constituent biomolecules (5) and is often promoted by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in proteins (6–8). IDRs lack stable secondary and tertiary structures and often retain a high degree of disorder when interacting with other molecules to form condensates (6, 9, 10). They show biased amino acid composition, with depletion in structure-promoting hydrophobic amino acids and enrichment of polar and charged amino acids, features that enable the dynamic multivalent interactions that underpin PS (11, 12). Analyses of amino acid patterns in IDRs across diverse biomolecular condensates have revealed the sequence-related physicochemical features that govern condensate formation (13–16).

  Although IDRs can autonomously drive PS in many instances, there are proteins wherein IDRs are not sufficient to induce PS alone but still play critical roles in the formation and regulation of biomolecular condensates. By participating in multivalent networks with other IDRs, folded domains with binding sites, and/or nucleic acids like RNA (9), they often lower the saturation concentration for PS, facilitate selective recruitment of specific partners into condensates, and/or tune material properties such as surface tension or viscoelasticity (3, 4, 11). In addition, their potential for posttranslational modification allows for dynamic regulation of condensate formation and dissolution in response to cellular signals (7). However, a key limitation in characterizing these behaviors is the strong context dependence of IDR interactions. Factors such as ionic strength, pH, temperature, and the presence of ligands or crowding agents can markedly alter their phase behavior, making it difficult to generalize across conditions. Thus, although IDRs are powerful modulators of phase behavior in multicomponent systems, their functions must be considered within the specific biophysical and cellular environments in which they operate, underscoring the importance of studying IDR-mediated processes under biologically relevant conditions.

  Multiple algorithms have been developed to identify features in IDRs associated with PS, and several predictors are available (17–27). For example, PhaSePred (22) predicts PS in autonomously self-assembling and partner-dependent proteins using multimodal features. DeePhase (23) uses a combined engineered features model and a neural network–based language model to predict the propensity of proteins to undergo homotypic PS. FuzDrop (28), a conformational entropy–based model, predicts droplet-promoting regions and proteins driving PS. Tools such as ParSe v2 (25) and catGranule (29) leverage sequence-specific features, such as sequence composition, prion-like domains, and charge patterning to predict PS, whereas others, such as MolPhase (30), integrate multidimensional data, including structural and physicochemical properties. The predictive algorithm PScore (17) estimates protein PS propensity on the basis of π-π interaction frequencies, whereas LLPhyScore (21) predicts protein PS potential using a comprehensive set of sequence-derived and physical chemistry–based features, offering valuable insights into mechanisms of IDR-driven protein PS. A recently developed predictor called PICNIC (31) uses protein-protein interaction networks to exclude proteins connected with known condensate-forming proteins. However, these predictors rely on literature-mined data, which were often obtained using nonstandard experimental conditions, leading to variability and noise. In addition, these studies often use information from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (32) or the human proteome as negative datasets, which results in false negatives. Curating a verified set of IDRs, including those that form condensates and others that remain diffuse under controlled experimental conditions, is crucial for training and validating predictive models (33). In our previous study, we developed the FO-Puncta machine learning (ML) model using experimentally validated datasets to predict the condensation behavior of fusion oncoproteins (FOs) in cells (34). However, this model does not identify the specific regions responsible for condensate formation nor does it elucidate the role and influence of IDRs present in these FOs on the condensation process. Recently, an ML model was developed that predicts homotypic PS of IDRs (35); however, heterotypic interactions (between IDRs and other proteins or nucleic acids) also contribute to the formation of many biological condensates. Thus, it is important to assess IDR condensate formation under conditions reflecting the compositional complexity of cellular environments.

  We addressed these challenges by examining the cellular condensation behavior of 215 IDRs derived from 149 human FOs, 96 of which were previously shown to form cellular condensates (34). Each IDR was tagged with monomeric, enhanced green fluorescent protein (mEGFP) and assessed in cells using standardized conditions and microscopy protocols, coupled with rigorous computational image analysis. These efforts yielded a dataset of condensate-forming [termed puncta(+)] and condensate-negative [puncta(−)] IDRs, and the data were used to develop an ML model (termed IDR-Puncta ML model) to predict IDR condensation with high accuracy, based on a large set of sequence-derived physicochemical features. Using the IDR-Puncta ML model, available via the SAK3.0 web server (https://sak.stjude.org), we demonstrate that 12% (1572 of 12,899) IDRs identified in the human proteome (termed the human “IDRome”) are predicted to form biomolecular condensates. We show that proteins containing puncta(+) IDRs (1393 of 8067 IDR-containing human proteins) are substantially enriched in Gene Ontology (GO) terms involving RNA-related biological processes, including transcription and processing, and others associated with cell division and actin cytoskeleton. Moreover, these proteins are also overrepresented as constituents of MLOs. These findings indicate that proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are specialized for functions associated with nuclear processes involving RNA processing, suggesting a role for PS-driven compartmentalization of proteins, RNA, and other biomolecules in these processes. We note that these findings are based on analyses of IDRs derived from cancer-associated FOs, and their functional associations may be biased toward biological processes altered in cancer. However, the physicochemical features of FO-derived IDRs broadly sample those of the human IDRome, supporting the generality of our conclusions on the specialized biological functions of puncta(+) IDR-containing human proteins. Last, the IDR-Puncta ML model, given its high accuracy, will be a powerful tool for uncovering the biological roles of proteins with puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs in organisms beyond humans.


## RESULTS

### Establishing the condensation behavior of diverse IDRs
  We previously compiled a database of 3174 FO protein sequences and initially tested 166 of them for condensate formation in HeLa cells, with 96 (58%) of these forming condensates [puncta(+)] and 53 (32%) remaining diffuse [puncta(−)]. The sequences of the 96 puncta(+) FOs were significantly enriched in amino acids associated with protein disorder and in physicochemical features associated with PS, including the likelihood of π-π and π-cation interactions and the presence of prion-like domains (34). These observations led us to hypothesize that IDRs contribute to FO condensation behavior and that this set of 96 puncta(+) and 53 puncta(−) FOs (149 total) could serve as a source of both puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs.

  We used IUPred2A (36) implemented within the SAK pipeline (34), and Metapredict (37), to identify 215 unique IDRs with long disordered regions (≥60 amino acids in length, a threshold determined by examining existing databases of phase separating proteins) within 83 puncta(+) and 47 puncta(−) FOs (see Materials and Methods). We note that 19 of the 149 puncta(+) and puncta(−) FOs did not display IDRs of sufficient length for inclusion. We expressed GFP-tagged forms of the 215 IDRs in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells and scored them for condensate formation using established procedures (34), including use of the PunctaTools image analysis pipeline (38) (fig. S1, A and B, and table S1). The PunctaTools pipeline uses a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) transformation that identifies regions of rapid fluorescence intensity change. This method effectively segments punctate structures but can also segment boundaries between cell compartments when there is a large difference in fluorescence intensity. To allow for the possibility of mis-segmentation, we established a minimum threshold of GFP-tagged IDR-expressing cells with segmented puncta (expressed as a % of cells) and manually confirmed or corrected puncta assignments. IDRs that formed condensates in ≥24% of the imaged cells with GFP intensity above background were scored as puncta(+); those that formed condensates in <24% of the imaged cells or were diffuse (e.g., not segmented) in all cells were scored as puncta(−) (fig. S1, C and D). We further supplemented PunctaTools automated labeling with a structured manual review to correct systematic (although infrequent) segmentation errors, apply alternate labels (Other, Nucleolar), and assign final classifications (see Materials and Methods for details).

  Some IDRs localized within nucleoli or other cellular structures lacking hallmark condensate features (e.g., round appearance with varied size) and were scored as nucleolar or other, respectively (fig. S1, E and F), and not included in subsequent analyses. Of the 215 IDRs examined, 19% (41 of 215) were classified as puncta(+), 64% (137 of 215) as puncta(−), 7% (15 of 215) as nucleolar, and 10% (22 of 215) as other (Fig. 1A). Among the puncta(+) IDRs, 61% (25 of 41) formed condensates within nuclei, 10% (4 of 41) within the cytoplasm, and 29% (12 of 41) within both cellular compartments (fig. S1, C and D) and the number and size of these condensates varied widely (fig. S1C). Puncta(−) IDRs also exhibited varied subcellular localization (fig. S1D) with many displaying diffuse fluorescence similar to that of mEGFP, the negative control (fig. S1G).

  Our results showed that 48% of IDR-containing, puncta(+) FOs (40 of 83) display at least one puncta(+) IDR (Fig. 1B), leading us to hypothesize that puncta(+) IDRs contribute to condensate formation by these FOs. We tested this idea through IDR deletion analysis of five puncta(+) FOs, each containing a single puncta(+) and multiple puncta(−) IDRs (fig. S2 and table S2). We observed loss of condensate formation for three of the five FO IDR deletion mutants, supporting that puncta(+) IDRs substantially contribute to condensate formation by these puncta(+) FOs (fig. S2), although the magnitude of this effect is dependent on the fusion protein domain structure. In ARID1B::ZNF384 and PAX5::ZCCHC7, no change in condensation behavior was seen, likely due to compensation by retained folded domains (e.g., ZNF384 zinc fingers; PAX DNA binding domain) and IDRs, indicating that an autonomous condensate-forming IDR may be sufficient but not strictly necessary for condensate formation by FOs depending on the context. For the remaining IDR-containing, puncta(+) FOs (43 of 83) that lack a condensate-forming IDR, condensate formation is likely influenced by additional factors, such as synergy between IDRs and folded domains or recruitment as clients into condensates formed by endogenous cellular biomolecules. Of the 47 puncta(−) FOs, one contained a puncta(+) IDR, and another contained an IDR that localized within nucleoli (Fig. 1B). For these two puncta(−) FOs, the condensate-associated IDRs they contain are insufficient to drive condensate formation by the full proteins, potentially due to solubilizing effects of folded domains and/or other IDRs in their sequences.

  Our results show that 19% (41 of 215) of the IDRs we tested form cellular condensates, with all but one of these derived from condensate-forming FOs. Most of the tested IDRs (64%, 137 of 215) scored as puncta(−), with many of these derived from condensate-forming FOs. These results suggest that autonomously puncta(+) IDRs that are present in roughly half of the puncta(+) FOs can mediate their condensate formation. However, puncta(+) IDRs may also cooperate with folded domains and other IDRs that also promote condensate formation by puncta(+) FOs. Furthermore, autonomously puncta(−) IDRs in puncta(+) FOs likely synergize with other protein regions (e.g., other IDRs and/or folded domains) in the portion of puncta(+) FOs lacking one or more autonomously puncta(+) IDRs. These observations highlight the complexity of interplay between puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs and folded domains within FOs in mediating condensate formation. This complex interplay is certainly also operative in the human proteins that are parents of FOs. It is possible that some IDRs with condensate-forming potential were overlooked due to the strict thresholds applied for IDR length and the percentage of cells exhibiting puncta (>24%) or that some FOs form condensates through mechanisms that do not involve disordered regions. Our previous studies of FOs indicated that sequence-derived physicochemical features are statistically accurate indicators of their condensation behavior. On the basis of these findings, we next asked whether various physical chemistry–based features could distinguish between puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs.


### Physicochemical features and amino acid enrichments governing IDR condensate formation
  We analyzed the amino acid sequences of the 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs to identify physicochemical features associated with autonomous cellular condensate formation (Fig. 2A). For each sequence, we calculated the values of 600 diverse features, including physicochemical features previously shown to be relevant to PS [38 features from the SAK pipeline (34)], numerical indices related to amino acid physicochemical and biochemical properties [553 features from AAindex v9.2 (39) grouped into 12 classes; fig. S3, A and B], and molecular interaction-based features [9 features from LLPhyScore (21)]. Feature values are reported as z-scores with respect to average values for the human IDRome (all IDRs ≥60 amino acids in length in the human proteome; see Materials and Methods). Among these 600 features, we identified 38 with average z-score values significantly different between puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs. To minimize redundancy, we removed 13 features that exhibited high mutual information (MI) scores with others (MI > 0.5) (Fig. 2A). The remaining 25 features sample a range of amino acid sequence–derived properties, including prion-like domain content, fraction of aromatic amino acids, potential for interactions, disorder content, charge content and patterning, and hydrophobic characteristics, which are differentially enriched or depleted in puncta(+) versus puncta(−) IDRs (Fig. 2B and table S3). Secondary structure–related terms associated with extended conformations, e.g., sheets and coils, are enriched in puncta(+) IDRs, whereas those associated with compact conformations, e.g., helices and turns, are depleted. This collection of 25 features defines the physicochemical properties that underpin condensate formation by IDRs in the crowded, heterogeneous cellular environment (table S3).

  We next used two-dimensional (2D) hierarchical clustering to determine whether the individual IDRs within the puncta(+) and puncta(−) sets exhibited similar or different features and whether they clustered into groups with similar features. The results showed that both sets were divided into four groups of IDRs, with members of each group exhibiting similar patterns of physicochemical features (Fig. 2C and figs. S4 to S6). For the puncta(+) IDRs, groups 1 and 3 (6 and 12 IDRs, respectively) are most highly enriched in features reporting on different types of molecular interactions (six features from LLPhyScore) and on aromatic residue content and prion-like domain content (three features from SAK). Puncta(+) IDR group 2 (13 IDRs) is weakly enriched in features related to aromatic residue content and prion-like domain content, and others reporting on charge balance (from SAK) and β sheet and coil secondary structure content (from AAIndex). In contrast, puncta(+) IDR group 4 (10 IDRs) exhibits moderate enrichment of charge-related features (four features from SAK) and otherwise mixed and weak feature enrichments and depletions.

  Average, groupwise feature enrichments for puncta(+) IDRs were accompanied by distinct patterns of average amino acid enrichments (see Materials and Methods), variably including enrichment of phenylalanine (group 1); tyrosine (groups 1 to 3); glycine, asparagine, and glutamine (group 1); and arginine and lysine (group 4; fig. S5, A and B), which are known from prior studies to be enriched in condensate-forming proteins (13, 17, 34, 40, 41). The puncta(−) IDR groups exhibited feature and amino acid enrichments that were either weaker than (groups 5 and 8) or different from (group 7) those of the puncta(+) IDRs, except for puncta(−) group 6, whose enrichments resemble those of puncta(+) IDR group 3 (fig. S6, A and B). However, the magnitude of the feature enrichments and depletions for group 3 puncta(+) IDRs are generally greater than those for group 6 puncta(−) IDRs (encoded as the red and blue color intensities in the feature heatmaps; Fig. 2C and figs. S4 and S5), suggesting that subtle differences in physicochemical feature profiles govern the condensation behavior of IDRs in these two groups. In summary, our results establish the physicochemical feature and amino acid enrichment landscape of FO-derived IDRs that do and do not form condensates in HEK293T cells.


### Predicting IDR cellular condensation using physicochemical features and ML
  We next asked whether our IDR dataset could be leveraged to accurately predict the condensation behavior of experimentally untested IDRs. Using 25 physicochemical features (Fig. 2B) for 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs as training data (termed the Training IDRs), we applied H2O AutoML (42) to evaluate 120 ML models for prediction of cellular IDR condensation behavior (Fig. 3A). A stacked ensemble model composed of three tree-based models was superior among the tested models (termed the IDR-Puncta ML model; see Materials and Methods) and displayed the following performance metrics based on 25-fold cross-validation (CV): AUC [area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve], 0.98; AUCPR (area under the precision-recall curve), 0.93; accuracy, 0.95; and balanced accuracy, 0.92 (Fig. 3B). Analysis using Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) (43) showed that diverse physicochemical features contribute to predictions of IDR condensate formation (see Materials and Methods), including those reporting on charge-related, disorder-related, and hydrophobicity-related properties (fig. S7A). We independently verified IDR-Puncta ML model performance using 30 human IDRs of the total 33 Verification IDRs (Fig. 3C; see Materials and Methods) and observed accuracy similar to that obtained during CV [AUC, 0.95; AUCPR, 0.88; accuracy, 0.90; and balanced accuracy, 0.92 (Fig. 3D)]. Confocal images of the 33 Verification IDRs were analyzed using the same threshold as the Training IDR set: IDRs were classified as puncta(+) if ≥24% of transfected cells showed puncta; otherwise, they were puncta(−). All classifications were manually validated (table S4). We excluded three Verification IDRs, which were experimentally categorized as either “nucleolar” or “other,” from the ML model performance evaluation. We next performed dimensionality reduction analysis using the 25 physicochemical features of the Training IDRs, Verification IDRs, and all IDRs (≥60 amino acids in length) from the human proteome. The analysis revealed that the physicochemical features of the FO-derived Training IDRs and Verification IDRs selected from the human IDRome spanned the feature landscape of the human IDRome, including the high-density regions (fig. S7, B and C). These results further confirm that the IDR-Puncta ML model can be applied to the human IDRome to understand the prevalence of puncta(+) IDRs in human proteins. The human proteome (20,396 proteins) contains 12,899 IDRs (derived from 8067 proteins; see Materials and Methods), and 1572 of these (12%) were predicted to be puncta(+) using the IDR-Puncta ML model (Fig. 3E and fig. S7D), suggesting that the ability to drive biomolecular condensate formation is not a general property of human IDRs but confined to a specialized subset within the human IDRome (25, 35). We have not applied our ML model to IDRs of other proteomes. The updated, openly accessible SAK3.0 web server (https://sak.stjude.org) performs the sequence-based analyses described in this study for exploring the physicochemical features and condensate formation propensities of other proteins and their IDRs, which are identified and analyzed separately. Furthermore, analysis of condensate predictions for the human IDRome at high confidence [e.g., condensate formation probability ranges (0.99, 1] for puncta(+) and (0, 2.28 × 10−7] for puncta(−) IDRs] reveals a distinct separation across the feature landscape (fig. S7E).

  Notably, we observed a substantial length bias, with puncta(+) IDRs being significantly longer than puncta(−) IDRs in both the Training IDRs and the human IDRome (fig. S8, A and B). These findings suggest that longer IDRs are more effective in promoting condensate formation, likely because they harbor a larger total number of interaction-prone regions that facilitate multivalent interactions. Although sequence length itself is not included as an explicit feature in the IDR-Puncta ML model, its influence is indirectly included through multiple length-dependent physicochemical features. Specifically, the features, protein-water interaction, protein-carbon interaction, number of negative and positive residues, ABT valence, and short-range electrostatic interaction, are strongly correlated with IDR length (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.7; fig. S8C). These features scale with the number of amino acids in an IDR and contribute to the model’s ability to capture length-related effects on condensate formation propensity. Consistent with this, the three Verification IDRs misclassified as puncta(+) are longer than all other experimentally determined puncta(−) IDRs in the Verification set (table S4), suggesting a potential length-related source of error that warrants further investigation into whether very long IDRs are more prone to false-positive predictions.


### Human proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are enriched for RNA processing–related functions
  Sequence mapping of the ML prediction results showed that 1393 human proteins contain one or more puncta(+) IDRs [some of these proteins also contain one or more puncta(−) IDRs] whereas 6674 proteins contain only puncta(−) IDRs (Fig. 3E). We next asked whether the human proteins that contain one or more puncta(+) IDR(s) are associated with specific biological functions using GO biological process enrichment analysis in comparison with all human proteins. A similar analysis was performed for human proteins containing only puncta(−) IDRs. Three of the top four most highly and significantly enriched parent biological process terms for proteins with puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs were similar (e.g., positive regulation of transcription, cell division, and actin cytoskeleton regulation; Fig. 4A), indicating that functions related to these processes are common to proteins with IDRs regardless of condensate formation. In contrast, the most highly enriched parent term for puncta(+) IDRs was RNA processing (3.5-fold enrichment; Fig. 4A), which aggregates numerous enriched child terms related to the regulation and processing of RNA, including metabolism and splicing (fig. S9A). We note that the magnitude of functional term enrichments was greater for proteins with puncta(+) than puncta(−) IDRs (Fig. 4A and fig. S9A). These results show that proteins involved in RNA-related processes are enriched in IDRs prone to condensate formation, raising the possibility that these processes often occur within condensate environments. Analysis of GO cellular component terms, which report on subcellular localization, showed that proteins with puncta(+) IDRs were enriched for terms including nucleolus, nuclear body, nuclear speck, nuclear protein–containing complex, and spliceosomal complex (Fig. 4B and fig. S9B), supporting the association of these proteins with several types of nuclear biomolecular condensates. The condensates associated terms (e.g., nucleolus, nuclear body, and nuclear speck) were not enriched in human proteins with puncta(−) IDRs (Fig. 4B and fig. S9B).

  In summary, our IDR-focused analyses indicate that 40% of human proteins (8067 of 20,396 proteins) exhibit one or more IDRs ≥60 amino acids in length, and these are enriched for functions associated with transcription, cell division, and actin cytoskeleton. The IDR-containing human proteins with potential for condensate formation [e.g., displaying puncta(+) IDRs; 1393 of 8067 proteins] are uniquely enriched in functional terms involving RNA-related processes and in localization terms associated with nuclear MLOs. Although others have previously associated condensate formation by proteins with RNA-related biological processes (7, 44–46), our findings extend these results by showing through large-scale, unbiased analyses that human proteins with condensation-prone IDRs are highly enriched for RNA-related biological functions and localization within nuclear MLOs.


### Proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are enriched in MLOs
  We independently tested our findings on preferential localization of human, puncta(+) IDR-containing proteins within biomolecular condensates by examining their occurrence in the MLO protein database, PhaSepDB (47), which compiles information on proteins demonstrated to undergo PS and/or reported to be associated with one or more MLOs. We retrieved 499 human MLO-associated proteins from PhaSepDB and identified 345 with one or more IDRs ≥60 amino acids in length. We also compiled a control dataset composed of membrane-associated proteins, which we reasoned do not autonomously form biomolecular condensates. Specifically, we examined proteins found at membrane-bound organelle contact sites [MCSs; compiled in MCSdb (48)], wherein membranes of two distinct organelles are in close proximity (typically within 10 to 80 nm), without undergoing membrane fusion (49). Proteins associated with MCSs facilitate direct, stable interorganelle communications and regulate the exchange of lipids, ions, and metabolites between organelles, such as the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, or the ER and plasma membrane (50, 51). Because of their structural rigidity and specialized roles requiring stable and specific interactions, we expect proteins in this collection not to be involved in biomolecular condensates. We obtained 199 human proteins from MCSdb and identified 90 with one or more IDRs. To enhance the robustness of our analyses, we only included proteins annotated with high confidence; our final MLO and MCS datasets included 345 and 90 human proteins, respectively, with only 2 proteins common between the two datasets (fig. S10; see Materials and Methods). We next applied our IDR-Puncta ML model to the two protein sets and determined that 134 of 345 MLO proteins (39%) and 5 of 90 MCS proteins (6%), respectively, contain one or more IDRs predicted to form condensates (Fig. 5A). In comparison, we reported above that 1393 of 8067 human IDR-containing proteins (17%) contain puncta(+) IDRs (Fig. 3E). As the nucleus is enriched in both proteins with known MLO associations and proteins with IDRs predicted to form condensates, we examined the proportion of nuclear MLO proteins that contain condensate-prone IDRs and found that they are especially prevalent. A total of 134 of 315 IDR containing proteins involved in nuclear MLOs (43%) were predicted to have at least one such IDR, compared to 759 of 3295 IDR-containing proteins in the nucleus (23%) (Fig. 5B). These results show that proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are much more highly enriched in MLO proteins than in MCS proteins or human IDR-containing proteins, supporting our independent findings based on analysis of GO cellular component localization terms (Fig. 4B and fig. S9B). Although the occurrence of a puncta(+) IDR within the sequence does not necessarily indicate that a protein will autonomously form condensates, it is highly suggestive of localization within an MLO. The 134 MLO proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are predominantly associated with nuclear MLOs, although some are associated with cytoplasmic MLOs, including stress granules and P-bodies (Fig. 5C). These proteins are most frequently associated with nuclear speckles, nucleoli, and paraspeckles, MLOs involved in different RNA-related processes, consistent with our observation that the functional term, RNA processing, was the most highly enriched amongst human proteins with puncta(+) IDRs (Fig. 4A and fig. S9A). These findings highlight a strong correlation between proteins containing puncta(+) IDRs and their preferential localization within MLOs, particularly those with roles in RNA-related processes. This relationship underscores the importance of condensate-driven compartmentalization in organizing and regulating RNA metabolism, indicating how MLOs support complex biochemical processes.


## DISCUSSION
  Protein PS plays a key role in cellular organization and function (13, 52). The ability to accurately predict PS propensities of proteins is critical to understand cellular compartmentalization and its roles in biology and disease. Recent advances fostered the development of several computational tools, each leveraging different approaches to analyze protein PS (21, 23, 30, 31). However, the lack of standardized PS testing methods and robust PS negative datasets introduces implicit and difficult-to-measure biases among these different prediction methods (33). Furthermore, many of these predictors use literature-mined datasets from different databases, which often exhibit inconsistencies and uncertainty due to nonstandardized experimental conditions. We previously developed the FO-Puncta ML model to predict the cellular condensation behavior of FOs (34). However, FOs differ from human proteins due to their unique sequence features, which result from aberrant gene translocation. As a result, the model is not optimized to recognize the broader sequence-based features associated with PS-prone human proteins. In addition, this model is designed to assess the condensation behavior of full-length proteins and does not identify which specific region(s) of the FO drives this behavior. Because many proteins harbor multiple IDRs, only some of which may contribute to PS, identifying the exact IDR(s) responsible for condensate formation is crucial. Such specificity not only illuminates the molecular basis of PS but also enables targeted sequence modifications. An improved understanding of key features of PS-prone IDRs, such as amino acid composition, charge distribution, and potential for multivalent interactions, is essential for rationally controlling the phase behavior of proteins.

  Building on these advances, we developed the IDR-Puncta ML model, an ML tool that accurately predicts the condensate-forming potential of IDRs, based on sequence-derived physicochemical features. The IDR-Puncta ML model was trained on experimentally tested IDRs using standardized cellular assays and does not report on the specific type (homotypic or heterotypic) of interactions that may drive condensate formation. Our model addresses limitations that may have variably affected existing tools, such as unvalidated datasets, lack of cellular context, potential overfitting due to feature redundancy, and narrowly focused features. We experimentally validated the IDR-Puncta ML model using randomly selected IDRs from the human proteome, confirming its accuracy and broad applicability. IDRs annotated as “nucleolar” (n = 15) or “other” (n = 22), based on their subcellular localization and/or irregular appearance, were excluded from model development. These categories contained relatively small numbers and their inclusion would have introduced substantial class imbalance, potentially compromising model performance. To enhance robustness and ensure a clearly defined IDR classification, we excluded these IDRs from both model training and performance evaluation. This allowed us to focus the analysis on puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs, which exhibited clearly defined morphological features and had robust representation in the dataset. Moreover, the nucleolus is a functionally distinct and extensively studied MLO. Given the depth of existing research focused specifically on nucleolar PS (53–56), we excluded nucleolar IDRs to focus our analysis on non-nucleolar IDRs to better define the sequence and physicochemical features governing condensate formation outside of the nucleolus.

  von Bülow et al. (35) recently developed an ML model by combining coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations that match experimental saturation concentrations with active learning and predicted that 5% of IDRs in the human proteome undergo homotypic PS under physiologically relevant conditions [e.g., sequences with transfer free energy values (ΔGtr) less than −2kBT (35)]. In contrast, the ML model developed in this work predicts cellular condensate formation and was solely trained on puncta classification of expressed GFP-tagged IDRs obtained from confocal microscopy images. Thus, our IDR-Puncta ML model and that of von Bülow et al. (35) were developed using very different training datasets. The von Bülow et al. (35) model focuses on homotypic interactions, whereas our IDR-Puncta ML model addresses cellular condensation behavior, which can occur through both homotypic and heterotypic interactions. As these approaches are distinct but complementary, we applied the von Bülow et al. (35) homotypic PS predictor to our set of 41 puncta(+) (see Materials and Methods) IDRs to gain additional insight. We found that group 1 IDRs are predicted to undergo homotypic PS, with a mean ΔGtr value of −4.89 kBT (fig. S11). Group 3 IDRs have a mean ΔGtr value of −1.87 kBT, indicating a moderate tendency toward homotypic PS. Conversely, IDRs from group 2 (mean ΔGtr = −0.82 kBT) and group 4 (mean ΔGtr = −0.29 kBT) are less likely to undergo homotypic PS (fig. S11). These trends align with the distinct sequence feature enrichment observed in each group (fig. S4). Groups 1 and 3 show similar feature enrichment patterns, although the enrichment is less pronounced in group 3. Groups 2 and 4 display unique feature enrichment profiles, distinguishing them from groups 1 and 3. These results highlight variability in PS propensities across different IDR groups. We hypothesize that puncta(+) IDRs, exhibiting distinct patterns of physicochemical features and amino acid enrichments (e.g., IDRs in groups 1 to 4; Fig. 3A), will display different conformational properties and engage in various intra- and inter-polypeptide chain interactions, both homotypic and heterotypic, that drive condensate formation. However, testing this hypothesis requires further investigation in the future.

  We explored the biological implications of protein condensate formation by conducting a detailed functional annotation of proteins containing condensate-forming IDRs. Using our predictive model, we identified 1393 proteins containing at least one predicted puncta(+) IDR (~17% of all IDR-containing human proteins). These findings align with studies suggesting that PS driven by IDRs alone is limited, with additional factors such as folded domains influencing condensate formation (25, 57). Our data indicate that proteins with predicted puncta(+) IDRs are highly enriched in RNA-related biological processes, specifically RNA processing and splicing. It is increasingly evident that RNA-related processes (including transcription, splicing, and translation) are frequently associated with condensate formation (4, 45, 58–61). Furthermore, the proteins with puncta(+) IDRs are enriched in nuclear speckles, nucleoli, and paraspeckles, MLOs known to facilitate various RNA-related processes. These data serve as a valuable resource for hypothesis-driven research into the roles and mechanisms of condensate formation by IDRs in RNA biology by providing insight into patterns of physicochemical features in IDRs and their cellular condensation behavior.

  Our study establishes a framework for examining the link between IDR-mediated protein condensation and biological function. Herein, we present a valuable resource for mapping the potential of human IDRs to form condensates in cells, offering insights into their physicochemical features and associated biological functions, especially in the context of PS. We anticipate that our prediction model will be useful in guiding cellular experiments to explore the role of other untested IDRs in PS. Furthermore, it can also be used to provide key insights into condensate pathology. For example, aberrant condensate formation has been associated with several human diseases, especially neurodegeneration and cancer (3, 4, 15, 62–64). Identifying specific IDRs that drive pathological condensate formation can aid in finding the association between PS and disease progression. As our model was trained on FO-derived IDRs, it is particularly suitable to analyze the cancer proteome. Identifying puncta-forming IDRs in cancer-associated proteins will provide critical insights into their possible oncogenic mechanism. The IDR-Puncta ML holds substantial potential in interpreting the impact of disease-associated mutations in IDRs that alter cellular PS behavior for understanding complex diseases, including neurodegenerative disease and cancer (2, 65). Beyond individual IDRs, our model can also be applied to a proteome-wide assessment of IDR-mediated PS across different organisms to explore the prospect of PS being an evolutionary conserved mechanism in regulating diverse cellular processes and stress response (66). The IDR-Puncta ML model can also be applied in synthetic biology and biomaterials engineering. Our work provides refined knowledge of sequence features associated with condensate-prone IDRs and tools to design and modify PS-prone IDRs, which will assist the engineering of synthetic protein condensates and biomaterials with variable phase-separating properties (67, 68).


### Limitations of our study
  We note a few factors to be considered when evaluating our findings and conclusions. First, the training set for our model used IDRs from a subset of FOs, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other proteins or IDRs with different characteristics. However, our analyses showed that the physicochemical features of the FO-derived IDRs we tested spanned the feature landscape of the human IDRome, mitigating this concern. Second, we applied stringent criteria to identify IDRs, in general, requiring a minimum length of 60 amino acids and requiring puncta formation in more than 24% of cells to score an IDR as puncta(+). Although these thresholds were chosen to ensure high confidence in identifying condensate-forming IDRs, they may have also excluded shorter IDRs or those with lower levels of cellular puncta formation, potentially overlooking some IDRs with genuine condensate-forming capacity. However, the scarcity of sequences <60 amino acids in length in established databases cataloging PS proteins suggests that they are relatively uncommon. Third, we focused on IDR-driven condensate formation of proteins and excluded PS promoted by folded domains (oligomerization domains and nucleic acid binding domains) (59, 69) as well as PS facilitated by multiple IDRs working in concert. These factors limit our ability to identify all condensation-prone proteins in the human proteome. Moreover, we segregated IDRs that flank a folded domain and tested them individually, potentially overlooking the enhanced effects of multiple IDRs acting together. The phase behavior of full-length proteins can be influenced by other domains or sequence elements beyond the tested IDRs. In addition, environmental factors such as pH, crowding, and the presence of cofactors, possibly dependent on cellular subcompartment localization, affect the driving forces of condensation. The presence of nuclear import or export signals in full-length parent proteins can cause localization in cellular regions different from those of the IDR alone, lacking such signals. Differential cellular localization, compared with that of the parent protein, may mask or accentuate the driving forces for condensate formation incurred by an IDR. Our approach identifies IDRs with intrinsic condensation potential but does not capture the full complexity of synergies with localization signals, other IDRs, and/or folded domains in native parent proteins. Thus, the observation of a puncta(+) IDR does not guarantee condensate formation by the associated parent protein nor does its absence rule it out. Our predictive framework is designed to identify IDRs with condensate formation potential upon expression in cells, but it does not provide detailed insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying condensate formation by IDRs. Condensate formation is inherently context dependent, influenced by environmental factors and by both homotypic and heterotypic interactions, and complementary analyses are needed to understand these complexities. In addition, although we identified physicochemical feature patterns linked to puncta(+) behavior in IDRs, we did not explore how these patterns affect IDR conformational dynamics or multivalent interactions, both crucial for condensate formation.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### IDR selection for in-cell expression
  IDRs were identified by analyzing the 149 FO sequences in our previous work (34) using sak.stjude.org, which identified IDRs on the basis of continuous stretches of disordered regions ≥ 60 residues with an 11-residue moving average with disorder propensity > 0.45, according to the IUPRED2A algorithm (36). In addition, regions <12 residues apart were joined into single regions, including up to 11 additional residues. Sequences were also analyzed with Metapredict (version: V2) (37), and Metapredict-identified IDRs were added to the IDR database when there was no analogous IUPRED2A-identified IDR. In some cases, IUPred2A and Metapredict identified multiple adjacent IDRs that did not form puncta. Combined IDRs containing all the adjacent disordered regions were created and tested in these cases. A total of 215 unique IDRs were identified, among which 188 were predicted using SAK, 23 with Metapredict, and 4 IDRs were identified by both IUPred2A and Metapredict. An IDR sequence length threshold of ≥60 residues was established after assessing the length profile of protein sequences that are known to be associated with PS or are members of biomolecular condensates. We determined that there are no sequences shorter than 60 residues present in the PhaSePro (20) [n = 121] or LLPSDB (70) [n = 190] databases. In addition, we found only 14 protein sequences (1.4 × 10−2%) with length < 60 residues in the CD-CODE (71) [n = 9792] collection that are members of biomolecular condensates and 1 protein sequence with <60 residues associated with PS in the PhaSepDB (47) [n = 840] database.


### Cloning
  We previously reported Escherichia coli codon-optimized full-length plasmids for cellular expression of FOs (34). IDRs and FO-IDR deletion mutants were generated using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the respective FOs as templates. PCR reactions were performed using Q5 High Fidelity 2x Mastermix, and primers were designed with 5′-Not1 and 3′-Xba1 restriction sites. PCR fragments and the destination vector (CL20) were cut with Not1 and Xba1 restriction enzymes and ligated using New England BioLabs’ Quick Ligation Kit per the manufacturer’s instructions. Ligation reactions were transformed into chemically competent bacteria (NEB 5-alpha). All plasmid sequences were confirmed by whole-plasmid sequencing.


### Cell culture and transient transfections
  HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection; RRID: CVCL_0063) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Gibco) and supplemented with 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone), and 6 mM l-glutamine (Gibco) and maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were tested for Mycoplasma every 2 months using PCR (e-Myco plus, LiLiF). Cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling (PowerPlex Fusion at the St. Jude Hartwell Center). Cells were transfected in a 96-well plate with 100 ng of plasmid DNA in the CL20 vector backbone using FuGENE HD (Promega) per the manufacturer’s instructions. All IDRs were N-terminally tagged with monomeric EGFP (A207K mutation in EGFP), and EGFP was used for the empty vector control plasmids. Cells were used for a maximum of 25 passages after thawing.


### Confocal microscopy imaging
  All microscopy images were acquired on a 3i Marianas system (Denver, CO) configured with a Yokogawa CSU-W spinning disk confocal microscope using a 100x Zeiss objective, 405 nm (Hoechst) and 488 nm (mEGFP) laser lines, and Slidebook (RRID: SCR_014300) 6.0 (3i). 3D images of cells were captured as z stacks with 0.3-μm spacing between planes, spanning 12 μm in total. Live HEK293T cells were imaged at 37°C in phenol red–free DMEM with high glucose (Gibco) supplemented with 1× penicillin/streptomycin, 10% FBS, 6 mM l-glutamine, and 25 mM Hepes.


### Quantitative image analysis to classify IDRs as puncta(+), puncta(−), Other, and Nucleolar
  Fluorescence microscopy images (images of at least 50 cells were recorded for each IDR) were segmented and quantified using the PunctaTools pipeline (38). In brief, cells were segmented using mEGFP as the primary signal and Hoechst as a secondary signal to establish nuclei. Cells were segmented as sets of 10 layers to minimize cell-segmentation errors and then combined into 3D stacks using the Cellpose algorithm (72). Condensates (termed puncta here) were then segmented by filtering respective channels with a scale-adapted LoG filter, thresholding the result, and applying watershed segmentation using the maxima of the LoG filtered image as seeds. PunctaTools computes a partition coefficient (Kp) for each cell, defined as the ratio of mean fluorescence intensity inside puncta to the mean intensity of the background, and uses a conservative cutoff (Kp > 2) to avoid assigning noise as puncta. Cells were recorded as puncta(+) or puncta(−), and constructs were called puncta(+) if ≥24% of expressing cells were positive, a threshold chosen for agreement with manual assessment. After quantification, IDR puncta status was manually verified by two independent researchers to assign final classifications to each IDR. In several cases, automated assignments were corrected via a structured manual review using a standardized taxonomy: false positives arising from cytoplasmic aggregates, nucleolar segmentation, or nuclear envelope oversegmentation driven by strong cytoplasm versus nucleus intensity differences; and false negatives arising from low apparent Kp (just below the 2.0 cutoff; despite consistent observation of discrete puncta across cells), blurred (“fuzzy”)/indistinct puncta boundaries, or relatively low nuclear versus cytoplasmic expression causing undersegmentation of nuclear puncta. All manual corrections and justifications are reported in table S1. For all puncta(+) and Other/Nucleolar IDRs, we conducted at least two biological replicates. For the puncta(−) IDRs, we performed two biological replicates on a randomly selected 20% subset. For every imaged IDR and protein, at least five representative cellular images and their corresponding PunctaTool segmentations are available in the BioImage Archive (accession S-BIAD2260).


### Calculation of amino acid sequence–derived physicochemical features of IDRs
  To understand the potential of condensate formation by IDRs, we deployed physicochemical features that were computed for each IDR amino acid sequence. Specifically, we obtained features from our previous work (34) through the SAK pipeline (sak.stjude.org), the amino acid index (AAindex) database (39), and the recently developed LLPhyScore resource (21). We accessed amino acid properties from the AAindex database, which is a curated set of numerical indices representing various physicochemical and biochemical properties of amino acids. Additional features were obtained from the LLPhyScore resource, which includes a predictor of IDR-driven phase separating proteins based on underlying physicochemical interactions, including solvent contacts, disorder, hydrogen bonds, π-π and cation-π contacts, electrostatic interactions, and secondary structure. The Python package protlearn (version: 0.0.3) was used to extract 553 amino acid properties from the AAindex database with no missing values that belong to 12 property classes (25) (fig. S3, A and B). The features from LLPhyScore were computed using the standalone package from GitHub (github.com/julie-forman-kay-lab/LLPhyScore) on the basis of the model trained on both folded proteins in the PDB and proteins from the human proteome.


### Human IDRome sequences
  To represent the human IDRome, we first obtained all human Swiss-Prot (reviewed) proteins from Uniprot release 2023_04. Sequences were excluded from the collection if their length was less than 10 residues or if any characters outside of the natural amino acids were present. This resulted in a total of 20,396 human protein sequences (termed the human proteome here). We identified 13,047 IDR sequences with lengths ≥60 residues (from 8067 human proteins) using the IUPRED2A algorithm accessed via the sak.stjude.org resource; this collection is referred to as the human IDRome. The same criteria were used to identify IDRs within FOs, as described above in the “IDR selection for in-cell expression” section.


### Calculation of amino acid enrichment for IDR sequences
  Amino acid enrichment for each IDR sequence was calculated using Eq. 1AAEnrichment=[100×(Composition of sequenceMean composition of human IDRome)]−100%(1)where “Composition of sequence” is the composition of a particular amino acid in the sequence being evaluated and “Mean composition of human IDRome” is the mean composition of a particular amino acid in a database of human IDR sequences (human IDRome). The amino acid composition describes the fraction of each amino acid type within a protein sequence. Amino acid compositions of the IDR sequences were computed using the extractAAC() function from the protr (73) package (version: 1.7.0) in R.


### Analysis of sequence-derived physicochemical features
  First, we identified that for the 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) Expressed IDRs (collectively termed the Training IDRs; 178 IDRs, in total) values of the 38 features obtained from sak.stjude.org, values of the features, “ABT valence,” “ABT balance,” and “ABT density,” were missing for ~5% of IDRs, whereas “PAPAprop” values were missing in ~8% of IDRs, due to the limitation of the IDR length requirement for the calculation of these features. Therefore, we replaced all the missing values using the nonmissing median values of these features from the puncta(+) and puncta(−) Expressed IDRs, respectively. We next performed the two-sided t test for the 38 features from sak.stjude.org and identified 21 features that showed significant differences (P value ≤ 0.01) between the 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs using the rstatix package (version: 0.7.2) in R (version: 4.2.1). Similarly, we identified 10 features from the AAindex database (with the most significant feature difference from each of the 12 classes) and 7 features from LLPhyScore (of the total of 9 LLPhyScore features) that showed significant differences (P value ≤ 0.01) between the 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs. Effect size was calculated using the effsize package (version: 0.8.1) (https://zenodo.org/record/196082) in R. Next, to identify interdependence of the 38 sequence-based physicochemical features for the 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs, we computed MI among these features using infotheo (version: 1.2.0.1) package in R. Features that displayed strong mutual dependence with others were removed, which resulted in 25 features with low MI (≤0.5). These 25 physicochemical features were converted to z-scores using the scale function in the R package, with respect to the human IDRome sequences. We next performed hierarchical clustering on the basis of Euclidean distance and using the complete linkage method, as implemented in the pheatmap package (version: 1.0.12) in R, using the z-scores for the noted 25 features to identify groups within the 41 puncta(+) IDR set with related physicochemical features. For the 137 puncta(−) IDR set, we performed hierarchical clustering on the basis of Manhattan distance and Ward’s minimum variance method, using the z-scores of the 25 physicochemical features.


### Supervised ML for puncta classification
  We used the automatic machine learning (AutoML) tool within the h2o.ai (42) (version: 3.44.0.1) package in R to classify the puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs and predict the probability of condensate formation using data for the 178 Training IDRs [consisting 41 puncta(+) and 137 puncta(−) IDRs from the Expressed IDRs]. Using the 25 sequence-based physicochemical features with low MI for the 178 Training IDRs, we set nfolds = 25 for 25-fold CV and generated 120 models from AutoML. In addition, we set include_algos to the H2O tree-based models [Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Distributed Random Forest (DRF) including Extremely Randomized Trees (XRT), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)] and Stacked Ensembles, using otherwise default parameters in H2O AutoML. A “Best of Family” Stacked Ensemble Model consisting of three base models GBM, XRT, and DRF performed the best amongst the 120 models tested (termed the IDR-Puncta ML model). The Stacked Ensemble metalearner using an Elastic net regularized (with CV) Generalized Linear Model (GLM) resulted in the ML model with GLM coefficients, 0.62, 0.46, and 0.18 for the three base models GBM, XRT, and DRF, respectively. Performance of the model was based on the metrics logistic loss or cross-entropy loss (the difference between predicted probabilities and actual values) value of 0.17 for the 25-fold CV set with 178 Training IDRs. We determined a threshold value for the condensate formation probability of 0.40 on the basis of a maximum accuracy value of 0.95, maximum F1 score (harmonic mean of the precision and recall) value of 0.89, and maximum absolute Matthew’s correlation coefficient value of 0.86 for classifying the puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRs for the 25-fold CV set with 178 Training IDRs. After its establishment, we applied the IDR-Puncta ML model to 30 Verification IDRs [with experimentally determined puncta(+) and puncta(−) status] of the total 33 Verification IDRs from human IDRome. We excluded three Verification IDRs, which were experimentally determined as either “nucleolar” or “other” from the ML model performance evaluation. Verification IDRs were chosen first by randomly selecting IDRs from the human IDRome until a panel of 20 IDRs was generated, which had a length distribution similar to the set of 215 IDRs according to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (P value < 0.004). Of these, 19 shared <20% sequence identity and one had <55% identity with any of the 215 Training IDRs. However, only 2 of 20 Verification IDRs were experimentally observed to be puncta(+). Therefore, 13 additional Verification IDRs were randomly selected such that their predicted condensate formation probability using the IDR-Puncta ML model ranged from 0.39 to 1, with 0.40 being the puncta(+) classification threshold. These additional 13 Verification IDRs exhibited <10% sequence identity with any of the 215 Training IDRs. Overall, the 33 Verification IDRs were chosen to have a low degree of sequence identity on the basis of pairwise alignment (<55% for one sequence and <20% for the remaining 32 sequences) with the Training IDRs, performed as follows. To measure the degree of similarity between the IDR sequences in the Training and Verification sets, we performed pairwise sequence alignment using protein BLAST (version: BLAST+ 2.14.1). We defined the pairwise normalized alignment score, fraction of identical matches asfident=2.nident/(qlen+slen)(2)where qlen is the length of the query sequence, slen is the length of the subject sequence, and nident is the fraction of identical matches between the query and subject sequences. fident varies between 0 and 1, with 0 indicating no identical residues between the query and subject sequence, and 1 indicating an exact match between the query and subject sequences. We used the package PRROC (version: 1.3.1) (74) in R to compute the performance metrics AUC and AUCPR for the Verification IDRs. We next applied SHAP analysis (43) to determine the importance of the 25 physicochemical features to IDR-Puncta ML model predictions. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach to explain the output of any ML model. First, we computed Shapley values (SHAPvalue) of the features for each of the 178 Training IDRs using the h2o.predcit_contribution function in the h2o.ai package in R. Next, we computed SHAP importance for each feature from the base GBM model with the highest contribution to the IDR-Puncta ML model asSHAPimportance=1n.∑k=1n∣SHAPvalue∣(3)where n is the number of IDRs in the Training set and |SHAPvalue| measures the importance of each feature for the model decisions.


### Dimensionality reduction analysis of the Training IDRs and human IDRome
  To validate whether the IDR-Puncta ML model can be applied to the human IDRome for predicting the probability of condensate formation and puncta classification. We performed dimensionality reduction analysis by applying Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (75) using the 25 physicochemical features of the Training IDRs and human IDRome combined. The UMAP algorithm aims to preserve both the local and the global data structure. The IDRs from human IDRome with missing feature(s) were excluded from the UMAP analysis. The feature values of the Training IDRs and human IDRome were converted to z-scores before applying UMAP. UMAP analysis was performed using the uwot package (version: 0.1.12) in R with the following parameters: n_neighbors = 100, metric = “euclidean”, min_dist = 1, and spread = 5.


### GO enrichment analysis of human IDRome
  GO enrichment analysis of the human IDRome was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (76) Knowledgebase (version: v2024q1) in the web server (davidbioinformatics.nih.gov). For the predicted puncta(+) IDRome, proteins with at least one puncta(+) IDR were used, whereas for the predicted puncta(−) IDRome, proteins with only puncta(−) IDRs were used, and proteins from the full human proteome were used as the background set. The lowest level of GO categories for biological process (GO_BP_ALL), cellular component (GO_CC_ALL), and molecular function (GO_MF_ALL) were used in DAVID. The adjusted P value (false discovery rate) cutoff of ≤0.05 based on Benjamini correction (default in DAVID) and fold enrichment of ≥1.5 were used to filter the GO terms. In addition, GO terms containing ≥2000 proteins from the background set (human IDRome) and GO terms containing <10 proteins from the target sets [predicted puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRomes] were removed. The rrvgo (77) package (version: 1.10.0) was used to simplify the redundancy of GO terms by grouping similar terms on the basis of their semantic similarity using the “Wang” method with similarity threshold values of 0.88 and 0.94 for the predicted puncta(+) and puncta(−) IDRomes, respectively. “Wang” is a graph-based method implemented in the rrvgo R package, which uses the topology of GO graph structure to compute semantic similarity (78). This method determines the semantic similarity of two GO terms on the basis of both locations of these terms in the GO graph and their relations with their ancestor terms. Higher thresholds lead to fewer groups of GO terms. For visualization purposes, the combined adjusted P value of a grouped GO term from semantic similarity was obtained by applying the sum of logs (Fisher’s) method using the metap (version: 1.11) R package. A log2 transformation was used to calculate the average fold enrichment of grouped GO terms.


### Puncta prediction of the IDRs from proteins in MLOs and MCSs and Human Protein Atlas
  Protein constituents of MLOs were obtained from the manually curated database of PS-related proteins [PhaSepDB; version: 2.1 (47)]. This database contains 499 human proteins identified by low-throughput methods, termed “MLO-lt,” that matched UniProt IDs found in our human proteome database. Of these 499 proteins, 345 were mapped onto our human IDRome database. We identified 199 human proteins from a manually curated database of experimentally supported MCS proteins and complexes [MCSdb (48)] labeled with “low-throughput experimental methods.” Two of the 199 proteins were not found in our human proteome database, and for the remaining 197 proteins, 90 proteins were mapped onto our human IDRome database. We obtained the subcellular location of 13,534 genes from The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (79), and after removing 781 genes with “Uncertain” reliability, 12,364 proteins were identified by mapping the Ensemble gene ID to our human proteome database. Of these 12,364 proteins, 5814 proteins were mapped onto our human IDRome database, and 5689 proteins were identified as “nuclear” proteins. Nuclear proteins from HPA were identified from one or more of the assigned main locations: “Nucleoplasm,” “Nuclear speckles,” “Nucleolar fibrillar center,” “Nuclear membrane,” “Nuclear bodies,” “Nucleoli,” or “Nucleoli rim.” Significance of the number of proteins containing puncta(+) or only puncta(−) IDRs in MLO, MCs, and human IDRome datasets was computed from Fisher’s exact test for count data using fisher.test from the stats (version: 4.2.1) package in R.


### Homotypic PS prediction
  Prediction of homotypic PS was performed using the Colab notebook (https://colab.research.google.com/github/KULL-Centre/_2024_buelow_PSpred/blob/main/PSLab.ipynb) provided by von Bülow et al. (35), which provides predicted IDR transfer free energies and saturation concentrations from protein sequences.
