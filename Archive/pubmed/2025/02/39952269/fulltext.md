# Membrane Association of Intrinsically Disordered Proteins


## Abstract
  It is now clear that membrane association of intrinsically disordered proteins or intrinsically disordered regions regulates many cellular processes, such as membrane targeting of Src family kinases and ion channel gating. Residue-specific characterization by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, molecular dynamics simulations, and other techniques has shown that polybasic motifs and amphipathic helices are the main drivers of membrane association; sequence-based prediction of residue-specific membrane association propensity has become possible. Membrane association facilitates protein–protein interactions and protein aggregation—these effects are due to reduced dimensionality but are similar to those afforded by condensate formation via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). LLPS at the membrane surface provides a powerful means for recruiting and clustering proteins, as well as for membrane remodeling.


## INTRODUCTION
  Membranes define a confined environment for cells and their organelles but are also where many cellular processes take place. Membranes are bilayer assemblies of lipids with a thickness of ~50 Å, where the lipid headgroups face the aqueous environment and the lipid acyl chains form a hydrophobic core. Transmembrane proteins are permanently localized in membranes; many other proteins spend much of their lifetime at membrane surfaces. In the latter case, intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) are particularly versatile in membrane association. Even transmembrane proteins often have IDRs that associate with membranes (72).

  Proteins can attach themselves to cell membranes by various modes (Figure 1). Transmembrane proteins have a transmembrane domain, in the form of an α-helix bundle (or a single a-helix; Figure 1a) or a β-barrel, that spans the thickness of a membrane. Many proteins rely on a cytoplasmic, disordered region that forms a membrane-bound amphipathic helix for membrane attachment (Figure 1b). Whereas a transmembrane helix is dominated by nonpolar residues, which are favored by the hydrophobic core of the membrane, an amphipathic helix has nonpolar residues lining one face but polar and charged residues lining the opposite face (Figure 2a). Other proteins rely on IDRs that remain disordered and form fuzzy membrane association, where membrane-contacting residues are in constant change (Figure 1c). Instead of an IDR, some proteins use a folded domain for membrane association (Figure 1d). One other mode of membrane attachment is through lipidation, i.e., posttranslational modifications where a lipid group is conjugated to a side chain (e.g., Cys) or an N- or C-terminal Gly (Figure 1e). Typical lipid groups used for protein lipidation include geranylgeranyl, palmitate, and myristate; the site of lipidation frequently occurs in an IDR. In particular, the Src family kinases use myristoylation for plasma membrane targeting (112). Last, proteins that are attached to membranes can serve as anchors to other proteins (Figure 1f–i). This indirect mode allows the latter proteins to be at the membrane surface. Often, proteins simultaneously use more than one mode of membrane attachment. For example, SepF binds membranes via both an N-terminal amphipathic helix and the subsequent IDR (30) (Figure 1b). The membrane binding via a single lipid modification may be too weak, so often another mode, including a second lipid modification, fuzzy association by the IDR harboring the lipidation site, and binding to a membrane anchor, is also involved (112).

  The intersection of intrinsic disorder and membrane attachment has been covered by a number of recent reviews. Kjaergaard & Kragelund (72) reviewed transmembrane proteins with IDRs. They cited two sequence motifs, one enriched in basic residues and the other being the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) (two YxxL/YxxI sequences separated by six to eight residues), that bind membranes and noted that membrane binding can be switched off by, for example, phosphorylation or a change in local lipid composition. Fakhree et al. (40) reviewed how membrane-bound IDPs can modulate membrane properties, including membrane curvature, local lipid composition, and bridging between two membranes. Curvature sensing and generation by IDP–membrane association are also the subject of three other reviews (9, 66, 73) and a part of a fourth review (58). The last review additionally covered membrane-disruption mechanisms of antimicrobial peptides. Snead & Eliezer (117) reviewed the roles of IDPs in SNARE-mediated vesicle fusion. Cornish et al. (27) reviewed the roles of IDP–membrane association in cellular processes, including cross-membrane signal transduction. Popelka & Uversky (105) reviewed the different membrane attachment modes of IDRs in yeast autophagy-related proteins. Pathak et al. (100) reviewed membrane interactions of amyloidogenic IDPs. Last, Case et al. (22) reviewed cross-membrane signal amplification by liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS).

  This review focuses on the physical understanding of IDP–membrane association and the mechanistic understanding of cellular processes involving IDP-mediated membrane attachment, generated from studies from the past few years. We pay particular attention to IDPs for which residue-level or even atomic level information has been obtained, including those that have been studied both experimentally and by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The membrane-associated actions to be covered include protein activation and LLPS.


## REPRESENTATIVE IDPs
  Cell membranes contain over 1,000 types of lipids (26). Many are zwitterionic but some are negatively charged, including phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), and cardiolipin. Phosphatidylserine and PIP2 are present in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane to make it acidic. Likewise, the mitochondrial inner membrane is enriched in cardiolipin. Below, we choose a representative set of IDPs to highlight their regions and domains that mediate membrane binding or interactions with other proteins.

  N-WASP (Figure 2b) has a basic region that binds the C-terminal acidic region to help maintain the protein in an autoinhibited state (97). PIP2 binding to the basic region contributes to its activation (63, 109, 113). NMDA receptors are a subtype of ionotropic glutamate receptors that form a tetrameric ion channel (Figure 1a) gated by agonists (glycine binding to GluN1 subunits and glutamate binding to GluN2 subunits). Both GluN1 and GluN2 have long disordered cytoplasmic C-terminal domains (CTDs) (Figure 2c). MD simulations predicted that the GluN1 CTD has a strong propensity to associate with acidic membranes, especially via basic residues in the region encoded by exon 21 (111). Dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) (Figure 2d) contains a long disordered region known as the variable domain (VD) (with varying lengths in different isoforms). The VD binds cardiolipin-containing membranes (86). The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is regulated by acidic lipids through interactions with basic residues in the first half of its juxtamembrane region (JM-A) (127) (Figure 2e). SepF, FtsQ, and ChiZ (Figure 2f–h) are components of the bacterial cell division machinery (divisome). SepF has an amphipathic helix, whereas FtsQ and ChiZ have a transmembrane helix for membrane attachment. In addition, polybasic motifs in IDRs of ChiZ and SepF and an Arg/Ala-rich amphipathic helix in FtsQ form further association with acidic membranes (30, 62, 116). Zona occludens proteins (Figure 2i) contain multiple domains and phase separate at the membrane surface (16). Their PDZ1 and PDZ2 domains are responsible for membrane localization, through anchoring to adhesion receptors of the claudin family and binding to PIP2, respectively. Synaptotagmin-1 (Figure 2j) is tethered in the vesicle membrane via a transmembrane helix and has two folded domains, C2A and C2B, that, when loaded with Ca2+, bind to the vesicle (or perhaps even plasma) membrane (Figure 1d). The linker between the transmembrane helix and C2A has a basic region at one end and an acidic region at the other end. Complexins (Figure 2k) bind the t-SNARE complex via their central and accessory helices and membranes via their C-terminal amphipathic helix (56). Amylin (also called islet amyloid polypeptide), tau, α-synuclein, Aβ40, and Aβ42 (Figure 2l–o) are amyloidogenic proteins. The first three use amphipathic helices for membrane insertion (7, 45, 52). Additionally, for tau, membrane anchoring may occur through annexin binding via an acidic region near the extreme N terminus (51) and Src family kinase binding via the proline-rich region (77). Aβ40 and Aβ42 typically form two β-sheets (named N-sheet and C-sheet) upon forming aggregates or fibrils (48); similar β-sheets may be formed in the membrane environment (71).


## PHYSICAL RULES GOVERNING MEMBRANE ASSOCIATION OF IDPs

### NMR Spectroscopy
  Among the various experimental techniques for characterizing IDP–membrane association, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) remains the most powerful and widely used. Solution NMR can reveal specific residues that bind with membranes by chemical shift perturbations or attenuation in crosspeak intensity (I/I0) upon adding liposomes to the IDP solution. Such measurements have revealed that the disordered CTDs of metabotropic glutamate receptor 2 (mGluR2) and mGluR3, which are family C G protein–coupled receptors, associate with acidic membranes mainly via the N-terminal basic region (87). For the Drp1 VD, solution NMR reported membrane binding residues in two predicted molecular recognition features (MoRFs): an amphipathic helix in the first MoRF and a short sequence, W552RG554, in the second MoRF; the latter is required for cardiolipin binding (86). By searching for cocrystal structures of membrane proteins and cardiolipin, the structure of an ADP/ATP carrier with a WRG sequence in contact with cardiolipin [Protein Data Bank identifier (PDB ID): 1OKC] was found. The WRG sequence is at the membrane interface of a six-helix transmembrane domain, with W interacting with a cardiolipin acyl chain, R exposed to water, and the backbone amide of G hydrogen bonding with a cardiolipin phosphate. A similar mode of cardiolipin interactions was suggested for VD W552RG554. However, we caution that the WRG sequence in the ADP/ATP carrier is a part of a folded domain, whereas the VD counterpart is a part of an IDR and hence has a strong tendency to be flexible. On the basis of chemical shift perturbations and paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE), Wu et al. (127) identified JM-A residues 649–659 in EGFR (Figure 2e) as the ones interacting with acidic lipids. For IDPs undergoing fast exchange between the membrane-bound and free states, Jin et al. (65) developed a membrane PRE method, by which an ensemble of IDP conformations was generated and selected to reproduce PRE rates. By this method, the second Tyr residue (Y199) of the ITAM in the disordered CTD of the CD3ε chain of the T cell receptor reached the hydrophobic core of an acidic membrane, but the first Tyr residue (Y188) did not.

  On the basis of the loss of NMR crosspeaks due to line broadening upon binding to liposomes composed of acidic lipids, Hicks et al. (62) isolated residues mediating this binding for the disordered N-terminal region of ChiZ to the midsection of the sequence (Figure 1c). However, the loss of crosspeaks precluded further characterization of the membrane association by solution NMR; Hicks et al. thus turned to solid-state NMR. Insensitive nuclei enhanced by polarization transfer (INEPT) 13C-13C correlation spectra showed that most residues remain dynamic upon membrane association, and cross-polarization 13C-13C correlation spectra showed that many Arg residues appear static, along with a subpopulation of Ala, Pro, and Leu residues. PRE effects from a spin label attached to a lipid headgroup confirmed Arg as the dominant amino acid type in mediating membrane binding. A similar combination of solution and solid-state NMR found that the N-terminal region of FtsQ is disordered except for a long Arg/Ala-rich segment (residues 45–74; Figure 2g), which forms an amphipathic helix both in solution and at the surface of an acidic membrane (116). For α-synuclein binding to acidic liposomes, attenuation in solution NMR crosspeak intensity indicated progressive weakening in binding propensity from the N terminus to the C terminus (34). Solution chemical exchange saturation transfer data and solid-state 13C-13C dipolar-assisted rotational resonance, INEPT, and PRE data defined three distinct regions: a 25-residue N-terminal region forming an amphipathic helix, a 40-residue C-terminal region that remains free and dynamic, and a middle region with lipid-specific binding propensities (45). Using confocal immunofluorescence microscopy, Jacob et al. (64) showed that PIP2 and an even more negatively charged variant PIP3 mediate the targeting of α-synuclein to the plasma membrane, and that this targeting is upregulated by the enzymatic conversion from PIP2 to PIP3.


### Other Experimental Methods
  Electron paramagnetic resonance/electron spin resonance is similar to NMR but is based on electron spin instead of nuclear spin. For a membrane-bound amphipathic helix, the electron paramagnetic resonance–derived depth parameter (Φ) exhibits a characteristic oscillation along the sequence. This method identified amphipathic helices formed by residues 9–22 of amylin (7); residues 253–261, 315–323, and 346–355 in microtubule binding repeats 1, 3, and 4, respectively, of tau (52); and residues 114–124 and 128–134 of complexin-1 (56).

  Residues mediating membrane association can also be identified by mutations that disrupt the association. Papayannopoulos et al. (99) mutated the basic region of N-WASP (Figure 2b) and assessed the effects on binding PIP2-containing liposomes by a cosedimentation assay. Single Ala mutations of the 10 basic residues or reversal of the amino acid sequence did not disrupt membrane binding, but Ala mutations of three or more of the basic residues did. Likewise, replacing the basic region by 10 Lys residues did not affect membrane binding, but replacing it with 7 Lys residues did. These observations demonstrate that a dense cluster of positive charges rather than their precise positions in the sequence is required for membrane binding. Yan et al. (128) used confocal imaging of EGFP-fused AKAP12 (also called gravin-α), an A-kinase anchoring protein, to test the roles of N-terminal myristoylation and three polybasic motifs in plasma membrane targeting. The targeting was unaffected when either an N-terminal portion or all three polybasic motifs were deleted, partially disrupted when an N-terminal portion and any two of the three polybasic motifs were deleted, and abrogated when all four elements were deleted. As demonstrated for the membrane association of N-WASP (32) (see below), the four elements do not necessarily have cooperative effects. Rather, each element increases the chance for the protein to be membrane attached. We summarize experimental methods in Table 1, noting the resulting residue-specific information.


### MD Simulations
  MD simulations can uniquely contribute to the characterization of IDP–membrane association. First, they provide atomistic details of the association. For example, simulations of the ChiZ N-terminal IDR revealed highly dynamic, or fuzzy, membrane association, with a different subset of basic residues interacting with a different subset of lipids at different times (62). Acidic residues may compete against acidic lipids to interact with basic residues, thereby reducing membrane binding stability. For the FtsQ N-terminal IDR, MD simulations found a shallow burial of the Arg/Ala-rich amphipathic helix, befitting a helical wheel with only a narrow hydrophobic arc lined by Ala residues (116). Second, MD simulations can yield physical explanations of experimental observations. As a simple example, in one of the 20 simulation trajectories of ChiZ IDR, a four-residue motif, A43PLR46, is stably found, with the nonpolar portion penetrating into the membrane hydrophobic core. This result explains the subpopulation of Ala, Pro, and Leu residues in the cross-polarization spectra. For WASP and N-WASP, MD simulations showed stable binding to PIP2-containing membranes (32). When prebound with Cdc42, N-WASP maintains stable binding (Figure 3a), but WASP is released from the membrane because its shorter basic region with fewer Arg/Lys residues does not allow for simultaneous binding with both PIP2 and an acidic patch on Cdc42.

  Third, MD simulations can generate mechanistic models. For WASP and N-WASP binding with PIP2-containing membranes, Dey & Zhou (32) tested whether two distant regions bind to the membrane independently in MD simulations. Independent binding was supported by the agreement between the observed probabilities of four outcomes (both regions bound, region one bound but region two free, region one free but region two bound, and both regions free) and the predictions assuming independence between the two regions. Furthermore, within the basic region preceding the GTPase binding domain (GBD), the distribution of the number of bound residues follows a binomial distribution, suggesting that the residues bind to the membrane largely independently of each other. The independence between residues is supported by the aforementioned mutational effects in Papayannopoulos et al.’s (99) membrane binding assay and the observation by Jin et al. (65) that the phosphorylation of CD3ε Y38 did not affect the burial of Y49 into the hydrophobic core. Likewise, the distribution of the number of bound PIP2 molecules followed a Poisson distribution, again indicating independence. A follow-up MD study of the role of PIP2 in activating N-WASP proposed that the acidic region can start to interact with Arp2/3 even before its full release from the basic region (31) (Figure 3b,c). The strong membrane association of GluN1, especially via exon 21, observed in MD simulations (111) (Figure 4) implicates potential differences in channel gating between splice variants. For SepF binding with acidic membranes, MD simulations showed that fuzzy association by basic clusters in the linker (Figure 2f) reinforces the membrane attachment of the N-terminal amphipathic helix (Figure 1b,g) and potentially keeps the Z-ring at a close distance from the membrane (30). MD simulations of a tau construct comprising microtubule binding repeats 1, 3, and 4 uncovered mimicry between membrane association and microtubule binding [the latter revealed by cryo-electron microscopy (19)] (85). In particular, a conserved Lys residue in each repeat forms salt bridges with either acidic lipids or microtubule acidic residues, and a conserved Val residue forms hydrophobic interactions with either lipid acyl chains or microtubule nonpolar residues. This partial mimicry may facilitate both the membrane anchoring of microtubules by tau and the transfer of tau from membranes to microtubules. These MD-derived mechanistic models await experimental validation.

  Fourth, MD simulations can present an ensemble view of the membrane-bound state. An example is the observation that the APLR motif of ChiZ is stably bound to the membrane in one trajectory but less engaged with the membrane in the other 19 trajectories. As another example, Y853 in the disordered CTD of mGluR3 was buried in the membrane for a fraction of the time in MD simulations; this residue mediates phosphorylation-dependent interactions with β-arrestins (87). The membrane PRE method produces an ensemble of conformations to match observed PRE rates, though the conformations were generated from the amino acid sequence instead of from MD simulations (65). Table 2 summarizes the residue-specific details from a representative collection of MD studies.

  MD is still limited by inaccuracies in force fields and undersampling. For example, MD simulations underestimated the population of mGluR3 in which Y853 is buried in the membrane (87). Still, the combination of MD and NMR presented a complete picture of how Y853 uses membrane association as a crucial regulatory mechanism. The complementarity between MD and NMR is also highlighted by the studies of ChiZ (62) and FtsQ (116). By integrating fluorescence cross-correlation spectroscopy, NMR, and MD, Endres et al. (38) and Arkhipov (11) concluded that JM-A dimerizes and changes from membrane inserted to membrane peripheral upon EGFR activation.


### Common Modes and Predictions
  From the experimental and MD studies, we can identify four common modes of IDP–membrane association: fuzzy membrane association through polybasic motifs (Figure 1b,c); membrane insertion via an amphipathic helix (Figure 1b); deep insertion of a short motif; and Tyr-mediated membrane insertion. Potential amphipathic helices can be easily identified by a helical wheel representation of their sequences (Figure 2a). However, membrane-bound amphipathic helices can tolerate deviations from an ideal amphipathic pattern (e.g., Ser or Thr on the nonpolar face); conversely, not all sequences with an ideal amphipathic pattern form membrane-bound amphipathic helices. Therefore, factors beyond an amphipathic pattern may need to be taken into consideration if one wants to predict membrane-bound amphipathic helices. Examples of deeply inserted short motifs include W552RG554 in Drp1 (86) and A43PLR46 in ChiZ (62). ITAMs provide the most notable example of Tyr-mediated membrane insertion. As the membrane PRE study (65) of CD3ε CTD showed, not all Tyr residues are created equal in membrane insertion. Tyr is well-suited for the membrane interface, as its bulky phenol ring is favored in the hydrophobic core while its hydroxyl is favored in the headgroup region. Changing to a smaller nonpolar side chain (Y853A in mGluR3) reduces the membrane insertion propensity, but removing the hydroxyl (Y853F) appears to strengthen membrane insertion (87). The repeated use of one mode or the use of multiple modes, even without any cooperative effects, can ensure an IDP’s membrane attachment (e.g., Drp1 VD, AKAP12, and WASP/N-WASP). For transmembrane proteins [e.g., mGluR2/mGluR3 (87), ChiZ (62), and GluN1 (111)], covalent linkage to a transmembrane domain enhances the membrane association of an IDR.

  As evidence for the growing understanding of IDP–membrane association, two sequence-based predictors for membrane association propensities appeared in 2022. One predictor, ReSMAP (residue-specific membrane association propensities of IDPs), calculates the propensity of a given residue from a mathematical formula by combining the contributions of the residue itself and its neighbors (111). The contribution of a residue depends on its amino acid type and its sequence distance from the residue under prediction. The mathematical formula has three parameters: the amplitudes of contributions from a positively charged residue, a negatively charged residue, and a neutral residue. These three parameters were determined by training with results from MD simulations of five IDPs associating with acidic membranes: ChiZ, GluN1, GluN2B, N-WASP, and WASP (e.g., Figures 3a and 4a). Accurate reproduction of MD results for GluN1 is illustrated in Figure 4b. ReSMAP predictions agree well with experimental results (mostly from NMR) for other IDPs, including the disordered N-terminal region of the Src kinase Hck (104) and the soluble portion of the SNARE protein synaptobrevin-2 (75). The other predictor, DisoLipPred, relies on a deep bidirectional recurrent network, using physiochemical properties associated with protein–lipid interactions as input features (69). The training/validation dataset contained only 11 membrane binding IDPs, obtained from the annotated DisProt database (60). Prediction methods are summarized in Table 3.


## FUNCTIONAL MECHANISMS OF IDPs INVOLVING MEMBRANE ASSOCIATION

### Protein Activation
  Membrane association or interaction contributes to the activation of a variety of proteins. A classic example is provided by WASP and its neuronal homolog, N-WASP (Figure 2b). These proteins are autoinhibited when the middle basic region and GBD are bound with the C-terminal acidic and cofilin homology regions, respectively (97). They are activated by the binding of PIP2 and the small GTPase Cdc42 to the basic region and the GBD, respectively (63, 109, 113). The intermolecular binding frees the C-terminal cofilin homology and acidic regions so they can bind Arp2/3; the resulting complex nucleates actin for polymerization. Endogenous Cdc42 is prenylated at the C terminus and attached to the membrane. Whereas Cdc42 prenylation had no effect on N-WASP activation (113), it was required for WASP activation (63). The reason for this difference was revealed by MD simulations of N-WASP and WASP at the surface of a PIP2-containing membrane in the presence of free and prenylated Cdc42 (32). Relative to the N-WASP basic region (Figures 2b and 3a), the WASP counterpart is shorter and has fewer basic residues, and therefore simultaneous binding by PIP2 and Cdc42 is unstable until the latter is prenylated. The role of PIP2 in freeing the acidic region from the basic region of N-WASP was elucidated by MD simulations (31) (Figure 3b,c).

  According to MD simulations, the CTDs of the GluN1 subunits in NMDA receptors associate with acidic membranes via basic residues in the region encoded by exon 21 (111) (Figure 4). As the CTD is covalently linked to the transmembrane M4 helix (Figure 2c) and the displacement of this helix, located at the periphery of the transmembrane domain, is crucial to efficient channel gating (4), one can speculate that CTD–membrane association may slow down the transition between the closed and open states of the channel. GluN1 has splice variants where exon 21 is deleted; faster gating transitions may be predicted for these variants as CTD–membrane association is abrogated. There is some evidence that these variants have faster deactivation (i.e., the transition from the open state to the closed state upon agonist release) (122). For the TRPV4 channel, PIP2 binding to the disordered N-terminal region via a polybasic motif is required for channel activation by hypotonic cell swelling or heat (50, 55). A stretch of amino acids near the N terminus competes with PIP2 for binding to the polybasic motif, leading to an autoinhibitory effect. Coarse-grained MD simulations revealed that PIP2 binding produces a pulling force on the cytoplasmic ankyrin repeat domain toward the membrane (55).

  The disordered CTDs of mGluR2 and mGluR3 associate with acidic membranes mainly via the N-terminal basic region (87). Cancer-linked mutations modulate CTD–membrane association. Membrane association affects CTD’s accessibility to transducers and regulators and may be regulated by phosphorylation or a change in local lipid composition. Mutations (R843A, G848E, Y853A, and R869Q) that weaken membrane association enhance glutamate-evoked receptor internalization, whereas a mutation (E870K) that stabilizes membrane association blunts internalization.

  Drp1 is a dimer formed via the stalk (Figure 5a). Oligomerization of Drp1’s G domains is interfered with by interactions with the disordered VD (83) (Figure 5b). When VD is inserted into the cardiolipin-rich mitochondrial membrane (86), one may speculate that it has less chance of interfering with G domain oligomerization (Figure 5b). Moreover, the membrane insertion may also generate membrane curvature (see below).

  EGFR dimers have an inactive population and an active population. In the inactive population, the two copies of JM-A form amphipathic helices that are separately bound to the membrane interface (11, 38). Acidic lipids such as phosphatidylserine and PIP2 stabilize the active dimer, where the two copies of JM-A form an antiparallel helix dimer, with basic residues projected toward acidic lipids (127).


### Membrane Anchoring
  In bacteria, the Z-ring, formed by polymerization of FtsZ, is the scaffold of the divisome. FtsZ is a cytoplasmic protein; membrane attachment of the Z-ring requires membrane anchors. Two proteins, FtsA and ZipA in Escherichia coli and FtsA and SepF in Bacillus subtilis, have overlapping roles as membrane anchors of the Z-ring (57, 103). ZipA is a transmembrane protein, whereas FtsA and SepF attach to the membrane via an amphipathic helix. FtsZ binds its disordered C-terminal tail to a folded domain in each of these three proteins anchoring (20, 81, 119) (Figure 1f). The membrane attachment of SepF in Mycobacterium tuberculosis is characterized by MD simulations (30) (Figure 1b,g). In addition to the N-terminal amphipathic helix, basic clusters in the linker between the amphipathic helix and the CTD reinforce membrane association. On the basis of coelution assay, solution NMR, and computational modeling, Smrt et al. (116) proposed that FtsQ serves as a second membrane anchor for the Z-ring in M. tuberculosis. The Arg/Ala-rich amphipathic helix of FtsZ likely docks to the intersubdomain cleft of the GTPase domain (Figure 1b). Together, SepF and FtsQ keep the Z-ring close to the M. tuberculosis inner membrane, allowing it to recruit and interact with late divisome proteins, including FtsW, CrgA, and ChiZ (Figure 6).

  AKAP12 is targeted to the plasma membrane via N-terminal myristoylation and three polybasic motifs and acts as a scaffold to interact with many proteins, including β-adrenergic receptors and protein kinase A (PKA) and protein kinase C (128). Recently, it was found that the two Lys residues in the PKA binding region can be myristoylated (12). Lys myristoylation drives β-adrenergic receptors to lipid rafts, leading to PKA activation and downstream signaling.

  Many proteins undergo phase separation at membrane surfaces. In addition to PIP2 and Cdc42, the SH2/SH3 protein Nck can activate N-WASP via interactions between the former’s SH3 domains and the latter’s proline-rich motifs (78) (Figure 2b). The SH2 domains of Nck also bind phosphorylated Tyr residues in the CTD of the transmembrane protein nephrin. The tripartite system, N-WASP/Nck/nephrin CTD, undergoes LLPS, resulting in a sharp increase in Arp2/3 stimulation. Membrane tethering of the CTD of nephrin via histidine–Ni interactions—mimicking the membrane embedment via its transmembrane helix—localizes the initiation of action polymerization to the membrane surface (13). Zona occludens proteins undergo LLPS; membrane anchoring of the resulting condensates through claudins also recruits and clusters the latter proteins for organizing the tight junction between cells (16). A similar mechanism applies to another member of the membrane-associated guanylate kinase family, PSD-95, and the resulting postsynaptic density clusters NMDA receptors (132).


### Membrane Curvature Sensing and Generation
  IDPs play crucial roles in sensing and generating membrane curvature through various mechanisms, which have been extensively reviewed elsewhere (59, 66, 73). Here is a brief summary plus some expansion.


#### Amphipathic helix insertion.
  Insertion of amphipathic helices is facilitated by lipid packing defects characteristic of positive curvature, as demonstrated for the amphipathic helix of epsin in generating curvature and tubulation in membranes (42).


#### Entropic mechanism.
  Zeno et al. (133) used confocal fluorescence microscopy to study membrane curvature effects on the tethering of the histidine-tagged CTD of AP180 to small unilamellar vesicles doped with Ni-labeled lipids. AP180 is an adaptor protein in the clathrin-mediated endocytic pathway and uses an N-terminal folded domain to bind PIP2-containing membranes; the CTD on its own does not bind membranes. Membrane tethering reduces the conformational entropy of an IDP; this reduction is minimized when the membrane is curved away from the IDP (positive curvature). Indeed, the level of membrane-tethered CTD per unit surface area is higher for smaller (i.e., more curved) vesicles.


#### Electrostatic mechanism.
  Positive curvature increases the distance of a tethered IDP chain from the membrane surface. Therefore, when electrostatic repulsion exists between an acidic membrane and IDPs with a net negative charge, positive curvature is preferred for tethering. Electrostatic repulsion between the IDP chains also favors positive curvature. These effects were also demonstrated on the AP180 CTD (134).


#### Crowding mechanism.
  With positive membrane curvature, the distance between tethered protein molecules is increased. This increase relieves the steric repulsion (or crowding) of membrane-tethered proteins (120). Another way to relieve steric repulsion is to change the orientation or conformation of the proteins so as to minimize their projected areas on the membrane (136).


#### Liquid-liquid phase separation.
  Conversely, attraction between IDP chains favors tethering to membranes with negative curvature. This effect is amplified when the attraction drives LLPS at the membrane surface, as demonstrated using the FUS low-complexity domain (130). In a follow-up study, curvature effects of chimeras linking the repulsive AP180 CTD and the attractive FUS low-complexity domain were tested (131). When the FUS IDR was membrane proximal, its condensation amplified the repulsion between the AP180 IDR chains, leading to positive curvature. In contrast, when the AP180 IDR was membrane proximal, attraction between FUS IDR chains dominated, leading to negative curvature.

  Solution NMR showed that complexin-1 favored binding to small unilamellar vesicles over large ones, indicating curvature sensing by the C-terminal amphipathic helix (118). α-Synuclein remodeled multilamellar vesicles into smaller structures, contributed by both membrane insertion of the N-terminal amphipathic helix and repulsion of the negatively charged C-terminal portion (41). For Drp1, we suggest that membrane insertion of VDs and crowding of G domains together generate significant membrane curvature (Figure 5c,d).


### Membrane Fusion and Fission
  Ca2+-triggered release of neurotransmitters involves the fusion of synaptic vesicles with the plasma membrane, a process driven by the assembly of the trans-SNARE complex and regulated by many other proteins. Synaptotagmin-1 is the Ca2+ sensor and interacts with SNARE proteins (125). MD simulations suggest that synaptotagmin-1 bridges between vesicle and plasma membranes via the translocation of C2B from the vesicle membrane to the plasma membrane (108) (Figure 7). The latter step is facilitated by the transient interaction between the acidic region upstream of C2A and the Ca2+ binding loops of C2B. α-Synuclein has also been proposed to bridge between the two membranes (33). The vesicle membrane–tethered SNARE protein synaptobrevin-2 binds (the vesicle) membrane via a basic region preceding the C-terminal transmembrane helix; this binding may reduce electrostatic repulsion by the plasma membrane and thereby facilitate the assembly of the SNARE complex (75). Complexin-1 is thought to bind SNARE proteins via its N-terminal helices and the vesicle membrane via its C-terminal amphipathic helix (56). Alternatively, the C-terminal amphipathic helix of complexin-2 may insert into the plasma membrane to stabilize the fusion pore (28).

  Drp1 is the primary driver of mitochondrial membrane fission. As explained above, away from membranes, Drp1 dimers are in an autoinhibited state, where VD–G domain interactions interfere with oligomerization (Figure 5b). The insertion of VD into the cardiolipin-rich mitochondrial membrane perhaps relieves the autoinhibition (Figure 5c). Moreover, the formation of an amphipathic helix and a WRG local structure at the membrane interface (86) generates curvature. Last, oligomerization via G domains produces crowding effects that further increase membrane curvature, leading to tubulation and fission (Figure 5d). Drp1 oligomers decorated on membrane tubules have been imaged by cryo-electron microscopy (101). The images present ring-shaped assemblies of Drp1, with VD inserted into curved membranes.

  LLPS at membrane surfaces presents a different mechanism of membrane fission (74). In plant vacuoles, storage proteins undergo LLPS; the resulting condensates spread over, or wet, the vacuole membrane, leading to membrane budding and vacuole division.


### Antimicrobial Peptides
  Antimicrobial peptides are characterized by their cationic nature and intrinsic disorder. They typically form an amphipathic helix when bound to microbial membranes. Two classical mechanisms of membrane disruption are (a) pore formation, either barrel-stave pores formed by a bundle-like assembly of peptides (15) or toroidal (or wormhole) pores lined by both peptides and lipid headgroups in a doughnut-like shape (84); and (b) carpeting or detergent-like, which posits that the accumulation of peptides on the membrane surface leads to membrane instability or lipid extraction (106). When one of the two Phe residues (Phe3 and Phe13) in aurein 1.2 was mutated to Ala, the mechanism changed from carpeting to pore formation. MD simulations revealed that piscidin 1 creates surface defects on membranes, leading to membrane thinning and water permeation (102). A follow-up study combining MD simulations with experimental techniques, including neutron diffraction, found that, relative to piscidin 3, the deeper burial of piscidin 1 correlates with its higher permeabilization ability (90).

  Machine learning has been used to predict antimicrobial peptides, such as a support vector machine classifier using 12 descriptors as input (76). The descriptors include net charge, fraction of nonpolar residues, physicochemical similarities between each residue and its second or fourth neighbor, and occurrence of particular residue pairs (e.g., LW or NK). Apart from antimicrobial peptides, the C-terminal amphipathic helix of complexin-2 forms pores as demonstrated by liposome leakage assays and MD simulations (28).


## DISEASE MECHANISMS OF IDPs INVOLVING MEMBRANE ASSOCIATION

### Membrane Disruption
  An often-cited avenue of cytotoxicity for amyloidogenic IDPs is membrane disruption, which can occur through the foregoing mechanisms and more.


#### Channel formation.
  Aβ40/42, tau, amylin, and α-Synuclein can all assemble into transmembrane channels. An early study reported that Aβ40 formed cation-selective channels across artificial membranes (10). More recently, only oligomers of Aβ42, not Aβ40, formed nonselective ion channels across membranes (17). It was further shown that the channel activity of Aβ42 correlated with β-sheet formation in membranes (68). In dodecylphosphocholine micelles, Aβ42 formed an antiparallel tetrameric C-sheet, which could further assemble into an octameric β-sandwich capable of ion conductance (24). The detergent-stabilized antiparallel tetramer seeds the formation of a 150-kDa oligomer (94), which can potentially act as an ion channel across membranes (49). Amylin formed pores in acidic membranes (135), as did α-synuclein and tau in mitochondria-like membranes (21, 53); cardiolipin enhanced the IDP–membrane interactions and pore-forming activity.


#### Carpeting or detergent-like mechanism.
  Aβ42 oligomers were reported to conduct ions in a nonchannel mechanism (70). Cryo-electron tomography revealed that Aβ42 oligomers carpeted the proximal leaflet of lipid bilayers (121). Aβ40/42, amylin, and α-synuclein oligomers can also extract lipids, leading to membrane disintegration (18, 110, 114, 123).


#### Generation of reactive oxygen species.
  Aβ peptides can interact with metal ions such as Cu2+. The metal–peptide complex can oxidize lipids and create free radicals, thereby accelerating the membrane damage through oxidative stress. High-molecular-weight oligomers of Aβ42 generated higher oxidative stress than did low-molecular-weight oligomers (129).


### Membrane-Assisted Aggregation
  Membranes can assist the aggregation of IDPs by concentrating them in a 2D space and inducing disorder-to-order transitions. Evidence suggests that the early stages of fibrillation of tau, α-synuclein, amylin, and Aβ40 are facilitated by cell membranes. Acidic membranes dramatically lowered the critical aggregation concentration of a tau construct (residues 244–372) encompassing the four microtubule binding repeats, and this effect was weakened with increasing pH (36). The pH dependence could be attributed to a decrease in the net positive charge of the IDP, implicating a role of charge neutralization by the membranes in promoting aggregation. A longer tau construct comprising residues 198–399 remained disordered and dynamic upon initial binding to phosphatidylserine and cholesterol-containing small unilamellar vesicles, with only Lys residues forming significant interactions with lipids (35). Over the course of weeks, the membrane-bound tau construct formed amyloid fibrils at the membrane interface. In the cell, formation of tau fibrillar aggregates may begin at the plasma membrane (47, 80). It is also noteworthy that negatively charged lipids such as phosphatidylserine are elevated in Alzheimer disease brains (107, 126).

  When bound to acidic lipid vesicles, the primary nucleation of α-synuclein was accelerated by over three orders of magnitude (46). Solid-state NMR revealed the conversion from an α-helical conformation to β-sheet fibrils (25). Antonschmidt et al. (6) captured intermediates in the fibrillation process. Residues 57–62 and 72–80 converted to their conformations in the final fibrils in an early intermediate, whereas all the residues between 38 and 80 completed their conversion in a late intermediate, but the N-terminal 35 residues remained a membrane-bound amphipathic helix.

  The conversion from α-helical to β-sheet conformations was observed in MD simulations of 17 copies of amylin bound to an acidic membrane (23). While the first 11 residues remained membrane bound (largely mediated by positive charges), the rest of the chain, especially residues 22–27, formed intercopy aromatic and hydrophobic interactions. An initial C-terminal α-helix was melted in all 17 copies, whereas an initial N-terminal helix melted in approximately two-thirds of the copies. Instead, a β-hairpin was formed, which perhaps represents an early event in the fibrillation process. Fibril elongation on the membrane surface damaged the membrane (37, 39).

  Multiple fibril structures of Aβ40 formed in a membrane environment have been solved by solid-state NMR and cryo-electron microscopy (43, 54, 71, 96). All these fibrils consist of an N-sheet (residues 17–22) and a C-sheet (residues 30–36); these β-sheets often assemble into a dimeric R- or open-shaped protofilaments (Figure 8). At the early stage of the membrane-assisted aggregation process, the N-sheet residues remained membrane bound and the C-sheet residues started to leave the membrane (71). All-atom MD simulations revealed multiple pathways of membrane-assisted Aβ40 aggregation (95) (Figure 8). Membranes stabilize a semiopen intermediate, which can either collapse into an R shape or extend into an open shape. Dimerization leads to R-shaped and open-shaped fibrils, respectively.


## MEMBRANE-ASSOCIATED LIQUID-LIQUID PHASE SEPARATION
  We have already seen the roles played by membrane-associated LLPS, including the initiation of actin polymerization at the membrane surface (13), clustering of membrane-embedded receptors (16, 132), and membrane fission (74). As found for membrane-tethered scaffolding of interaction networks (Figure 6) and membrane-assisted aggregation (Figure 8), the restriction to a 2D space concentrates proteins. LLPS also concentrates proteins in the dense phase. These two effects are synergistic in membrane-associated LLPS, as demonstrated by significant reductions in threshold LLPS concentrations for the N-WASP/Nck/nephrin CTD system (13).

  Many other roles of membrane-associated LLPS have been identified, including organizing synaptic vesicle clusters (91), catalyzing the initiation of clathrin-mediated endocytosis (29), enabling fast endophilin-mediated endocytosis (93), regulating Ca2+ binding to synaptotagmin-1 (88), and sequestering of p62-containing droplets in phagosomes for degradation (2). In the last case, cargo specificity is ensured by the anchoring of p62’s LC3-interacting region to the ubiquitin-like protein LC3 (Figure 1i). LC3 itself is attached to the phagosomal membrane via conjugation to the lipid phosphatidylethanolamine (Figure 1e).


## CONCLUDING REMARKS
  IDP–membrane association regulates a host of cellular processes, including initiation of actin polymerization, ion channel gating, and membrane fusion and fission, but it also facilitates aggregation. The physical rules governing IDP–membrane interactions are becoming clear thanks to techniques such as NMR spectroscopy and MD simulations, and sequence-based predictions of membrane binding patterns are now possible.

  Membrane association via polybasic motifs and amphipathic helices is now well-understood, but membrane insertion of short motifs and Tyr-based motifs is context dependent and under-studied. For example, what is special about the WRG motif in Drp1? Why do some Tyr residues insert into membranes while others do not? Here, integrating MD simulations and experimental techniques, including NMR, may prove fruitful.

  Sequence-based prediction methods have shown great promise. Further improvement will require much larger, carefully curated databases for training and testing.

  Solid-state NMR and cryo-electron microscopy are powerful techniques for determining the final fibril structures from membrane-assisted aggregation, but structural data are still sparse for intermediates along aggregation pathways. Here again MD simulations may be useful in filling in the gaps. Likewise, LLPS at membrane surfaces is now an important area of biophysics; coarse-grained MD simulations are beginning to appear (79, 92) and will have much more to contribute.
