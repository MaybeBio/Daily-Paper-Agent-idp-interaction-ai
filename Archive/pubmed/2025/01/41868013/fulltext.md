# Modeling biomolecular condensates across scales: Atomistic, coarse-grained, and data-driven approaches


## Abstract
  Biomolecular condensates are integral to processes underlying cellular function and dysfunction, and they also present a versatile platform for engineering living cells. Understanding how molecular interactions give rise to condensate form and function is therefore a major area of research. Computational modeling has emerged as a powerful tool for uncovering the biophysical principles underlying condensates. Because condensate biophysics spans multiple spatiotemporal scales, from interactions of amino acid side chains to the emergent material properties of entire condensates, decoding their behavior requires multiscale strategies. In this review, we discuss three core classes of computational modeling approaches that extend our ability to probe condensates. We first examine atomistic modeling, which enables a detailed examination of interactions that encode condensate behaviors. We then discuss coarse-grained modeling, with a focus on residue-resolution models, which advance our ability to predict condensate properties with both precision and efficiency. Finally, we summarize advances in data-driven and machine learning approaches, which leverage molecular simulations to map sequence–property relationships of condensates at a fraction of the cost. Throughout the review, we highlight the key ingredients of each approach, the types of simulations and modeling strategies employed, and the primary observables that can be measured. In doing so, we aim for this review to serve as both an informative and practical guide for leveraging these approaches to understand and engineer biomolecular condensates.


## CAPTURING THE COMPLEXITY OF BIOMOLECULAR CONDENSATES VIA COMPUTATIONAL MODELING
  The interior of living cells is comprised of a multicomponent mixture of biomolecules. To control diverse biochemical processes, cells face the challenge of organizing their many components in space and time. Accordingly, different compartments are created, each with a distinct chemical composition and function. While some of these compartments may be separated from the surrounding intracellular environment by lipid membranes, the great majority are membraneless [1-5]. Research on membraneless compartments—more widely known as biomolecular condensates—has exploded in the past decade, attracting significant interest from experimentalists, computer simulators, and theoreticians alike.

  A vast body of work has revealed that condensates form through phase separation, where biomolecules like proteins and nucleic acids separate into dynamic droplets that respond to environmental changes [1, 6, 7]. The recognition that phase separation and the resulting condensate formation serve as an organizing principle in biology has revolutionized the field. Recent studies have shown that condensates may fulfill a range of vital functions, including genome organization, RNA transcription and processing, signaling, and adaptation to environmental stressors [3, 8-12]. Alongside these functions, condensates have also been implicated in the progression of various disease states, including neurodegenerative disorders [13-26], cancers [17, 27-31], and infectious diseases [32-39]. Thus, there is an urgent need to understand how condensates encode their functions and how they may contribute to cellular dysfunction. Such insights will aid in targeting disease-associated condensates. Furthermore, due to their lack of membranes and their dynamic and tunable nature, condensates present enormous opportunities for engineering living cells. For example, they can be designed to form enzymatic condensates that reprogram metabolism or to function as biological sensors and actuators, among other applications [40-50]. Altogether, studies of biomolecular condensates have revealed fundamental insights into how cells function, how dysfunction arises, and potential avenues for cellular engineering.

  A major open question in condensate research is understanding the molecular underpinnings of their form and function. Given a collection of proteins and nucleic acids, what physicochemical codes dictate the material properties and functions of condensates? This question is inherently multiscale—spanning interactions of individual building blocks (such as amino acids and nucleotides) to the collective behaviors of entire condensates (e.g., viscoelastic responses, biochemical activities). Thus, elucidating how condensates emerge, are regulated, and function inside cells is a formidable challenge that demands an interdisciplinary effort, integrating strategies across multiple spatiotemporal scales.

  In addition to bridging scales and connecting them to emergent properties, another challenge lies in capturing the physicochemical diversity of condensates. Indeed, conden sates are complex multicomponent materials composed of hundreds of proteins, nucleic acids, chromatin, and smal molecules (e.g., metabolites, cofactors, ions, water) [4, 51-58]. Some molecules serve as scaffolds, providing the multivalent interaction network indispensable to condensate for mation. In contrast, other molecules act as clients, lower valency components that partition into condensates through interactions with scaffolds but do not drive phase separation on their own [59-63]. Furthermore, environmental condi tions (e.g., temperature, pH, and salt) can dramatically tune the phase boundaries of biomolecules [64-73]. Thus, there is no such thing as a unique classification of biomolecule that phase separate and those which do not, but rather, the capacity of a biomolecule to phase separate should continuously be assessed considering a specific environment and the available binding partners. Taken together, understanding which molecules are integral to function, how this physicochemical diversity shapes condensate microstructure and macroscopic behaviors, and how such behaviors change a a function of environmental shifts requires probing a rich high-dimensional landscape—further challenging our ability to map molecules to condensate function.

  Complementing experimental advances, computationa modeling has been instrumental in uncovering the fundamental biophysical and molecular mechanisms that regulate biomolecular condensates [74]. Such approaches often seek to capture the complexity of biomolecular condensates, offering high-resolution insights into their thermodynamics and dynamics [75-77]. For instance, computational-methods have been instrumental in identifying the molecular codes that enable biological phase separation under different conditions, in zooming in to probe interactions at submolecular length scales that give rise to emergent condensate properties, and in providing tools to predict condensate behavior directly from sequence [78, 79]. In thireview, we survey the state of the art in computational modeling of biomolecular condensates. Specifically, we focus on three classes of approaches: (1) atomistic simulations, which probe how interactions at atomic resolution shape condensates; (2) coarse-grained models, particularly residue-resolution representations, which retain chemical specificity while enabling efficient exploration of phase separation; and (3) data-driven and machine learning methods, which integrate molecular simulations with experiments to accelerate predictions of condensate properties and guide the design of novel condensates. We dissect these approaches, discussing the types of information they require, the questions they can address, and their current limitations. Finally, we highlight future directions and challenges in computational modeling that must be overcome to deepen our understanding of condensates and to advance their rational engineering.


## ATOMISTIC SIMULATIONS
  All-atom models of condensates are detailed molecular representations in which every atom of each constituent, such as proteins, nucleic acids, water, and ions, is explicitly modeled (Figure 1a). As such, all-atom models preserve properties such as hydrogen bonding, secondary structure, base- and side-chain stacking, and interactions with water and ions—offering a detailed, chemically explicit view into the molecular forces underlying biomolecular phase separation. Accordingly, these simulations have been widely used to investigate biomolecular condensates across a range of length and time scales—from simulations of individual residues in proteins and nucleic acids, aimed at understanding how their interactions vary with environmental conditions (Figure 1b,c), to large-scale simulations of entire condensates that capture their dynamics and interactions with the surrounding solvent (Figure 1d-e), discussed in detail in a recent review [76]. In this section, we highlight some of these contributions, providing an example of data relevant to biomolecular phase separation that can be extracted from atomistic simulations (Figure 1f-i).


### Studies via all-atom models
  At smaller length scales, atomistic simulations can be used to characterize the relative strengths of molecular interactions that drive condensate formation and how these interactions vary with environmental parameters such as salt concentration (Figure 1a). For instance, computing potentials of mean force (i.e., free energy of interaction) for various amino acid pairs has been used to determine the relative strengths of π–π, cation–π, and non-π interactions, which can inform how vital these interactions may be in driving protein phase separation [80, 81]. These computations are typically performed using umbrella sampling in explicit solvent, often at physiological salt concentrations, thereby offering a high-resolution view of the drivers of biomolecular condensation. Similar computations have been conducted for nucleic acids, such as RNA, and for combinations of nucleic acids and amino acids [82, 83]. Based on these studies, it has been hypothesized that interactions involving groups with sp2 hybridization (such as those found in aromatic rings or the guanidinium group of arginine) may play a dominant role in driving protein/nucleic acid phase separation [84-86].

  In addition to probing these interactions at physiological salt concentrations, atomistic potentials-of-mean-force (PMF) calculations can also be used to examine how pairwise interactions among amino acids or nucleic acids change as a function of salt concentration [72]. For example, by performing atomistic PMF calculations at different salt concentrations, it was demonstrated that pairwise interactions can exhibit both monotonic behavior (e.g., charge–charge interactions becoming less favorable with increasing salt) and non-monotonic variation (e.g., lysine–π interactions, which display hybrid electrostatic–hydrophobic behavior: strong at low salt due to electrostatics, weakened at intermediate salt due to screening, and strengthened again at high salt due to hydrophobic effects). Together, it was demonstrated that at low salt, both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions contribute to phase separation of proteins such as FUS, while at high salt, hydrophobic interactions dominate. These findings help explain the reentrant phase separation of proteins observed in experiments at larger length scales, where proteins form condensates at low salt concentrations, dissolve at intermediate salt concentrations, and reform at high salt concentrations due to hydrophobic effects [72].

  Similarly, PMFs were used to assess the role of protein backbone in phase separation, by comparing pair interaction profiles between capped amino acids and compounds mimicking side chains [87]. It was shown that peptide backbones enhance interactions in dilute environments but suppress them under crowded conditions, highlighting the dual role of backbone structure in mediating context-dependent interactions. In addition, the PMF profiles for larger protein fragments can be used to study condensate aging. For example, umbrella sampling was conducted for LARKS peptides to assess the propensity for β-sheet formation in the low-complexity domain (LCD) of hnRNPA1 [88]. These studies revealed that proteins containing regions with a higher tendency to form intermolecular β-sheets were more prone to gelation, driven by the accumulation of irreversible and long-lasting contacts. Collectively, such small-scale simulations provide valuable insights into the molecular interactions that sustain condensates and how these are modulated by environmental conditions (Figure 1b).

  Simulations of single chains representing molecules at infinite dilution conditions can offer valuable insights into their behavior within condensate environments (Figure 1c). This is achieved through simulations of single chains in a cubic box, often with the aid of enhanced sampling techniques. Such studies enable the characterization of the conformational ensemble of molecules under defined conditions and the mapping of the interaction landscape between different components, identifying the dominant contributors to phase separation (Figure 1g). Several studies have applied this approach to FUS, providing detailed insights into its conformational preferences and interaction patterns at the single-chain level. For example, Murthy et al. [67] simulated single- and two-chain simulations of the FUS low-complexity domain and calculated the average number of hydrogen bonds formed, concluding that glutamine-mediated hydrogen bonding and aromatic contacts are key drivers for FUS phase separation. Berkeley et al. simulated HSPB1 dimers in complex with several chains of the FUS low-complexity domain, tracking contacts and dynamics to see how FUS binding perturbs HSPB1 inter-domain interactions [89]. They concluded that binding of FUS fragments to the HSPB1 dimer disrupts HSPB1’s oligomer-stabilizing contacts, loosening intramolecular and intermolecular interactions and thereby exposing HSPB1 interfaces that are needed for client engagement.

  Simulations of such simplified systems can also provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying droplet aging. One study combining NMR with MD simulations of measles virus NTAIL in three regimes (diluted, crowded, and condensed) [90] suggested that internal dynamics within condensed droplets remain rapid and fluid-like, sustained by short-lived, rapidly exchanging interactions. In another study, a direct comparison between FUS protein dynamics in condensed and dilute phases demonstrated a marked slowdown in large-scale motion within condensates, while preserving local flexibility, revealing internal dynamic heterogeneity [91]. In addition to probing interactions within phase-separating biomolecules, such simulations can also be used to assess how small molecules influence their phase behavior. For example, the effect of drug binding on the phase transition of the androgen receptor activation domain was investigated by simulating its sub-domain, rich in aromatic residues, Tau-5, in a complex with a set of candidate therapeutic compounds. Such simulations enabled identification of drugs capable of modulating condensate formation through strong direct interactions with phase-separating regions of Tau-5 [92].

  Larger-scale all-atom simulations of dense condensate-mimicking systems can directly probe condensate environments (Figure 1d,h). Paloni et al. [86] simulated fragments of NDDX4 at high concentration to characterize sequence-specific interactions within the condensed phase, further highlighting the role of arginine and aromatic residues in cation–π and π–π interactions that contribute to condensate stability. Additionally, simulations of amino acid mixtures [93], containing residues that encode both strong (“stickers”) and weak (“spacers”) interactions, have demonstrated that even in minimal systems, demixing can occur once the appropriate composition is reached. Impact of condensate environment on RNA structure has also been investigated using a system of RNA in a solution with RGG/RG motifs, effectively simulating multivalent interactions within a condensate and demonstrating subsequent weakening of the RNA structure [94]. Simplified models also helped explore how the presence of ions influences condensate organization and dynamics. For instance, simulations of multiple chains of the phase-separating protein PopZ in ionic environments of varying concentration and valence revealed that multivalent cations significantly affect internal condensate rearrangements [95].

  Additionally, simulating such minimal condensate-mimicking systems provides a framework for investigating condensate–drug interactions, a growing area of interest in therapeutic development. For instance, simulations representing MED1, HP1α, and NPM1 condensates studied physicochemical features that govern the preferential localization of small molecules into various condensate environments. This study showed that the differences in condensate environments result in various compounds’ partition coefficients [96].

  Moving beyond single-chain and condensate-mimicking models, all-atom simulations of coexisting dense and dilute phases (“slab simulations”) provide direct insight into equilibrium phase behavior (Figure 1e,i). Because such simulations are computationally expensive, a common approach is to backmap the atomistic structure from coarser equilibrium configurations, which are discussed in more detail in Section IV. One of the insights that can be gained from such simulations is the partitioning of compounds within the condensed phase. For instance, microsecond-scale atomistic simulations on backmapped condensates can reveal differential ion partitioning between droplets (as an example in FUS and LAF-1 condensates [97]). Phase separated full-length FUS chains have also been studied through microsecond-scale all-atom MD simulations to probe intermolecular interactions at atomic detail [98]. The study showed that FUS phase separation is mainly driven by IDR interactions—especially aromatic, arginine, and polar residues—while folded domains like RRM and ZnF act as modulators by influencing chain conformation and stability rather than directly driving multivalent contacts. Small-molecule partitioning in model peptide-based condensates, as well as condensates formed by the low complexity domain of FUS, has also been probed with such large-scale atomistic simulations [99], demonstrating how condensate chemical environment shapes preferential distribution of the compounds between phases.

  All-atom slab simulations can also provide information on heterogeneity in phase-separated systems: for example, Welsh et al. investigated surface charge density in condensates and reported heterogeneities in ion distributions—wherein Cl− preferentially accumulated at the interfaces of polyU–PR25 droplets [100]. Macroscopic properties such as viscosity and surface tension can also be probed at this representation. For example, single-molecule fluorescence and all-atom MD were combined to probe the internal dynamics of histone H1 and prothymosin-α condensates, revealing that despite high macroscopic viscosity, individual protein chains remain highly dynamic due to transient electrostatic interactions [101, 102].


### Challenges of simulating condensates in atomistic detail
  While atomistic simulations provide a high-resolution lens for probing condensate biophysics with full chemical specificity, such simulations are not without their challenges. All-atom molecular simulations of phase-separating biomolecules are limited by the accuracy of the force fields and their ability to faithfully sample the free energy landscapes of interest, which are generally rough and contain numerous competing local energy minima. Additionally, atomistic force fields must properly capture the statistical weights of transient secondary structural motifs of IDPs [103-105] as well as polarization effects that influence solvation and intermolecular interactions [106, 107]. Recent advances have significantly improved the accuracy of these models, enabling more realistic simulations of IDP behavior in both dilute and dense phases in line with experimental data [105, 108], and other developments have been extensively reviewed elsewhere [103-105]. It is also worth emphasizing that the most widely adopted atomistic force fields rely on fixed point charges, which prevent them from adapting to changes in their local environment or capturing polarization effects, as outlined comprehensively in earlier reports [109, 110]. This becomes especially important in situations where the electrostatic environment shifts significantly, such as in highly ionic environments. Polarizable force fields can address these shortcomings, but their wider use has been limited by their considerably higher computational cost.

  Hence, simulations of condensates (even those containing a single type of phase-separated protein) remain computationally exhaustive, involving millions of atoms per system and requiring months of supercomputer time [101, 102]. Additionally, even in long simulations (in the microsecond to millisecond regime), biomolecular systems can become trapped in local minima. Therefore, in many cases, enhanced sampling techniques are paramount to interrogate conformational ensembles efficiently. To this end, numerous approaches have been developed such as replica exchange, metadynamics, and multicanonical MD [111-114], which improve conformational exploration and convergence and are now widely used for simulations of condensates (see review in Ref. [115]), with varying degrees of success. Long-timescale simulations on specialized hardware, such as Anton 2, have also facilitated the benchmarking of force fields and the atomistic interrogation of condensate composition, structure, and dynamics [116]. Approaches based on defining a priori collective variables may pose additional issues in the context of disordered systems such as condensates, as it is often difficult to find suitable reaction coordinates.

  Despite the enormous progress, simulating phase separation of biologically relevant proteins and nucleic acids at atomistic resolution remains intractable. Even with enhanced sampling protocols, capturing the full thermodynamics and kinetics of condensate formation remains out of reach due to the sheer computational cost. As a result, most all-atom simulations are limited to preformed condensed phases (i.e., derived from coarser simulations) or simulations at smaller length scales, as discussed above. Consequently, significant efforts have been devoted to developing coarse-grained models for probing bimolecular condensates.


## RESIDUE-RESOLUTION COARSE-GRAINED MODELS
  Residue-resolution coarse-grained models have emerged as indispensable tools for studying biomolecular phase separation, enabling access to time and length scales that remain inaccessible to atomistic simulations. These approaches trade atomic detail for computational efficiency, while retaining sequence-dependent interactions. Their design has been strongly informed by polymer physics and mean-field theories, consistent with the observation that intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (IDPs and IDRs) are key components of biomolecular condensates.

  While there are several levels of coarse-graining one can adopt, here we focus on residue-resolution coarse-grained models. In these models, each amino acid (or nucleic acid) is represented by a single interaction site (i.e., a ‘bead’ or ‘particle’). This mapping preserves the molecular sequence and chemical specificity while significantly reducing the number of degrees of freedom. By reducing the dimensionality of the sampling problem, such models enable access to spatial and temporal resolutions that are otherwise infeasible in all-atom simulations. This makes it possible to model and measure collective and bulk properties of large or multicomponent systems.

  In the following sections, we review the state-of-the-art in residue-resolution coarse-grained modeling of condensates (Figure 2). We focus on the collective physical behaviors and experimental observables these models can predict—such as phase diagrams and condensate material properties—and highlight how they advance mechanistic understanding of biomolecular phase separation.


### Capturing sequence-specific effects
  The strength of residue-resolution coarse-grained models lies in their ability to maintain the chemical specificity of each component (Figure 2a,b) and their environment (Figure 2c,d), thereby uncovering how individual residues (amino acids or nucleic acids) influence collective phase behavior[67, 85, 117-123]. Biomolecules can show dramatic differences in phase behavior with even subtle sequence changes [123-125]; thus, to address this, effective coarse-grained models incorporate residue-specific interaction parameters that reflect the underlying chemistry. For instance, aromatic residues (such as tyrosine or phenylalanine) tend to favor π–π contacts [84, 126], charged residues engage in electrostatic and cation–π interactions [127-129], while polar and aliphatic residues contribute hydrophilic or hydrophobic interactions [130, 131]. In nucleic acids, different base pairs exhibit varying strengths due to differences in hydrogen bonding and base stacking. For instance, G–C pairs form three hydrogen bonds, while A–T or A–U pairs form two. Effective coarse-grained models tune residue–residue interactions to capture these differences in interactions.

  A landmark example is the hydropathy-scale (HPS) family [132], where non-bonded interaction strengths are assigned from a hydropathy parameter and electrostatics modeled via a Debye–Hückel potential [132]. Many subsequent models have built upon this work, with the primary objective of optimizing the non-bonded interaction terms to capture more nuanced sequence features and achieve closer agreement with experiments. Given the dominant role of π–π and cation–π interactions in driving phase separation of many characterized disordered proteins [84], a central focus has been the development of residue-resolution models that appropriately balance these contributions in the interaction potentials [80, 129, 133-136]. For example, experiments have shown that arginine-rich sequences promote phase separation much more effectively than lysine-rich ones, despite both being positively charged—an effect attributed to the ability of arginine to form bidentate cation–π interactions with aromatic residues and to participate in stronger hydrogen bonding, interactions that lysine cannot readily form [84, 137, 138]. Capturing these effects in residue-resolution coarse-grained models, such as Mpipi [80] and CALVADOS [133, 139], has led to enhanced predictability and accuracy for protein and nucleic acid phase-separated systems.

  Apart from describing the chemical specificity of individual residues, a related objective is also to capture effects due to chemical modifications —largely post-transcriptional modifications (PTMs), such as phosphorylation, methylation, and acetylation. In the case of phosphorylation, a phosphate group is added to residues such as serine and tyrosine, which changes their overall charge. Since such modifications can dramatically tune the ability of proteins to undergo phase separation, there has been much interest in describing them via residue-resolution coarse-grained models. This is done one of two ways: exploring the impacts of mimetics or designing specific coarse-grained parameters for PTMs. For instance, in experiments, Asp or Glu have been used as mimetics for phosphoserines—i.e., to mimic the impact of enhanced negative charges due to phosphorylation, and such strategies can also be adopted in simulations. Phosphorylation-mimicking mutations in prion-like domains often reduce phase separation by adding negative charges. Simulations with the original HPS model could qualitatively capture the shift in the phase boundary caused by phosphomimetic mutations in FUS [132]. Another study demonstrated that the Mpipi model correctly reproduces experimental phase separation trends upon single-residue mutations in FUS, DDX4, and LAF-1, underscoring that sequence-specific predictions hold even at the level of point mutations [80, 140]. However, as in experiments, mimetics may not always faithfully represent the impact of ‘true’ PTMs; therefore, there have been efforts to incorporate more chemically specific parameters in coarse-grained models [141-144]. It is also worth noting that in addition to proteins, nucleic acid residues can also undergo modifications—such as post-transcriptional modifications of RNA, which also impact their phase behavior [145]. Thus, designing coarse-grained parameters for these effects would be a worthwhile undertaking—helping to decode the sequence–phase behavior landscape of molecules such as RNA.

  All together, state-of-the-art coarse-grained models have moved beyond treating sequences as generic polymers and now encode chemically-specific interactions that allow us to predict biomolecular phase behavior more robustly.


### Multicomponent biomolecular condensates
  Residue–resolution coarse–grained models allow us to encode sequence specificity, and key molecular features that are crucial for phase separation (i.e., charge patterning, cation–π, π–π, hydropathy) while remaining fast enough to simulate mixtures of different components at once. In multicomponent settings, these models typically represent proteins at one bead per residue and RNAs/DNAs at one bead per nucleotide, coupling short–range sequence–dependent interactions with screened electrostatics.

  Critically for mixtures, residue–level coarse-grained models recover composition–dependent phase behavior. Specifically, coarse-grained models have been employed to show that (i) charge stoichiometry and patterning can control reentrant phase separation in a salt-dependent manner [72]; (ii) RNA can act as a scaffold, client, or suppressor depending on its chain length and relative concentration [146]; (iii) multicomponent condensates can form multilayered architectures via changes in their relative heterotypic vs. homotypic interaction strengths [147, 148]; and (iv) sequence modifications can modulate both partitioning and mobility in the dense phase [149, 150].

  While limitations remain—current nucleotide coarse-grained terms (stacking/planarity, ion specificity) and protein–RNA cross–parameters are still being refined—residue–resolution models offer a practical, sequence-sensitive route to study multicomponent condensates without invoking minimal abstractions, bringing us one step closer to a physicochemically accurate description of the cellular environment.


### Predicting saturation concentrations and accurate phase diagrams
  A primary outcome of residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations of biomolecular phase separation is the quantitative prediction of phase boundaries—i.e., determining the saturation concentration (csat) at which condensates form or the critical solution conditions, such as critical temperature Tc or critical salt concentration at which condensates form or dissolve. Experimentally, saturation concentration is often measured by the threshold concentration for turbidity or droplet formation. In contrast, the concentrations of dilute and dense phases can sometimes be determined by techniques such as centrifugation or NMR. Critica solution temperatures can be inferred from temperature-dependent phase separation assays. An accurate and transferable coarse-grained model should reproduce these observables across different sequences and conditions. Recent residue-resolution models have made significant strides toward this goal, allowing direct quantitative comparison of computed and experimental phase boundaries.

  To probe biomolecular phase separation using residue-resolution coarse-grained models, two simulation strategies dominate. Direct-coexistence slab simulations place dense and dilute phases in the same elongated box and have been used for proteins [132, 134, 139, 151, 152], protein–RNA droplets [121, 153] and even chromatin fibres [56, 57] (2h,l) In this approach, molecules are first compressed into a cubic slab, which is then elongated along one of its dimensions This setup lowers the nucleation barrier, enabling faster equilibration and allowing the dense and dilute phases of the system to be simulated simultaneously. By conducting such simulations under various conditions, such as at different temperatures, full phase diagrams can be computed. In contrast, other approaches extract phase boundaries without pre-constructing an interface; a prime example being Monte Carlo simulations [154-156], performed in either the canonical or Gibbs ensemble.

  By leveraging such techniques, recent coarse-grained simulations now achieve near-experimental accuracy across very different chemistries [72, 80, 123, 129, 139, 157, 158] Direct comparisons against experimental data—including cloud-point measurements, turbidity assays, centrifugation-based partitioning, and coexistence curves visualized by microscopy or light scattering—show good agreement for several state-of-the-art coarse-grained force fields. Because the interaction matrices are residue- or nucleotide-specific single-point mutations, post-translational modifications, or base substitutions translate directly into measurable phase-diagram shifts [123, 159].

  Advanced sampling further sharpens predictions. Techniques such as thermodynamic integration (TI), Gibbs ensemble simulations, and histogram reweighting have also been used to improve sampling and extract free energy differences across phases. For example, Espinosa et al. applied TI in conjunction with direct-coexistence simulations to obtain high-resolution binodal curves and concentration thresholds for minimal protein condensates [160].

  Overall, the wide range of chemistries studied and the strong agreement with experiments show that residue-level coarse-grained simulations can reliably map phase behavior and guide the design of biomolecular condensates.


### Measuring condensate material properties
  In addition to predicting phase boundaries, residue-resolution coarse-grained models are increasingly leveraged to quantify material properties of biomolecular condensates. Key observables include surface tension, shear viscosity, and viscoelastic moduli, as well as fusion and coalescence dynamics, and molecular diffusion and conformational behavior within the dense phase.


#### Surface tension
  Surface tension determines the energetic cost of creating an interface, which in turn influences droplet shape, capillary flows, and coarsening kinetics of biomolecular condensates. In residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations, γ is computed from either the pressure-tensor anisotropy across a planar interface in a direct-coexistence setup [161, 162], or from capillary-wave analysis of equilibrium shape fluctuations of a droplet, which provides an interface-based estimate that is independent of previously obtaining an explicit equation of state [157, 163]. Across protein-only, protein–RNA and nucleic-acid–rich condensates, both experiments and residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations consistently report interfacial tensions around the same order of magnitude (ranging from tens to a few hundred μN m−1), although achieving exact quantitative agreement is still proving to be a challenge. Nevertheless, simulations capture key trends, showing, for example, that γ decreases with salt in systems where electrostatics are the main drivers of biomolecular condensation, and increases with the density of interaction-promoting residues or stronger cation-π interactions [157, 164]. Coarse-grained simulations also show that in mixed systems, interfacially enriched molecules can act as natural surfactants that lower the surface tension and, in turn, promote multilayered organization—for example, short RNAs can accumulate at interfaces and lower γ, and amphiphilic/surfactant-like proteins similarly reduce interfacial tension in a concentration-dependent manner [121, 153, 164].


#### Shear viscosity
  Viscosity governs the internal rearrangement, diffusion, and the rate at which droplets relax after deformation. In residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations, η can be obtained either via Green–Kubo relations (time integral of the equilibrium stress autocorrelation), or from long-time particle diffusion inside the dense phase [121, 150, 165, 166]. Experimental analogues for these measurements are FRAP/microrheology and single-particle tracking (Figure 2f,j). A robust outcome across studies is that these models can reliably capture relative viscosity trends—e.g. how sequence changes, post-translational modifications, or RNA fraction shift η—without having been parametrized to fit experimental viscosity data [167]. Absolute η values however, tend to be systematically low because the energy landscape is smoother at residue resolution, a result of fewe side-chain sterics and specific H-bond barriers. Additionally, long-range hydrodynamics are not resolved by conventional thermostats. As a result, reported η should therefore be used primarily for rankings or condition-dependent shifts [165-167], rather than quantitative accurate predictors of shear viscosity.


#### Viscoelastic response
  Moving beyond single viscosity measures, we find that condensates often exhibit Maxwell-like viscoelastic moduli, with distinct relaxation times that depend on thei amino acid composition and nucleic acid content. Residue-resolution coarse-grained models compute viscoelastic moduli by transforming the stress autocorrelation tensor into a time-dependent modulus G(t), and then fitting to Maxwel modes, or by applying small-amplitude shear to measure G′ (ω) and G"(ω) [165, 166, 168]. Experimental analogues include microrheology and oscillatory optical-tweezer assays and simulations can reproduce the frequency-dependent crossover from viscous to elastic response as well as show how sticker density or RNA chain length and composition can shift relaxation times, consistent with tunable viscoelasticity observed in protein–RNA condensates [121, 166] (Figure 2f,j). Similar to η, the missing hydrodynamics and smoothed interaction energy landscapes lower the absolute measured values, making comparative trends the most reliable readout.


#### Fusion and coalescence dynamics
  During a simulation, two previously equilibrated droplets can be brought into contact, and coalescence can be computed by tracking (i) the increase in the radius of the liquid bridge (“neck”) that forms between them and (ii) how fast the initially elongated merged droplet becomes spherical Simulations consistently reproduce the ordering of fusion times seen upon sequence mutations or changes in RNA fraction: systems with higher inferred γ/η fuse faster (more liquid-like), while those with weaker interfacial driving or higher resistance fuse slowly [102, 169, 170] (2g,k). As with γ and η, the main caveat is, once again, that solvent hydrodynamics are coarse-grained, so absolute times differ nonetheless, relative differences are robust and trace back to the same residue-level interactions that dominate γ and η [169].


#### Diffusion and conformational behavior
  A key appeal of MD simulations is their ability to provide a consistent framework in which microscopic descriptors of biomolecules (e.g., single-chain conformations, contact maps - 2e,i) can be systematically connected to mesoscopic transport properties (e.g., self-diffusion) and, ultimately, to macroscopic material responses of biomolecular condensates. For instance, self-diffusion inside and outside droplets can be quantified from mean–squared displacements and van Hove functions, and directly rationalized from underlying single-molecule conformations and interaction patterns [132, 140, 157, 171].

  Measuring the conformational states of molecules within condensates is typically challenging to probe experimentally. To this end, residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations have recently been leveraged to characterize conformational ensembles of IDPs within condensates. In particular, it has been shown that IDPs adopt more expanded conformations inside condensates compared to the dilute phase [150, 172]. Simulations can also reveal the interactivity of molecules within condensates, thereby shedding light on condensate microstructure. For example, interactivity within condensates has been found to be highly heterogeneous, even for condensates formed by the same protein [172]. Recent work has demonstrated strong connections between single-IDP conformations and their interactivity within condensates, illuminating how emergent macroscopic material properties are encoded in these systems [173]. Together, by characterizing single-molecule structure and dynamics inside condensates, residue-resolution simulations can provide valuable insight into the origins of condensate emergent properties.


### Modeling nucleation kinetics and droplet size distributions
  Residue-resolution coarse-grained modeling has made progress in describing how condensates nucleate and grow, shedding light on kinetic aspects such as nucleation barriers and the size distributions of droplets [63, 174]. A practical strategy is to run many small-scale simulations in the canonical ensemble and analyze their outcome through the lens of droplet nucleation theory [174]. From steady state droplet statistics, we can estimate the nucleation free-energy barrier without pre-imposing an interface [175]. Time-dependent phenomena like lag times, Ostwald ripening, or the effect of volume and concentration on nucleation rates can be explored in silico, allowing for the capture of how sequence features influence nucleation: for instance, simulations indicate that proteins with lower surface tension (often due to charge–charge repulsion or high solubility) tend to nucleate droplets more easily [100, 153]. This kind of insight provides a mechanistic understanding of droplet size distributions observed in cells (many small vs. few large droplets) as an emergent property of the underlying nucleation landscape.


### Accounting for the environment: temperature, salt, and pH effects
  Environmental conditions heavily modulate condensate stability and material properties, and modern residue-resolution models encode these in several ways: salt conditions are typically introduced via screened electrostatics (implicit ions) or ion-specific terms, and pH is implemented by altering net charge and patterning via residue protonation states. Together, these features let the same simulations link sequence determinants to phase behavior under realistic solution conditions [80, 132, 133, 139].

  Because reduced temperature T* couples to contact energetics, changing T tunes coil–globule equilibria and coexistence curves. Models such as Mpipi and CALVADOS translate this into near-quantitative sequence-dependent shifts in critical temperatures for systems exhibiting upper critical solution temperature behaviors [80, 133, 139]. Similar efforts have focused on developing residue-resolution models—such as Mpipi-T [176] and HPS-T [70]—that describe protein phase separation at elevated temperatures; i.e., lower critical solution temperature (LCST)-type behaviors.

  For multivalent and charged biomolecules, raising monovalent salt screens electrostatic attraction and repulsion and can both suppress [101, 102] or promote phase separation [55, 56]. At higher salt, non-ionic attractions can take over and restore phase separation [72]. Residue-resolution coarse-grained models implement salt either implicitly—for instance via a Debye–Hückel or a non-symmetric Yukawa screening term—-or explicitly, with coarse-grained ions and/or water [177]. Both implicit and explicit approaches capture composition-dependent trends, including reentrant regimes and RNA-mediated modulation in mixtures [72, 81, 132, 134, 178]. Practical caveats, nonetheless, remain: implicit screening misses ion partitioning and specific ion effects (e.g., Mg2+); while coarse-grained models with explicit ions improve realism they incur in significantly higher computational costs, and introduce parameterization limits. To address this gap, the residue-resolution model Mpipi-Recharged considers ions implicitly but introduces a pair-specific electrostatic treatment, thereby approximating some specific ion effects without the full overhead of explicit models [81].

  pH reshapes charge patterning and thus electrostatic driving forces [73]. At residue resolution, the simplest treatment fixes protonation states at a target pH; increasingly, constant-pH coarse-grained schemes or titratable beads allow for protonation dynamics during simulation, thereby improving transferability across pH without requiring reparameterization. Recent coarse-grained implementations demonstrate pH-dependent conformations and phase tendencies, suggesting a route to connect pH, charge regulation, and phase separation within residue-resolution simulations [179-181].

  Currently, residue-resolution models support environment-aware predictions with growing realistic predictions. The main frontiers are accurate ion specificity, mixed-salt buffers, and robust constant-pH treatments that scale to multicomponent condensates while preserving sequence fidelity.


### Describing biomolecular structure
  Many of the studies discussed above have focused on residue-resolution coarse-grained modeling of disordered biomolecules. Several state-of-the-art residue resolution force fields have been parameterized to describe the role of disordered proteins in biomolecular phase separation. However, beyond disordered sequences, biomolecular condensates also contain structured protein domains, such as those found in multidomain RNA-binding proteins and enzyme structures, as well as RNA molecules that can adopt a wide range of secondary and tertiary folds. Within disordered sequences themselves, regions can adopt transient secondary structures. These structured domains can significantly alter the interaction landscapes of biomolecules and strongly influence condensate material properties [182]. There is, therefore, an increasing need to design models that better account for the full configurational landscapes that biomolecules can sample inside condensates.

  In general, in residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations, structured domains in proteins have been represented using rigid-body dynamics. In this approach, experimentally solved structures, such as those from protein data, or predicted structures, such as those from AlphaFold2 [183], are first coarse-grained to the residue resolution and then modeled as rigid bodies [80, 81, 132]. This representation preserves the topology of the folded regions and has been used to qualitatively capture the behavior of multidomain proteins in condensates [72, 89, 98, 100, 132, 184, 185]. However, rigid body dynamics do not allow for structural changes in these domains that may occur in condensates, and efforts are underway to describe structured domains with more flexible potentials [56, 186-189].


## CONNECTING ATOMISTIC AND COARSE-GRAINED LENGTH SCALES
  Coarse-grained models offer substantial computational speed-ups by simplifying molecular representations, but this comes at the expense of atomic-level detail. As a result, key interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, side-chain packing, and specific solvent-mediated effects, are not captured faithfully at coarse resolution. A practical workflow involves first simulating biomolecular phase separation using a coarser model, which remains computationally feasible over large spatial and temporal scales, and then investigating specific molecular interactions at higher resolution [97, 99-101].

  To achieve this, a common strategy involves first equilibrating the condensate using a coarse-grained model, followed by the reconstruction of an all-atom configuration—a process known as backmapping. Backmapping is an efficient method for bridging spatial and temporal scales, as it enables the atomistic analysis of systems initially sampled at coarse resolution. This allows for the examination of fine-grained interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and side-chain packing, that are not captured in coarse-grained models. Additionally, backmapping provides a means to validate coarse-grained predictions against higher-resolution data.

  This procedure, however, poses several challenges, as a single coarse-grained configuration can correspond to many possible atomistic structures, making the reconstruction ambiguous. One approach is to use lookup tables or fragment libraries that map coarse-grained configurations to representative all-atom structures, as well as geometrically guided initialization [190, 191]. Several pipelines integrate this, such as the Martini backmapping script [191] and PULCHRA [192]. While such methods provide a practical means of reconstructing atomistic detail, accurately restoring local geometry is non-trivial and prone to steric clashes of side chains or unrealistic bond lengths and angles, with the required energy minimization often distorting the origina conformational ensemble.

  Data-driven approaches improve reconstruction accuracy by systematically incorporating structural information derived from empirical data and large-scale databases [193-196]. Recent developments in this area have demonstrated substantial improvements in recovering clash-free structures with correct bond geometries: one example is the DiA-MoNDBack denoising diffusion model [197]. Another notable approach is cg2all, introduced by Heo and Feig [198] which employs a deep learning framework with a loss function that combines data-driven components with physics-based terms, including torsional energy penalties. Such methods demonstrate higher accuracy in reconstructing both side-chain conformations and backbone geometries Together, the existing backmapping approaches enhance the reliability of multiscale modeling workflows and enable more seamless integration between coarse-grained and atomistic simulations.


## DATA-DRIVEN AND MACHINE-LEARNING APPROACHES
  As computational power grows, it becomes increasingly feasible to model biomolecular systems at finer resolution and on even greater length and time scales, thereby enabling the screening of a large number of molecules without the need for costly experiments. This enables more data driven approaches to studying phase separation. For instance, coarse-grained simulations of bulk condensates have been used to derive scaling rules that allow us to predict how the upper solution critical temperature for phase separation Tc, changes with respect to different sequence mutations for a particular class of IDPs, prion-like domains [123].

  Of course, bulk simulations of hundreds of protein chains are still relatively expensive to run, compared to simulations of a single IDP sequence. In this regard, the correlation between bulk properties like Tc, and single-molecule properties such as the theta temperature for coil-to-globule transition temperature and single-chain compaction measured via its radius of gyration [106, 131] can be exploited to make predictions of bulk phase behavior from less computationally-expensive single-chain simulations [199]. The correlation between phase separating propensity of IDPs, their second viral coefficient, calculated from the potential of mean force determined from two-chain simulations, and diffusion in the condensed phase has also been recently demonstrated [200]. Interestingly, the authors then applied an active learning approach to identify protein sequences that break this correlation, where there is increased mobility while still maintaining the stability of the condensate, to identify the sequence determinants of the ‘thermodynamics-dynamics trade-off’. This demonstrates how sequence design using machine-learned approaches can be used to tune thermodynamic and dynamic properties independently.

  In this section, we summarize some of the recent data-driven and machine-learning (ML) approaches for modeling biomolecular phase separation (Figure 3).


### Direct predictors of properties from sequence
  Given the wealth of pre-existing data on biomolecular phase separation, from both experimental and computational works, this could be readily exploited to train machine-learned predictors for predicting properties of biomolecules regarding phase separation. Indeed, machine-learning approaches have been used to develop sequence-based predictors of protein phase separation behavior (Figure 3d). These predictors are largely trained on experimental data to predict whether an IDP will phase separate or not [201-204]. Predictors that use calculated likelihoods of π–π contact frequency in IDPs from sequence composition to determine phase separating propensity have also been developed [84]. While most of these predictors are developed to classify IDPs into phase-separating and non-phase-separating groups, recent efforts have been made to develop models that can provide quantitative or context-specific predictions. For example, semiquantitative predictions for phase diagrams have been produced from estimates of homo- and heterotypic interactions derived from coarsegrained model parameters using a mean-field approximation [205]. Neural-network and deep-learning models have also been trained on single-chain and bulk direct-coexistence simulations of IDPs (Figure 3c), as well as experimental data (Figure 3b), to make quantitative predictions based solely on the IDP sequence (Figure 3g). These have included predictions of both single-chain conformational properties (e.g., radius of gyration, scaling exponents, asphericity) [205], as well as phase properties such as the free energies of transfer from the dilute to the dense phase and saturation concentrations [206].

  Models that predict whether a particular protein will partition into condensates in vivo have also been developed, largely trained on information of known components of membraneless organelles in cells rather than purified systems [207, 208]. Overall, these machine-learned models can first confirm pre-existing established rules and potentially provide new insights into the relationship between sequence features and phase separation propensities. From a more practical standpoint, harnessing the predictive power of such models to generate results typically obtained from simulations, but at a fraction of the computational cost, can be helpful in engineering protein sequences with specific properties and aid in the design of experimental studies. Although these ideas have largely been applied to single-component condensates, they could be extended to include multi-component systems and make predictions for mixtures of proteins and RNAs, and even small molecules that could function as drugs.

  A current limitation is that data for such multi-component systems are not readily available for training, making machine-learning approaches infeasible. Nevertheless, recent efforts have been made in building predictive models for multi-component phase separation. For example, a combination of two predictive models, one for in vitro phase separation propensity and one for partitioning of proteins into reconstituted RNA-rich condensates has been used to predict the effect of oligonucleotides on phase separation [209]. Altogether, as more data on multi-component mixtures and their phase behavior become more readily available, along with more studies investigating the molecular determinants that control multiphase condensation, we expect that machine-learned approaches and predictive modeling will become more common.

  By harnessing the power of molecular simulations, especially residue-resolution coarse-grained simulations, there is potential in generating larger datasets to train ML-based models. However, this will, of course, transfer inherent biases and limitations from the underlying force field to the predictions, as the ML models can only be as good as their input training data. As such, care has to be taken when curating the initial dataset, as well as being aware of the context and limitations of the resulting model when using it for other applications.


### Machine-learning approaches to improve modeling accuracy
  In addition to property prediction from sequence machine-learning approaches have also been used to aid in force-field development [135, 136, 139, 210]. Interaction parameters in coarse-grained protein models have been optimized using Bayesian inference on phase separation data (Figure 3e). For example, the CALVADOS model [133, 139, 188] was parameterized using a top-down Bayesian approach to optimize parameters for the residue-resolution model against experimental SAXS and NMR data (Figure 3b). Besides using ML-based approaches to optimize force field parameters, recently machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) have also emerged as powerful tools for simulating atomic and molecular systems with near quantum mechanical accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost [211, 212]. Traditional electronic structure methods can provide reliable energy landscapes but are computationally expensive, limiting their applicability to large system sizes or long-timescale simulations needed to study biomolecular phase separation. MLIPs can potentially bridge this gap by leveraging machine-learning methods to learn potential energy surfaces using a bottom-up approach from high-accuracy quantum mechanical data, enabling efficient and accurate molecular simulations. These models including Gaussian approximation potentials, moment tensor potentials, and various forms of neural network potentials, have demonstrated remarkable success across diverse domains such as materials science and catalysis [213]. Recent advancements have led to MLIPs that have already been pre-trained on diverse datasets, showcasing how universal force fields and foundational machine learning models can be developed [214, 215], with some of these foundational models being able to be further fine-tuned to refine a pre-trained model on a new dataset. Although these approaches are currently still not feasible to be used for biomolecular systems, as computational chemistry moves toward increasingly complex systems, MLIPs could offer a promising avenue for examining molecular interactions and resulting phase behavior with unprecedented speed and precision (Figure 3h).


### Direct sampling from generative models
  In addition to using ML-based approaches to improve the accuracy of force fields for bimolecular modeling, ML methods have also been used to carry out direct sampling of the structural and conformational ensembles of IDPs via generative models (Figure 3f), as an alternative to traditional molecular simulation-based sampling. These generative models can learn to sample the equilibrium distributions of different conformations of e.g., an IDP, by training on a combination of atomistic or coarse-grained simulation data (Figure 3b) and/or experimental data (Figure 3c) such as structures derived from SAXS, NMR spectroscopy, depending on the implementation. Among these, diffusion models have emerged as particularly promising for application to IDP structural modeling. By learning to reverse a noise-injection process to generate structure from randomness progressively, diffusion models can learn the distribution of conformations representative of a target ensemble [216]. Recent examples such as ExEnDiff [217], IDPFold [218], and STARLING [219] apply this framework to IDPs and other flexible biomolecules, using either backbone-level or coarse-grained representations. These models often incorporate physical priors, such as the expected scaling behavior of the radius of gyration, local torsional constraints, or repulsive energy terms, into their training objectives, helping to enforce plausibility and consistency with polymer physics. Ideally, we want a generative model to be able to sample the conformational ensemble of an IDP with only its sequence and surrounding conditions as input. In this manner, generative models are especially attractive because of their potential for generating large conformational ensembles at low computational cost (Figure 3i). While they do not capture the dynamics of a system, they provide an efficient and scalable tool for characterizing disordered states.

  However, it is essential to note that current generative models are typically limited to single-molecule ensemble sampling and are not yet feasible for capturing collective behaviors that emerge in condensed phases with thousands of proteins. As such, they cannot yet account for intermolecular interactions or emergent properties, such as phase separation, which often require an explicit treatment of many-body interactions. Additionally, as with any other machine-learning approach, their performance can be sensitive to the quality and diversity of the training data, and generalization to sequences or conditions vastly different from those in the training set could be a challenge. As such, while generative models offer a powerful tool for sampling individual chains, modeling the full complexity of bulk systems, such as biomolecular condensates, still requires more physically grounded simulation-based approaches at present.


### Simulation- and ML-guided design of phase-separated biomolecular systems
  Finally, beyond sampling and modeling, ML approaches have increasingly been used to guide the rational design of biomolecular systems, ranging from condensates of single protein sequences to more complex multicomponent biomolecular mixtures with specific structural or material properties and phase behavior (Figure 4). In the context of IDPs, ML models trained on sequence–ensembl or sequence–property relationships derived from simulation or experimental data (Figure 4a, b) can be used to propose mutations or entirely new sequences that shift the conformational ensemble or modulate condensate behavior towards what is desired [205]. Recent methods have also extended these design approaches to multicomponent systems, including protein–RNA mixtures or synthetic IDR blends, optimizing not only individual sequences but also their collective interaction profiles to achieve programmable co-assembly, selective partitioning, or multilayered phase separation. For example, evolutionary approaches, such as genetic algorithms (Figure 4c), have been used to fine-tune multiphasic behavior by exploring vast sequence spaces, enabling control over phase composition and spatial organization in biomolecular condensates [147, 220], and can also be employed to propose amino acid substitutions that deliberately drive condensates toward increased or decreased stability [159]. Such methods can also be applied to optimize sequences and mixtures of biomolecules towards other properties that may be of interest, such as finding sequences that form condensates in which certain small molecules or specific components are enriched or excluded. Recent work has also been done to design pairwise interaction networks in highly multi-component mixtures to control the resulting phase behavior. For example, genetic algorithms have been used to iteratively optimize interaction matrices (Figure 4c), producing a precise number of resulting coexisting phases in a mean-field pairwise-interaction model [221]. Surprisingly, obtaining interaction matrices that give a certain target phase count was easily achieved, and multiple solutions were often found. Although structured interaction matrices can produce reliable phase counts, the genetic algorithm approach was able to find solutions that are more random and less obviously structured. Although this work focused on obtaining robust phase counts without specificity for the exact composition of the phases that form, the large number of possible solutions might allow for the control of partitioning of key components with additional optimization. Altogether, these tools offer a promising avenue for designing biomolecular materials with specific properties (Figure 4d), with many potential applications ranging from synthetic biology to biomaterials and to therapeutic condensates.


## OUTLOOK AND OPEN QUESTIONS
  Computational modeling has become an indispensable tool for studying biomolecular condensates. In this review, we have highlighted advances in atomistic modeling, coarse-grained simulations, and data-driven approaches for probing condensates. These approaches enable us to probe condensate behaviors across spatiotemporal scales that are often inaccessible to experiments, while also enhancing our ability to predict condensate properties from molecular composition at a fraction of the cost. Moreover, these techniques provide fundamental insights into the physical and molecular driving forces that dictate emergent condensate behaviors, helping connect features of individual molecules to collective phase properties. However, computational approaches can vary widely in their resolution (i.e., level of molecular detail), timescales, length scales, and, as a result, the kinds of questions they can address. Additionally, different approaches often provide complementary insights on condensate behavior. Table I summarizes each class of approaches discussed in this review—highlighting their key advantages and caveats, as well as remarking on the alignment of their predictions with experiments.

  While enormous progress has been made over the past decade in leveraging computational modeling for studying biomolecular condensates, several open challenges remain. Below, we outline some key considerations:


### Polarization effects, ions, and solvent.
  Atomistic models can, in principle, capture polarization, ionic, and solvent effects, but they remain prohibitively expensive for studying collective condensate behaviors. To our knowledge, no study has yet considered polarization at atomistic resolution for entire condensates. Coarse-grained models, particularly residue-resolution approaches, typically neglect polarization and treat ions and solvent implicitly. How much is lost by these approximations remains an open question [74]. While such models can reproduce qualitative trends in material properties, their predictions are often off by orders of magnitude from experimental values. A uniform ion background may be too crude to capture the physics of highly charged condensates [222]. Furthermore, if such effects are incorporated, how much data would be required to train machine-learning surrogates effectively?


### Non-equilibrium behavior.
  Cellular condensates exist in inherently non-equilibrium environments, yet most current computational studies focus on thermodynamic equilibrium. Non-equilibrium simulations could yield representations that more closely resemble phase separation in cells, where active processes (e.g., enzymatic reactions) regulate condensate formation and size. Theoretical advances have been made in this space [223, 224], and recent simulation studies have implemented algorithms for select systems condensate aging [88, 166, 185, 225, 226] and phosphorylation-dependent regulation [227]. However, these algorithms are not straightforward to implement, as they require accounting for complex processes such approximating the effects of inter-protein disorder-to-order transitions or considering bond breaking and formation.


### Condensate kinetics.
  Closely related to non-equilibrium modeling are open questions about the kinetics and dynamics of condensate formation. For example, what are the relevant nucleation pathways and timescales, and are these accessible to molecular simulations? How can we design enhanced sampling methods that accelerate access to relevant timescales while still retaining information about the system’s dynamical evolution?


### Modeling larger condensates.
  As computational resources continue to grow, it is becoming increasingly feasible to simulate larger systems and approach length scales representative of cellular condensates. Can we model entire condensates with all their molecular components, and what insights would such simulations provide? Relatedly, how do multiple condensates interact with each other and with the crowded cellular environment? These questions point to new directions for exploring condensate biophysics at biologically relevant scales.
