## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, and figure legends. Supplementary figures and tables are referenced but not provided in full.
- **Assessment boundary** Assessment is limited to the scientific content, methodology, and claims as presented in the provided manuscript text. No assessment of editorial fit or journal-specific policy is made.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report that in ternary mixtures of two RNA homopolymers (purine-rich poly-rA and pyrimidine-rich poly-rC) and polyethylene glycol (PEG), differential interaction strengths determine which RNA acts as a scaffold (driving phase separation) versus an adsorbent (localizing at interfaces). Lattice-based Monte Carlo simulations reproduce experimental observations and predict distinct orientational preferences for scaffolds versus adsorbents at condensate interfaces. Single-molecule tracking reveals anisotropic motion near interfaces, with faster diffusion parallel to the interface than perpendicular to it.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures 1–5 with legends; methods section; figure legends for supplementary figures S1–S10; supplementary table 1 is referenced but not shown.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures S1–S10 and Supplementary Table 1 are referenced but not provided. Source data files are mentioned but not accessible. The full methods for the single-molecule tracking analysis (e.g., RoSE-O algorithm details, channel registration) are only partially described. The exact fitting procedures for phase boundaries and tie-line proxies are not fully detailed.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a systematic and well-integrated study combining experiments, simulations, and single-molecule imaging to address a timely question in biomolecular condensate biology: what determines whether a molecule acts as a scaffold or an adsorbent at condensate interfaces, and what are the resulting interfacial properties. The combination of a well-defined model system (poly-rA, poly-rC, PEG), quantitative phase diagram measurements, lattice-based simulations, and single-molecule tracking is a strength. The central claims regarding differential interaction strengths as the primary determinant of scaffold versus adsorbent behavior, and the resulting orientational and dynamical anisotropies at interfaces, are plausible and supported by the presented evidence. However, several technical details and quantitative validations are needed to fully establish the case, particularly regarding the interpretation of the single-molecule tracking data and the generality of the simulation predictions.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of biomolecular condensates, phase separation, soft matter physics, and RNA biology would be interested. The work provides a conceptual framework for classifying condensate components as scaffolds versus adsorbents, which is relevant for understanding the organization and function of membraneless organelles. The findings on interfacial anisotropy have implications for designing synthetic condensates with tunable material properties and for understanding how reactions might be spatially organized at condensate interfaces. The methodological combination of experiments and simulations is also of interest to the broader biophysics community.
- **Major strengths**
  1. The study uses a minimal, well-controlled model system (two RNA homopolymers and PEG) that allows systematic variation of interaction strengths, concentrations, lengths, and stiffness, enabling a clean test of the governing principles.
  2. The integration of experiments (confocal imaging, microfluidics-based phase diagrams) with lattice-based simulations (LaSSI) provides a strong complementary approach, with simulations reproducing the experimental phenomenology and extending predictions to molecular orientations.
  3. The single-molecule tracking experiments directly probe dynamics at interfaces, revealing a clear and quantifiable anisotropy (faster parallel vs. perpendicular motion), which is a novel and important observation.
  4. The manuscript is generally well-written and logically structured, with clear figures that support the main narrative.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No, Axis: Technical soundness of single-molecule interpretation)
    - **Claim pointer** The claim that "motions of probe molecules parallel to the interface are faster than motions perpendicular to the interface" is presented as evidence of dynamical anisotropy at condensate interfaces.
    - **Evidence pointer** Figure 5, section "Observations of anisotropic motions near condensate interfaces", Methods "Single-molecule image analysis, localizations and tracking".
    - **Concern** The single-molecule tracking experiments use YO-PRO-1, a dye that binds to RNA. The authors interpret the tracked motion as that of poly-rA molecules. However, YO-PRO-1 is a small molecule that can potentially diffuse freely, and its binding to RNA may be transient. The measured motion could reflect a combination of dye diffusion, binding/unbinding kinetics, and RNA motion. The manuscript does not clearly establish that the tracked trajectories report on the motion of the RNA polymer itself, rather than the dye's own dynamics. Furthermore, the authors state that "we interpret a continuous burst of fluorescence as a single YO-PRO-1 molecule emitting while bound to a single poly-rA molecule before photobleaching or dissociation," but the criteria for distinguishing a bound state from a freely diffusing dye near the interface are not fully described. The potential for the dye to perturb the local RNA structure or dynamics is also not discussed.
    - **Why it matters** The central dynamical claim of the paper rests on this single-molecule measurement. If the tracked motion is not representative of RNA polymer dynamics, the conclusion of dynamical anisotropy at interfaces would be significantly weakened. The interpretation of the anisotropy as arising from the viscoelastic properties of the interface also depends on this.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should provide control experiments or analyses to demonstrate that the tracked motion reflects RNA dynamics. This could include: (1) varying the dye concentration to show that the measured diffusion coefficients are independent of dye loading; (2) comparing the motion of YO-PRO-1 in the absence of RNA (if it does not bind) or in a non-condensing control; (3) using a covalently labeled RNA (e.g., with a bright organic dye at a specific position) to directly track RNA motion; (4) providing a more detailed analysis of the fluorescence intensity traces to distinguish bound vs. unbound states and showing that the bound state duration is consistent with the observation window.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No, Axis: Generalizability of simulation predictions)
    - **Claim pointer** The claim that "relative interaction strengths are the most important variable in determining whether polymers adsorb to interfaces" is presented as a general principle.
    - **Evidence pointer** Figures 3, 4, S5–S8, section "Differential interaction strengths are the main drivers of adsorption".
    - **Concern** The simulations systematically vary interaction strengths, concentrations, lengths, and stiffness, and conclude that interaction strength is the dominant factor. However, the parameter space explored is limited to a specific set of homopolymer models with a particular interaction scheme (nearest-neighbor on a lattice). The conclusion that interaction strength is "the most important" variable may not hold for other polymer architectures (e.g., block copolymers, branched polymers), other interaction types (e.g., specific, saturable binding sites), or in systems where the solvent quality is significantly different. The authors do not provide a quantitative measure of "importance" (e.g., a sensitivity analysis) to support this ranking.
    - **Why it matters** The paper aims to provide design rules for synthetic condensates. If the dominance of interaction strength is not robust across a wider parameter space, the design rules would be of limited utility. The claim is strong and needs to be tempered or supported by a broader exploration of parameter space.
    - **Resolution test** The authors could perform a more systematic sensitivity analysis, varying each parameter over a wider range and quantifying the effect on adsorption (e.g., using a measure like the excess interfacial concentration). They could also test a few additional polymer architectures (e.g., a block copolymer of A and C) to see if the qualitative conclusion holds. If not, the claim should be revised to be more specific to the class of systems studied.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No, Axis: Quantitative link between simulation and experiment)
    - **Claim pointer** The statement that "Lattice-based simulations reproduce the phenomenology observed in experiments" implies a quantitative or semi-quantitative match between simulation and experiment.
    - **Evidence pointer** Figures 1–4, section "Differential interaction strengths are the main drivers of adsorption".
    - **Concern** The comparison between simulations and experiments is largely qualitative. The simulations use a coarse-grained model with arbitrary interaction energies (in units of kT) and lattice spacing, while the experiments use specific polymers (poly-rA, poly-rC) with known molecular weights and PEG. The manuscript does not provide a mapping between the simulation parameters and the experimental conditions (e.g., how does a change in εB-A in the simulation correspond to a change in NaCl or PEG concentration in the experiment?). Without such a mapping, it is difficult to assess how faithfully the simulations reproduce the experimental system, and whether the predicted orientational preferences are quantitatively relevant.
    - **Why it matters** The predictive power of the simulations is a key part of the paper's contribution. A qualitative match is suggestive but not fully convincing. A quantitative or semi-quantitative link would greatly strengthen the claim that the simulations provide mechanistic insight into the experimental system.
    - **Resolution test** The authors should attempt to map simulation parameters to experimental conditions. For example, they could use the measured tie-line slopes or critical concentrations to estimate the effective interaction strengths in the simulation. They could also compare a quantitative observable, such as the interfacial thickness or the partition coefficient of the adsorbent, between simulation and experiment. If a quantitative match is not possible, the authors should clearly state the limitations of the qualitative comparison.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity of presentation, Affected element: Figure 1, Evidence pointer: Figure 1 legend)
    - **Issue** The figure legend for Figure 1 is dense and could be clearer. The distinction between "homogenous" (i of f, I of g) and the various stages of adsorption and wetting is not immediately obvious from the legend alone.
    - **Required correction** Consider adding a schematic or a more explicit description of the different architectures (e.g., "homogeneous," "adsorbed layer," "wetting layer," "three-phase coexistence") in the legend or in a separate schematic panel.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Statistical reporting, Affected element: Figure 5h, Evidence pointer: Figure 5h legend, Methods "Statistics and reproducibility")
    - **Issue** The manuscript states that "Figure 5h shows the results from 6 condensates for 3 different interfaces and 2 interiors." It is unclear how many trajectories or displacements were analyzed per condensate and whether the error bars represent the spread across condensates or within a single condensate.
    - **Required correction** Please clarify the number of trajectories analyzed per condition and the definition of the error bars (e.g., standard error of the mean across condensates, or across displacements). This information is partially in Supplementary Table 1, but it should be stated in the main text or figure legend for clarity.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Discussion of alternative explanations, Affected element: Discussion section)
    - **Issue** The discussion of the dynamical anisotropy focuses on the viscoelastic properties of the interface. However, other factors, such as excluded volume effects, steric hindrance from the densely packed interface, or the presence of the PEG-rich dilute phase, could also contribute to the reduced perpendicular mobility. These alternative explanations are not discussed.
    - **Required correction** A brief discussion of alternative or contributing factors to the observed dynamical anisotropy would strengthen the interpretation and provide a more balanced view.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Terminology, Affected element: Throughout)
    - **Issue** The terms "scaffold" and "adsorbent" are used throughout, but the definition of "adsorbent" in the context of a condensate interface (as opposed to a solid surface) could be more explicitly defined early in the manuscript.
    - **Required correction** Consider providing a clear operational definition of "adsorption" at a liquid-liquid interface in the introduction or results section.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
  - The single-molecule tracking interpretation (R1-M1) needs to be validated with control experiments or a more rigorous analysis to ensure that the measured anisotropy reflects RNA dynamics.
  - The claim that interaction strength is the dominant factor (R1-M2) needs to be supported by a more systematic parameter exploration or tempered.
  - A quantitative or semi-quantitative link between simulation and experiment (R1-M3) is needed to strengthen the claim that simulations reproduce experimental phenomenology.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality:** The work is original in its systematic combination of experiments and simulations to address the specific question of what determines scaffold versus adsorbent behavior and the resulting interfacial anisotropy. The concept of orientational preferences of different components at interfaces is a novel contribution.
  - **Scientific importance:** The findings are important for the field of biomolecular condensates, providing a framework for understanding the organization of multicomponent condensates and for designing synthetic systems with tailored interfacial properties. The dynamical anisotropy observation is a new and potentially significant finding.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership:** The work should appeal to a broad readership, including biophysicists, soft matter physicists, cell biologists, and RNA biologists. The concepts are general and the methods are accessible.
  - **Technical soundness:** The experimental and simulation methods are generally sound, but the interpretation of the single-molecule data (R1-M1) and the generality of the simulation conclusions (R1-M2) require further validation. The lack of a quantitative link between simulation and experiment (R1-M3) is a weakness.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is well-written and the main concepts are explained clearly. The figures are helpful. However, some sections, particularly the simulation methods and the single-molecule analysis, may be challenging for nonspecialists. The abstract is clear and accessible.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core findings are interesting and potentially important, but the interpretation of the single-molecule data and the generality of the simulation predictions need to be addressed before the case is fully established.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the tracked YO-PRO-1 motion directly reports on poly-rA dynamics is not fully supported by the presented evidence.
- The claim that "relative interaction strengths are the most important variable" is not quantitatively supported and may not be generalizable beyond the specific simulation model.
- The claim that the simulations "reproduce the phenomenology observed in experiments" is only qualitatively supported, as no quantitative mapping between simulation and experimental parameters is provided.
- The statement that "the interface may limit movement to the dilute phase and thereby reduce perpendicular motion" is speculative and not directly tested.
- The claim that "interfacial viscoelasticity, which likely originates from interfacial structural anisotropy, also underlies the dynamical anisotropy" is presented as a reasonable expectation but is not directly demonstrated.
