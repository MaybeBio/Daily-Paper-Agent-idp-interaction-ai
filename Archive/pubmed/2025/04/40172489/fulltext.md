# Towards predictive coarse-grained simulations of biomolecular condensates


## Abstract
  Phase separation is a fundamental process enabling cellular organization by forming biomolecular condensates. These assemblies regulate diverse functions by creating distinct environments, influencing reaction kinetics, and facilitating processes such as genome organization, signal transduction, and RNA metabolism. Recent studies highlight the complexity of condensate properties, shaped by intrinsic molecular features and external factors such as temperature and pH. Molecular simulations serve as an effective approach to establishing a comprehensive framework for analyzing these influences, offering high-resolution insights into condensate stability, dynamics, and material properties. This review evaluates recent advancements in biomolecular condensate simulations, with a particular focus on coarse-grained 1-bead-per-amino-acid (1BPA) protein models, and emphasizes OpenABC, a tool designed to simplify and streamline condensate simulations. OpenABC supports the implementation of various coarse-grained force fields, enabling their performance evaluation. Our benchmarking identifies inconsistencies in phase behavior predictions across force fields, even though these models accurately capture single-chain statistics. This finding underscores the need for enhanced force field accuracy, achievable through enriched training datasets, many-body potentials, and advanced optimization techniques. Such refinements could significantly improve the predictive capacity of coarse-grained models, bridging molecular details with emergent condensate behaviors.


## Introduction
  Phase separation is a fundamental cellular process that drives the formation of biomolecular condensates, such as the nucleolus, stress granules, and P-bodies.1–5 The capacity of biomolecules to undergo phase separation and form dynamic, tunable assemblies enables diverse cellular functions by regulating molecular distributions and selective partitioning. This creates distinct environments that influence reaction kinetics and facilitate various cellular processes.1,6 These condensates are implicated in numerous biological functions, including genome organization, signal transduction, and RNA metabolism.7–9 Dysregulation of phase separation is associated with diseases such as neurodegenerative disorders and cancer.2,10 Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms governing phase separation and the ability to modulate this process hold significant potential for advancing disease treatment strategies.

  Over the past decade, experimental studies have revealed the remarkable complexity of biological condensates, particularly in terms of their composition,1 material properties,4,11 and dynamics.12,13 These emergent properties are influenced by both intrinsic molecular features, such as interaction patterns,4,14,15 and external environmental factors, including temperature, pH, and ionic strength.15–17 Developing a theoretical framework that quantitatively links intrinsic and extrinsic factors with these emergent properties is essential for advancing our understanding of condensate formation and regulation. However, this goal is difficult to achieve through experimental studies alone due to their limited resolution in capturing the structural organization of condensates and inefficiency in systematically exploring the key determinants.

  Molecular simulations are valuable complementary tools for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying condensate formation and regulation 18,19. Often described as computational microscopes, they offer high temporal and spatial resolution to characterize systems of interest. These simulations provide a detailed view of the chemical environment within the condensate 12,20,21. With advancements in efficiency, they enable the quantification of condensate stability and the identification of thermodynamic and chemical factors, such as temperature, pH, and ionic strength, that influence this stability.15,22–25 Moreover, the analysis of simulated phase separation kinetics facilitates the study of condensate dynamics and material properties, linking these characteristics to specific molecular interactions for mechanistic insights.

  In this review, we examine recent advancements in molecular simulations of biomolecular condensates, with a particular focus on coarse-grained (CG) protein models. Additionally, we highlight the development of OpenABC,26 a software that significantly lowers the entry barrier for condensate simulations and facilitates the cross-examination of various force fields. Our benchmarking revealed inconsistencies among existing force fields in predicting phase behaviors, underscoring the need for further improvement in CG force field accuracy. We emphasize the importance of incorporating diverse training data from atomistic simulations of both folded and disordered proteins, employing many-body potentials to describe interactions with CG particles, and leveraging the latest optimization algorithms for systematic force field parameterization. These advancements could fully realize the potential of CG models to connect molecular sequences with the emergent behaviors of condensates.


### Characterizing condensate chemical environment with atomistic simulations
  Explicit solvent molecular dynamics simulations with atomistic force fields, commonly referred to as atomistic simulations, provide the most robust approach for characterizing biomolecular structure and dynamics.27–29 These simulations have also proven highly effective in studying biomolecular condensates.

  Atomistic simulations have been employed to investigate chemical interactions within biomolecular condensates. Zheng et al.20 conducted simulations on condensates formed by the low-complexity domain of FUS and the N-terminal disordered domain of LAF-1, identifying a range of residue-residue contacts that contribute to the stability of the condensed phase. Latham et al.30 examined the interior of simulated elastin-like poly-peptide (ELP)33 condensates (Figure 1A), revealing an interfacial environment with an incomplete separation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues. This interfacial environment supports the prevalence of protein-water hydrogen bonds. Furthermore, Rekhi et al.31 demonstrated that phase separation is influenced by a broader array of residue contacts beyond those typically recognized as “stickers” (Figure 1B). Residues previously categorized as “spacers” were shown to provide stabilizing interactions, challenging and extending the traditional sticker-spacer framework.4,14

  In addition, all-atom simulations have been utilized to explore the interactions of small molecules with condensates. For example, Wang et al.21 examined various condensate-ligand systems, identifying different binding poses of small molecules (Figure 1C). Their findings emphasized the existence of heterogeneous, non-specific binding environments within protein condensates. This non-specificity is consistent with reports that the hydrophobicity of small-molecule drugs correlates with their partitioning coefficients into condensates. However, the prevalence of these heterogeneous environments varies among condensates, resulting in distinct preferences for small-molecule partitioning across different condensates.34–36


### Quantifying emergent phase behaviors using coarse-grained models
  Although atomistic simulations have been effectively employed to study biomolecular condensates, most research emphasizes the solvation environment and interactions between proteins and small molecules 20,21,30,31. These simulations encounter substantial challenges in adequately sampling the conformational dynamics of proteins within condensate systems, which is necessary to obtain converged statistics on protein contacts. Consequently, it remains difficult to directly link protein-protein interactions and molecular sequence characteristics with condensate stability, composition, and other emergent properties through atomistic simulations 37–40.

  To enhance simulation efficiency, a range of CG models have been devised, aiming to simplify biomolecular representations. By reducing the system size, these models significantly accelerate simulations. For instance, the Martini force field groups roughly four heavy atoms into a single CG bead,41 while explicitly representing solvent molecules and ions. By tuning parameters for protein-protein interactions, this force field accurately reproduces the excess protein transfer free energy between dense and dilute phases of the low-complexity domain of FUS.42 Additionally, Martini simulations indicate that protein-water interactions significantly influence whether salt addition promotes or inhibits phase separation, highlighting the coupling between distinct interaction patterns in this process.43

  Several CG models remove solvent molecules entirely, incorporating solvent effects implicitly through effective interaction energies. In this review, we focus on CG protein models; however, CG models for nucleic acids have also been developed and summarized in several comprehensive reviews.44–46 A widely employed class of CG protein models used to simulate biomolecular condensates represents each amino acid with a single CG bead. We collectively refer to these models as 1BPA, denoting “1 bead per amino acid”. The bead positions are typically assigned based on the coordinates of α-carbons or the amino acid’s center of mass. Although 1BPA models do not accurately predict secondary structure transitions—requiring explicit backbone atom representation47–57—their computational efficiency makes them suitable for large-scale simulations, particularly condensate simulations.

  Multiple 1BPA models have been developed by different research groups 22,58–68. While these force fields share covalent bonds for chain connectivity, they differ in non-bonded contact interactions, which describe contact energy between amino acids. These variations stem from distinct optimization algorithms used to calibrate interaction strengths, each tailored to capture specific molecular properties.

  The hydrophobicity scale (HPS) models represent one class of 1BPA models 23,58–61,64,66,69. In these models, the interaction between two amino acids, i and j, is determined by the hydropathy scale parameter λij, with larger values denoting stronger attractions (Figure 2). These models assign a per-residue hydropathy parameter λi and apply the mixing rule λij=λi+λj/2, reducing the total of 210 possible λij parameters to 20 independent λi values. The λi parameters can be derived from existing data, such as atomic partial charges23,69 or inverse temperature transition experiments,58,70 or they can be systematically optimized to align with experimental measurements such as average radius of gyration (Rg) values 59–61,63,66.

  Lindoff-Larsen and coworkers significantly extended the HPS model and performed systematic optimiztion based on a Bayesian algorithm,71,72 leading to the development of CALVADOS models. In CALVADOS1 and CALVADOS2, the optimization aimed to reproduce the experimental radius of gyration for a broad set of IDPs.59,60 CALVADOS3 introduced further improvements by incorporating multi-domain proteins, achieving significant enhancements by adjusting the CG bead position in ordered domains from the Cα atom to the center of mass of heavy atoms within each residue.61

  Another 1BPA model called Mpipi has been developed by considering the π-π contact frequencies in PDB structures73 and fitting the potential of mean force between pairs of amino acids, which is computed with all-atom simulations and umbrella sampling.62 This force field emphasizes the importance of π-π and cation-π interactions in protein condensates, with arginines providing stronger attractions than lysines due to the guanidinium group (Figure 2), consistent with diverse experiments.73–75 Recent advancements in Mpipi have led to the development of the Mpipi-GG68 and Mpipi-Recharged67 models. Lotthammer et al.68 fine-tuned the interaction parameters to achieve more accurate Rg predictions for a set of IDPs. Meanwhile, R. Tejedor et al.67 substituted the Debye-Hückel potential with the Yukawa potential for electrostatic interactions, improving the model’s performance for highly charged species by employing parameters derived from atomistic simulations.

  Latham and Zhang63 developed a 1BPA model, MOFF, incorporating a novel optimization algorithm. This algorithm integrates the maximum entropy principle76,77 to calibrate force field parameters against experimental data and draws on energy landscape theory, which necessitates an energy gap between folded and unfolded structures. Notably, the algorithm supports the inclusion of both folded and disordered proteins in the training set, producing balanced interaction parameters among amino acids. Consequently, MOFF is inherently transferable to both folded and disordered proteins—a capability absent in other models but essential for accurately predicting condensate stability. The simultaneous optimization across folded and disordered proteins yields more nuanced interaction patterns among amino acids compared to alternative models (see Figure 2 and Figure S1). Beyond the novel optimization approach, MOFF distinguishes itself from other 1BPA models by incorporating structure-based energy terms derived from reference structures, which enhance the representation of protein backbone conformations.

  The computational efficiency of 1BPA models enables quantification of phase diagrams through slab simulations that investigate the coexistence of both condensed and dilute phases.22,23,25 Such quantification links protein sequence features with condensate emergent properties, allowing the identification of sequence-specific characteristics that influence these properties under perturbative conditions. Simulation studies have identified the stabilizing contributions of nonspecific hydrophobic interactions, as well as hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and π-π and cation-π interactions, in condensate formation.20,31 Amino acid specificity in these models enables differentiation in phase behaviors among homologs,63 post-translational modifications, and various mutants,62 providing valuable insights into the regulation of condensate stability in normal cellular processes and the impact of pathogenic mutations.


### Benchmarking coarse-grained models and streamlining condensate simulations using OpenABC
  The success of 1BPA models in providing mechanistic insights into condensate organization supports their broader adoption by the scientific community. However, selecting an appropriate model remains challenging due to the availability of multiple variants. These models are often implemented in diverse software platforms, such as HOOMD-Blue,78 LAMMPS,79 and GROMACS,80 complicating performance comparisons. Additionally, many current implementations of CG models do not exploit recent advancements in computational hardware, limiting the efficiency and scalability of CG simulations.

  To address these challenges, we developed a CG simulation toolkit, OpenABC26 (Figure 3A), which integrates various protein 1BPA models, including HPS, MOFF, and Mpipi.22,62,63 OpenABC also supports the SMOG and 3SPN protein-DNA models,81–84 which have proven valuable for elucidating chromatin folding mechanisms 85–89.

  The integration of diverse models within a single platform allows for direct benchmarking of their performance by utilizing a consistent numerical integration scheme across different force fields. We evaluated the performance of two HPS models—HPS-Urry and CALVADOS2—alongside MOFF and Mpipi across 18 disordered proteins. Simulation setups followed the OpenABC tutorials available online, with additional details provided in the Supporting Information. As shown in Figure 4A, all four models accurately reproduce the average Rg of most disordered proteins. However, some of these proteins were involved in the training set of certain models, which may introduce bias into the evaluation. MOFF exhibits particularly poor performance on A1 variants, primarily due to its reliance on reference structures generated by ColabFold version AlphaFold290,91 for constructing the structure-based potential (see Supporting Information for details). In contrast, other models do not depend on reference structures when simulating IDPs. The Rg values of the reference structures for A1 variants exceed 4.0 nm, substantially larger than the experimental average (< 3.0 nm). Excluding A1 variants reduces the average rRMSE to 0.159.

  Beyond IDPs, we also simulated a set of folded proteins, with distinct amino acid compositions from IDPs,94 to evaluate whether the models accurately capture the collapse transition characteristic of these structures. To enable a consistent comparison, secondary structure potentials were incorporated into all models rather than being restricted to MOFF, as was done for IDPs (see Supporting Information for details). Importantly, no biases favoring long-range native contacts were introduced. The simulations specifically focused on contacts between secondary structure motifs, with their formation being driven primarily by non-bonded interactions in the various 1BPA models.

  We found that most 1BPA models overestimate Rg (Figure 4B), while MOFF’s results more closely align with values from all-atom simulations.28,92,93 We focused exclusively on the Rg and the overall size of the protein conformations, rather than the accuracy of their tertiary structures. As illustrated in Figure S2, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) relative to the folded structure is large for all four models, although MOFF yields the smallest values. Given the simplicity of these models, it is unreasonable to expect accurate predictions of tertiary conformations.

  Finally, we conducted simulations of multi-domain proteins, which contain one or more folded domains connected by disordered linkers. In these simulations, the folded domains were restrained to maintain their native structures, in contrast to the simulations of single-domain folded proteins (see Supporting Information for details). The primary objective was to examine inter-domain interactions rather than the conformational behavior of individual domains.

  Most models, in their default settings, produced conformations that were more compact than experimental measurements (Figure 4C). Cao et al.61 reported that shifting the CG bead position in ordered domains from the α carbon to the amino acid’s center of mass improved Rg predictions. Consistent with this finding, we observed that moving the CG bead position to the heavy atom center of mass in ordered domains significantly enhanced simulation accuracy (Figure 4D). Repositioning the beads likely reduced interdomain contacts,61 thereby reducing interaction strength.

  OpenABC further facilitates CG condensate simulations through Python scripting and optimizes performance via OpenMM and GPU acceleration.95 Remarkably, single-GPU performance can rival that of parallel processing on hundreds of CPU cores.26 Additionally, OpenABC enables the conversion of Cα representations to all-atom models (Figure 3B), supporting multi-scale computational modeling.

  The computational efficiency provided by OpenABC facilitates a systematic evaluation of protein 1BPA model performance in predicting phase diagrams. While these models demonstrated similar results for single-chain properties of IDPs, they exhibited considerable divergence in condensate simulations. Specifically, the relative stability of condensates formed by the three synthetic IDPs, (GRGDVPYS)25, (GRGDSPYS)25, and (ARADSPYS)25, varied significantly across the models tested (Figure 5), underscoring the challenges in quantitatively predicting phase behavior.96,97 The phase diagram was not calculated using Mpipi, as the OpenABC implementation of the model requires double-precision to maintain simulation stability, which considerably impacts computational efficiency.


### Advancing the accuracy and transferability of coarse-grained models
  The inconsistent results among the different CG models, as presented in the previous section, underscore the need for further model development. We propose that advancing force fields for the next generation of CG models will require incorporating diverse training datasets, many-body interaction potentials, and novel optimization algorithms. These improvements, we argue, will enable CG models to more accurately predict phase diagrams and establish clearer connections between molecular sequences and the emergent behaviors of condensates. In the following sections, we explore each of these three directions in detail.


### Training data from atomistic simulations for both folded and disordered proteins.
  The parameterization of existing models frequently relies on low-resolution experimental data. A common example is the use of average radius of gyration (Rg) derived from small-angle X-ray scattering experiments98 to fine-tune force field parameters 59–61,63,66,99. While such data are valuable, they provide insufficient details to fully characterize the energy landscape of proteins. As we have already shown in Figure 4A, different 1BPA models can all reproduce average Rg values for a broad set of disordered proteins, despite notable differences in their relative interaction strengths (Figure 2).

  Obtaining additional high-resolution experimental data that offer greater structural insights can be challenging, if not impossible, particularly for IDPs, due to the inherent difficulties in resolving their structures. On the other hand, atomistic force fields have made significant strides in accurately characterizing the structures of both folded and disordered proteins.27,28,93 Unlike experimental techniques, simulations with atomistic force fields can generate complete configurational ensembles rather than population averages, offering high-resolution structural information for proteins of interest. These structural ensembles, in principle, could serve as reference data to benchmark existing CG models and provide valuable training sets to further improve their accuracy (Figure 6A).

  Besides improving structural resolution, it is equally important to include both ordered and disordered proteins in the training data.100 Existing models specifically designed for IDPs often assess their performance solely on IDPs. However, when applied to folded proteins, these models frequently fail to collapse the structures, leading instead to extended conformations (see Figure 4B).

  When applied to folded domains, additional biasing potentials that stabilize tertiary structures are often incorporated into 1BPA models to prevent the expansion observed in Figure 4B. This raises the question of whether it is meaningful to assess whether these models predict collapsed or expanded conformations in the absence of tertiary biasing potentials, given that such conditions fall outside their intended application. We argue that improving the accuracy of 1BPA models for folded proteins, particularly in predicting the collapse transition, is crucial for reliably describing condensate stability. The interactions that stabilize the tertiary structures of folded proteins are chemically analogous to those that stabilize protein-protein interfaces.101 Many proteins that form condensates contain both folded and disordered regions,102 and interactions between folded domains are essential for maintaining condensate stability. Therefore, improving the accuracy of force fields for folded proteins could also enhance predictions of protein-protein interactions and condensate stability.

  At first glance, the multi-domain protein results suggest that non-bonded interactions in 1BPA models are generally reasonable and may even be slightly stronger than necessary for describing protein-protein interactions. This is evident from their tendency to predict overly compact conformations without adjustment (Figure 4C). However, this interpretation contrasts with findings from single-domain folded proteins, where most 1BPA models exhibit interactions too weak to drive protein collapse (Figure 4B). One possible explanation is that protein-protein interactions in multi-domain proteins are primarily non-specific and weak, differing in magnitude from the specific interactions that stabilize protein complexes or induce collapse. Further studies are needed to assess the models’ accuracy in predicting interaction strengths across a broader range of protein-protein interfaces.


### Many-body potentials in CG force fields.
  Improving CG force fields requires the incorporation of flexible functional forms for the interaction potentials. Traditionally, the design of CG force fields has been based on the framework of atomistic force fields, which typically assume that non-bonded interactions can be pairwise decomposable. However, many-body interactions often arise as degrees of freedom are reduced.103–105 This occurs because configurational entropy is not factorizable and cannot be accurately captured by pairwise interactions alone. Consequently, accounting for many-body interactions is crucial for improving the accuracy of CG force fields.

  A promising approach to modeling many-body interactions involves the use of neural networks or machine learning force fields 106–123. Neural networks predict the contribution of individual atoms to the total energy based on the spatial and chemical environment surrounding each atom. Since the numerical representation of these environments requires knowledge of the instantaneous positions of all neighboring atoms, neural network potentials naturally capture many-body effects. Furthermore, their high expressiveness enables neural networks to represent complex interactions without explicitly specifying functional forms. Despite their promise, neural network-based force fields incur much higher computational costs for force evaluations109,124 and typically require reduced integration timesteps to address artificially stiff forces, thus limiting their practicality for simulating condensate systems.

  Alternative, local density-dependent multi-body terms have been proposed as transferable force fields in coarse-grained (CG) models.47,125,126 These analytical functional forms offer a compromise between flexibility and computational efficiency. Their application in CG protein force fields has already been demonstrated,47,127 yielding promising outcomes in predicting protein tertiary structures and protein-protein interfaces.101,128 Further refinement of these potentials may result in force fields transferable across both folded and disordered proteins.


### Algorithms for optimizing parameters in CG force fields.
  The parameterization of CG models, which involves a growing number of parameters and flexible functional forms, requires the application of systematic optimization algorithms, particularly when using atomistic simulation data. Several approaches have been developed to address this challenge,65,104,105,129,130 focusing on optimizing CG force field parameters to reproduce the potential of mean force, which governs the conformational distributions observed in atomistic simulations.

  One such approach is force matching,104 which seeks to align the mean force—i.e., the negative gradient of the potential of mean force—from all-atom simulations with the gradient of the CG force fields. This method has been applied to train numerous CG force fields, including those based on neural networks.107,111,131,132 A significant challenge in these methods is the accurate estimation of forces, particularly due to the limited number of samples available from all-atom simulations. To address this, Kohler et al.133 introduced a novel method utilizing a generative model, specifically normalizing flows, to learn the conformational distribution. This model enables the efficient generation of statistically uncorrelated samples, which supports the accurate and convergent estimation of mean forces.

  Alternatively, maximum likelihood approaches, such as relative entropy minimization,129 directly optimize force field parameters to reproduce the conformational distributions obtained from all-atom simulations. These methods aim to align the probability distribution produced by CG force fields with that of the atomistic simulations by minimizing relative entropy or Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence,134 which is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood of the model’s probability distribution on the training data. For instance, Bottaro et al.135 successfully applied the relative entropy method to optimize an implicit solvent model, effectively capturing the structure of disordered peptides. However, these methods tend to be computationally expensive due to the iterative sampling required for force field parameter updates. At each iteration, computational simulations are necessary to generate samples for ensemble averaging, making it difficult to apply these methods to large datasets, such as those involving multiple proteins, where individual simulations must be conducted for each protein.

  The inefficiency of maximum likelihood methods, primarily due to the need for iterative sampling, is well documented within the machine learning community. To address this limitation, various approaches have been proposed to enhance their computational efficiency. These methods have also gained traction in the optimization of force fields.136,137

  For example, Ding and Zhang65 developed the method potential contrasting for CG force field parameterization using noise contrastive learning138,139 (Figure 6C). Rather than maximizing the likelihood of the force field directly on the training data, noise contrastive learning incorporates noise samples and optimizes a loss function designed to distinguish true data from these noise samples. In the case of molecular systems, Ding and Zhang65 demonstrated that advanced sampling techniques could generate effective noise samples, efficiently covering the relevant configuration space. The potential contrasting approach was shown to be highly effective, successfully parameterizing a CG 1BPA model with a neural network potential that reproduced the free energy profile of protein folding, as determined from atomistic simulations.

  This method’s efficiency is particularly noteworthy, as it facilitates the use of large training datasets in the development of transferable models. For instance, Airas et al.109 demonstrated that potential contrasting could be used to learn a transferable implicit solvent model capable of accurately reproducing the folding landscapes of multiple proteins.

  In conclusion, recent advances in atomistic force field accuracy, along with developments in the design and optimization of many-body potentials, provide an ideal foundation for integrating these elements. This integration can support the development of next-generation CG force fields that achieve a more effective balance between accuracy and computational efficiency for simulating biomolecular condensates.
