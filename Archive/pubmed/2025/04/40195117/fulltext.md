# Recent progress and future challenges in structure-based protein-protein interaction prediction


## Abstract
  Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) play a fundamental role in cellular processes, and understanding these interactions is crucial for advances in both basic biological science and biomedical applications. This review presents an overview of recent progress in computational methods for modeling protein complexes and predicting PPIs based on 3D structures, focusing on the transformative role of artificial intelligence-based approaches. We further discuss the expanding biomedical applications of PPI research, including the elucidation of disease mechanisms, drug discovery, and therapeutic design. Despite these advances, significant challenges remain in predicting host-pathogen interactions, interactions between intrinsically disordered regions, and interactions related to immune responses. These challenges are worthwhile for future explorations and represent the frontier of research in this field.


## Introduction
  Proteins, the fundamental building blocks of life, often assemble into complexes to carry out intricate biological functions such as DNA replication, transcription, translation, immune responses, and signal transduction.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 It is estimated that over 80% of human proteins participate in approximately 74,000–650,000 protein-protein interactions (PPIs).8,9 Mapping PPI networks and determining the three-dimensional (3D) structures of protein complexes elucidate the molecular mechanisms of biological processes and provide functional insights for poorly characterized proteins, as their functions can often be inferred from their interacting partners. PPI dysregulation is frequently associated with diseases, such as genetic disorders and cancers.10,11,12,13,14,15,16 Additionally, human pathogens often exploit interactions with host proteins to colonize cells and evade immune responses.17,18 Therefore, studying PPIs is essential for understanding the mechanisms of genetic and infectious diseases and developing effective therapeutic strategies. As a result, this area of research has garnered significant attention in both basic science and translational research efforts.19,20,21,22,23,24

  Most of our PPI knowledge has emerged from experimental studies conducted over the past 40 years. Techniques (Figure 1, right) such as co-immunoprecipitation, pull-down assays, yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), bioluminescence resonance energy transfer, proximity labeling, and affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP-MS) can identify the interacting partners of proteins.25,26,27,28,29,30,31,32,33 Some of these methods, such as Y2H and AP-MS, as well as some recently developed techniques that label each protein with unique barcodes and monitor interactions by next-generation sequencing,34,35 can be performed in a high-throughput manner, enabling the construction of comprehensive PPI networks in various organisms.36,37,38,39,40 High-throughput experiments can generate extensive biological insights by detecting PPIs at scale. Although combining various experimental methods allows for more comprehensive and accurate PPI mapping,41 they are still prone to false positives and false negatives. Many biologically significant interactions are weak and transient, making them difficult to detect in experimental assays.42 Conversely, some proteins can exhibit nonspecific interactions under non-physiological conditions, resulting in a substantial fraction of false positive PPIs.43Figure 1Common strategies for studying PPIsThe asterisks mark methods to detect functional associations, and functionally associated proteins do not necessarily interact directly.

  Another critical focus of PPI research is determining the 3D structures of protein complexes. Historically, X-ray crystallography has been the primary method for obtaining high-resolution structural data. However, recent advances in cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) have significantly improved its resolution, enabling the effective determination of 3D structures for large complexes that are difficult to crystallize.44,45 These breakthroughs have accelerated the acquisition of structural and functional insights into macromolecular complexes at an unprecedented pace.

  Leveraging the vast amount of experimental data, many computational methods have been developed to complement experimental studies in PPI detection and complex structure modeling (Figure 1, left). Broadly, these methods can be categorized into network-based, structure-based, and evolution-based approaches. Network-based methods infer missing PPIs by analyzing the topology of known interaction networks and applying concepts and techniques from network science.46 Structure-based approaches traditionally focus on predicting the 3D structures of protein complexes; with recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), these methods are increasingly capable of predicting interacting partners for a given protein. Evolutionary principles can be integrated with network- and structure-based approaches to leverage experimental data across species. Moving forward, we anticipate that all these strategies must be integrated with experimental data to construct a comprehensive and accurate interactome for model organisms. This review focuses on structure-based PPI prediction methods and encourages readers to consult established studies and reviews on network-based PPI predictions.47,48,49

  Structure-based computational approaches typically rely on both evolutionary information and physical principles. First, the observation that homologous proteins often share similar interacting partners enables the prediction of PPIs through homology-based inference. Second, residues at PPI interfaces often coevolve due to selective pressures to maintain functionally important interactions, and these coevolutionary signals can be used to predict residue-residue contacts between interacting partners. Third, PPIs are governed by thermodynamic principles, allowing bound states to be evaluated based on energetic terms to identify correct conformations or predict binding affinities. Modern deep learning networks provide an ideal framework for integrating these principles.50 The combination of evolutionary and physical insights, the massive accumulation of experimental data for training deep learning models, and the rapid development of deep learning techniques have collectively transformed the accuracy of in silico PPI predictions to a level that is comparable with experimental approaches.51,52

  This review highlights the recent progress in the computational prediction of physical interactions between proteins, focusing on the methods driven by the rapid development of AI. We also discuss the potential biomedical applications of PPI research. Finally, we address the current challenges the field is facing and outline future directions to drive progress in the research community.


## Key developments in computational methods for PPI modeling
  The methods to model protein complexes have stemmed from the field of protein structure prediction because the same physical laws govern the interaction between proteins and within proteins.4,53 Progress in this field has been objectively evaluated by the biannual community-wide experiments Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction (CASP), and Critical Assessment of Predicted Interactions (CAPRI).54,55 The significant progress in PPI modeling (Figure 2) in the last decade can largely be attributed to the explosion of biological data. On the one hand, the accumulation of experimentally determined 3D structures in PDB provides rich templates for modeling new interactions. On the other hand, the surge of genomic data provides deep multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) for many protein families, which offers abundant evolutionary signals that have been proven helpful for protein modeling.Figure 2Timeline of key advances in methodologies for PPI prediction and modeling

  Statistical analysis of MSAs reveals covariation between positions. This covariation frequently reflects coevolution between residues in direct physical contact,56 and coevolution signals from deep MSAs can be utilized for inter-residue contact prediction, both within and between proteins. The early development of such concepts can be traced back to the 1960s, when Yanofsky et al. first discovered the relationship between covarying residues and their relationship in structures.57 Fitch and with Markowitz further explored such phenomena from a mathematical perspective and proposed the concept of “covarion.”58,59 Since the 1990s, researchers have attempted to utilize coevolutionary information for structure prediction.60,61,62,63 During this period, the primary focus was local statistical methods to capture covariation between residues, one pair at a time. Later approaches shifted to global statistical approaches that analyze all pairs of residues to distinguish direct covarying residues from indirect ones due to transitivity. Notable methods include GREMLIN, mfDCA, EVfold, PSICOV, CCMpred, and plmDCA.64,65,66,67,68,69 Coevolving residue pairs detected by such methods frequently form contacts in the 3D structures,56 allowing the modeling of both single proteins and interacting protein pairs.

  Meanwhile, substantial progress has been made in modeling PPIs using physics-based methods, commonly known as protein-protein docking (PPD). PPD relies on extensive sampling of different conformations and evaluating these docked poses by scoring functions. One particularly successful sampling strategy is fast Fourier transform (FFT) docking.70,71 By expressing the scoring function as a correlation function, FFT docking uses the Fourier transform to sample possible interaction poses between two proteins within the frequency space, enabling efficient conformation sampling.72 Scoring functions used for PPD can typically be categorized as physics-based potentials (derived from fundamental physical laws73) and statistical potentials (empirically derived from statistical analysis of known molecular structures and experimental data74,75). Examples of widely used PPD tools include ZDOCK,76,77 HADDOCK,78,79 RosettaDock,80,81,82 PIPER,83 ClusPro (a server built on top of PIPER),84,85 GRAMM,86 and HDOCK.87,88 These methods typically integrate both statistical potential and physics-based potential. Some programs, for example, ClusPro, will conduct additional energy minimization to remove steric clashes and select the best docking conformation.

  One challenge for PPD is the difference between unbounded and bounded states of the interacting proteins. When two proteins interact, the induced fit could result in considerable changes in their conformation, especially at the PPI interfaces, from unbounded state to bounded state.89 Docking methods could tackle this problem either implicitly by sampling many monomer decoys and docking these decoys or explicitly by allowing free movement of backbone and side chains while sampling the relative orientations of two proteins.90

  In recent years, we have witnessed the rapid development of AI. A notable breakthrough in applying deep learning to protein structure prediction can be attributed to the work of Wang et al., who utilized an ultra-deep convolutional neural network (CNN) to predict protein contact map,91 which led to their top performance in contact prediction at CASP12, outperforming traditional coevolution-based methods.92 Zeng et al. applied this concept to predict contacts between putative interacting proteins.93 DeepMind subsequently trained a larger and deeper AI model to predict contacts, with a CNN as its major component. Their method, AlphaFold (AF), emerged as the top-performing group in CASP13.94 There were refinements of CNN-based contact prediction methods after CASP1395; however, the next breakthrough only happened in CASP14, where DeepMind introduced AlphaFold2 (AF2), with an architecture vastly different from AF. AF2 outperformed other groups by a remarkable margin and achieved an atomic-level accuracy.50

  Instead of relying on existing neural network architectures, AF2 uses Evoformer, which is tailored to extract coevolution information from MSAs. AF2 also includes a structure module. By treating each residue’s N, C-alpha, and C atoms as a “frame” that can move freely relative to other frames, the structure module is a simplified yet effective way to represent amino acids’ spatial relationship, allowing the network to be trained in an end-to-end fashion. In addition, AF2 successfully integrates physical constraints of protein structure into its network design, for example, through triangular attentions used in Evoformer.

  AF2 provided researchers with a neural network that can encapsulate knowledge about protein folding and an unprecedented enrichment of high-quality monomer structures.96,97 Various groups have immediately attempted to utilize AF2 for protein complex structure prediction by directly modifying AF2 architecture or by integrating AF2 prediction with docking methods.98,99,100,101,102,103,104,105,106 These successes demonstrated the feasibility of using AF2-like networks to predict protein complex structures. DeepMind subsequently developed AlphaFold-Multimer (AFM), a model tailored for predicting protein complex structures.107 Apart from methods based on AF2, a wide range of other network architectures, such as protein language models and graph neural networks, have been used for PPI prediction.108,109,110,111,112 However, AF2-based methods remain the best based on results from CASP15.113,114

  Encouraged by these successes, researchers started to build more advanced neural networks that can handle other molecules, such as nucleotides115 and small molecules,116 in addition to proteins. RoseTTAFold All-Atom is among the initial works to generalize structure prediction to various molecule types.117 Subsequently, DeepMind released AlphaFold3 (AF3), which is capable of modeling molecules beyond proteins. AF3 replaces the structure module in AF2 with a diffusion-based model, which introduces flexibility in structure generation. In addition, AF3 only processes MSA representations in the first few blocks, which reduces the computational cost and the method’s reliance on deep MSA. AF3 generalizes to a broader range of molecule types. It achieves significant performance improvement in challenging tasks, such as antibody-antigen structure prediction, establishing itself as state-of-the-art.118


## From PPI modeling to proteome-wide PPI screening
  A closely related task to protein complex structure modeling is determining which pair of proteins should interact in physiological conditions. In bacteria, being encoded in the same operon is probably the most reliable predictor of interactions.119 In eukaryotes, however, predicting the interacting partners of a protein is challenging. While modern AI models help predict the 3D structures of known PPIs, they can be misleading to experimental biologists by predicting a decent 3D model of a protein complex for pairs that do not interact in biological conditions.120 Thus, there is an urgent need to distinguish true interactions from false ones.

  Screening for interacting protein pairs from non-interacting ones across the entire proteome has long been a formidable challenge for computational methods. For example, in Escherichia coli, it is estimated that there are approximately 10,000 interacting protein pairs, while the total possible pairs among 4,450 proteins reach 10 million.121 This results in a signal-to-noise ratio at a scale of 1:1,000 and makes proteome-wide PPI screening a challenging task. Nevertheless, the accumulation of protein sequences and structure data and the development of computational methods made structural biology at the proteome scale possible.122

  The PrePPI method and the associated database are among the initial efforts to screen the human proteome for PPIs in silico. PrePPI is conceptually a template-based PPI screen method in which protein pairs are searched against the PDB for structural template and evidence of interaction (Figure 3, left). PrePPI includes a naive Bayes predictor to score the predicted PPIs, and selected predictions have been validated by wet-lab experiments.123,124,125Figure 3Two established strategies to conduct proteome-wide PPI screening

  In parallel with template-based methods, researchers have long aimed to predict PPIs from sequence information.126,127,128 Grounded in the assumption that interacting proteins exhibit stronger coevolutionary signals, Cong et al. performed de novo PPI screening in E. coli by concatenating MSAs of orthologs from different species and applying global statistical approaches to the paired MSAs to evaluate the coevolution between residues in different proteins and differentiate interacting pairs from non-interacting ones (Figure 3 right).129 Green et al. adopted a similar coevolution-based approach to screen PPIs in E. coli, with advances in that they provide a confidence estimation for each prediction.130

  Deep learning methods provide a powerful framework for integrating multiple sources of evidence to enhance PPI prediction performance. Humphreys et al. combined coevolutionary analysis and deep learning networks (e.g., RoseTTAFold, AF2) to identify interacting protein pairs in yeast131 and 19 human pathogens.132 These deep learning methods can also identify a subset of more confident PPIs from large-scale experimental data plagued by false positives. For example, Burke et al. used AF2 to predict the complex structures for 65,484 human PPIs revealed from experimental screens, demonstrating that higher-confidence predictions are enriched within those supported by multiple experimental methods.133 To tackle the challenge of de novo PPI screening in humans, Zhang et al. developed RoseTTAFold2-PPI by optimizing the network architecture and augmenting the training dataset with domain-domain interactions extracted from massive AlphaFold models. Combined with richer coevolutionary signals harvested from deeper MSAs derived directly from unprocessed genomic data, they significantly improved the accuracy of proteome-wide PPI prediction. They subsequently applied their methods to screen PPIs from the entire human proteome (200 million pairs), nearly tripling the number of human binary protein complexes with highly accurate 3D structural data.134

  As in silico PPI prediction gains increasing attention from the broader scientific community, researchers have developed various tools to streamline the setup and evaluation of PPI screens. For example, Yu et al. created AlphaPulldown, a Python package that provides users with an automated pipeline for PPI screening given a set of protein sequences of interest.135 Building on AlphaPulldown, Bellinzona et al. reduced the number of models sampled without compromising the accuracy.136 Mischley et al. trained PPIscreenML, a machine-learning network that can distinguish true interacting protein pairs from non-interacting pairs in AF2 models.137 Kim et al. developed a new evaluation metric to improve the sensitivity of the PPI screen based on AFM.138 Using AF2Complex,101 Gao et al. screened for interacting proteins of the kinase Lck, a critical factor in T cell modulation.139

  Finally, various methods have combined template-based and de novo predictions using evolutionary information. Bell et al. developed PEPPI, a pipeline that integrated structural similarity, sequence similarity, functional association, and a naive Bayesian classifier to predict PPIs at a proteomic scale.140 Zhao et al. recently developed ZEPPI, which combined the strengths of both evolution-based and template-based methods, making it possible to predict PPIs with very shallow MSAs.141


## Biomedical applications of PPI prediction
  PPI studies can help address fundamental scientific questions and offer solutions to various biomedical challenges. Below, we highlight key areas where PPI research can provide valuable insights (Figure 4, top).Figure 4Biomedical applications of PPI studies and future challenges


### Novel biological discoveries
  A direct application of AF2 assists in resolving protein complex structures that were previously intractable. For instance, several research groups have utilized AF2 to support the experimental structure determination of nuclear pore complex proteins—a challenging task often regarded as the holy grail of structural biology.142,143,144 In another example, Meleppattu et al. used AF2 to construct atomic models for a subunit of IFT-A, a protein complex in the intraflagellar transport (IFT) system that functions in the assembly and maintenance of cilia and flagella in eukaryotic cells. The predicted model was further docked into a cryo-EM map for refinement and complex assembly.145 Williams et al. used AF2 to predict the binding interfaces between Ufd1/Npl4 and Cdc48, which assembles into a complex involved in extracting polyubiquitinated substrates and further delivering them for degradation. AF2 helped them identify key residues for substrate recognition and complex formation.146

  In addition, by identifying interacting partners of proteins, AF2 helps uncover new components of biological complexes and provides functional insights into poorly characterized proteins. For example, Lim et al. used AFM to identify interacting partners of DONSON, a key player in DNA replication. They screened DONSON against ∼70 core DNA replication factors in several model organisms. AFM predicted high-confidence interactions between DONSON and several proteins, including SLD5 (a GINS subunit), TOPBP1, POLE2 (a Pol ε subunit), and MCM3 (an MCM2-7 subunit), all of which were validated experimentally to be crucial for CMG helicase assembly.147 Yu et al. discovered that Mrc1/CLASPIN, a replication fork protection complex component, might interact with H3-H4 tetramer based on AFM predictions. Through experimental validation, they demonstrated that Mrc1/CLASPIN acts as a histone chaperone to facilitate the transfer of parental H3-H4 tetramers during DNA replication, ensuring epigenetic inheritance of heterochromatin.148 In a recent work by Deneke et al., the authors employed AFM to identify Tmem81 as a candidate binding partner for the essential fertilization factors Izumo1 and Spaca6, leading to the discovery of a trimeric complex consisting of Izumo1, Spaca6, and Tmem81, that plays a crucial role in sperm-egg interactions during fertilization.149 Similarly, numerous other studies150,151,152,153,154,155,156 have utilized AF2 or AFM to screen for potential interacting partners and gain functional insights, highlighting their growing role as essential tools for interaction discovery and functional characterization.


### Understanding PPI-related diseases
  Many diseases are related to the dysregulation of PPIs.157,158 Identifying interacting partners of proteins and modeling their 3D structures could improve our understanding of diseases. Belizaire et al. investigated how mutations in CBL could enhance its interactions with other proteins, such as LYN (an Src family kinase) and PIK3R1 (a regulatory subunit of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase [PI3K]), driving downstream PI3K/AKT signaling and promoting chronic myelomonocytic leukemia.159 Mizuguchi et al. have shown that the mutations of PQBP1 will prevent its interaction with the spliceosomal protein U5-15k, which causes pre-mRNA splicing defects and results in X-linked mental retardation disorders.160 Homma et al. used AFM to screen 1,879 small, secreted proteins from 7 different tomato pathogens against 6 defense-related hydrolases from tomatoes, identifying 376 high-confidence protein complexes based on the interface and template modeling scores. Manual inspection of the models identified 15 putative inhibitors, 4 of which were validated experimentally.161

  Computational biologists have developed many valuable tools for the biomedical community to understand how genetic variations may interfere with PPIs and cause diseases. For example, Jankauskaité et al. curated SKEMPI, a benchmark of changes in protein-protein binding energy, kinetics, and thermodynamics upon mutation.162 Cheng et al. provided evidence for widespread perturbations of PPIs in human diseases caused by both germline and somatic hypermutations identified in large-scale sequencing studies, suggesting the crucial role of PPIs in human diseases.163 Zhang et al. utilized AF2 to identify and model approximately 1,800 protein complex structures involving cancer driver proteins, offering valuable insights into numerous cancer-related processes.164 Xiong et al. developed PIONEER to predict binding partner-specific interfaces for experimentally detected PPIs in humans and seven other model organisms. They also demonstrated that disease-associated mutations are enriched in PIONEER-predicted PPI interfaces, which is worth further investigation.165


### Drug discovery and protein binder design
  PPIs are pivotal to virtually all cellular processes, making their modulation a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Unlike traditional drug targets such as enzymes or receptors, PPIs often feature large, flat, and dynamic interfaces, which have historically been considered “undruggable.” However, progress in screening technologies, computational modeling, and chemical biology have transformed the landscape of PPI modulator discovery, enabling the development of inhibitors and stabilizers. Vassilev et al. designed small molecules targeting the MDM2-p53 complex, which can lead to the activation of p53 and inhibition of tumor growth.166 A PPI modulator, lenalidomide, was developed to treat multiple myeloma. It functions by binding to an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and modulating its substrate specificity, resulting in the proteasomal degradation of IKZF1 and IKZF3, two zinc-finger transcription factors that would generally be considered undruggable.167 Computational modeling and the advent of near-atomic accuracy predictions of PPI structures have accelerated the discovery of critical and druggable interfaces and facilitated the rational design of small-molecule PPI modulators.168,169,170,171 We recommend that readers read more relevant reviews about PPI modulator discovery.172,173,174

  In addition to modulating existing PPIs, designing new binding partners for proteins to regulate their functions presents another promising avenue for therapeutics. Peptides have recently emerged as a potent class of drugs, showing advantages such as high specificity and low cytotoxicity.175 Therefore, accurate modeling of protein-peptide interaction has shown significant potential in drug discovery.176 Traditionally, protein-peptide docking algorithms have been employed to model these interactions.177,178,179 More recently, deep learning tools,180,181,182,183 including some models based on AF2,184,185,186 have been increasingly used to predict protein-peptide interactions. Improvement in these methods could drive rapid development in this field.

  In addition to peptides, designed protein binders represent another promising area for future drug development. For example, Cao et al. designed miniprotein inhibitors targeting the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, preventing it from binding its ACE2 receptor.187 The revolution in AI-based protein modeling has significantly enhanced our ability to engineer endogenous binders or design new ones for specific proteins. AF2 has been proven helpful in selecting true binders for a target out of a large pool of designed candidates. Bennett et al. used iPAE from AF2 outputs to screen for potential binders within the design sequences,188 significantly reducing the number of designs one needs to test experimentally to obtain a true binder.

  Another key challenge in protein binder design is efficiently navigating the vast structure and sequence space to identify the optimal scaffolds and sequences for targeting a specific protein. Traditional methods for binder design rely on extensive sampling coupled with energy-based computations to select favorable designs189; this process requires rich experience and is not accessible to the broader scientific community. Advances in generative AI methods have made protein design a more automatic and efficient process, making it possible for non-experts to use it as a tool in their research. Tools like RFdiffusion and Chroma rely on the diffusion model, a popular approach in the field of image generation, to generate protein backbones independently or in complex with their target binding partners.190,191 Methods like ProteinMPNN further generate protein sequences for these designed backbones.192 The combination of such generative AI models and in silico screening by AF2 has been widely used in a series of projects on protein binder design.193,194,195,196,197,198,199


## Challenges in PPI prediction and future perspectives
  Despite significant success in predicting protein complex structures and identifying interacting protein pairs across the proteome, many challenges remain unsolved. Here, we outline several key directions for the research community to prioritize in future endeavors (Figure 4, bottom).


### Interactions mediated by intrinsically disordered regions
  Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) refer to segments of protein sequences that do not fold into a definite 3D structure. It is estimated that 30%–40% of residues in the eukaryotic proteome are disordered, and IDRs are also prominently represented in the AlphaFold protein structure Database.200,201,202 Despite their lack of a stable 3D structure, IDRs can perform critical functions, and they are receiving increasing attention from the scientific community. On the one hand, IDRs, especially the short linear motifs within them, can interact with other regions of the same protein or with a different protein, thereby regulating their function.203,204 On the other hand, interactions between IDRs can drive liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a process related to immune response, gene transcription, cell death, and human diseases.205,206,207,208,209 However, the high structural diversity and low sequence conservation of IDRs present a grand challenge for their experimental and computational characterization.210

  Nevertheless, leveraging the growing body of experimental data, researchers have begun using computational methods to understand how IDRs drive LLPS. Saar et al. developed DeePhase, an integrated model that distinguished LLPS-prone sequences from structured and intrinsically disordered proteins with a lower LLPS propensity by combining knowledge-based features with unsupervised embeddings.211 Chen et al. developed two machine learning models that predict whether a disordered region will undergo LLPS independently or with other partners.212 Rekhi et al. took a molecular simulation approach instead.213 They concluded that LLPS of IDRs is driven by a wide array of residue-residue interactions, not just a few key “sticker” residues like arginine (Arg) and tyrosine (Tyr). Hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and π-cation interactions have been shown to collectively contribute to LLPS,213 underscoring the complexity of interactions between IDRs. More recently, Omidi et al. explored the potential of AFM in predicting IDR-mediated interactions.214 They found that when an IDR adopts a fixed and homogeneous structure upon binding to its partners, often ordered domains, AFM effectively predicts its bound structure and distinguishes true interacting partners from decoys. However, they also highlight that AFM struggles to capture the dynamic and fuzzy interactions between IDRs, as shown by higher distance violations compared to NMR data.214 Despite growing efforts to study IDRs, the field remains in its early stages, with many challenges yet to be addressed.


### Interactions between host and pathogen proteins
  Studying host-pathogen PPIs is essential for developing therapeutic strategies to combat infectious diseases. Compared to PPIs within an organism, host-pathogen PPIs are challenging due to the limited availability of evolutionary information. Pathogen proteins frequently evolve rapidly as part of an “arms race” with their host, leading to significant sequence divergence that undermines the accuracy of homology-based PPI predictions via sequence similarities.215,216 The relatively short evolutionary history for host-pathogen PPIs also limits coevolution between residues on such PPI interfaces.217,218 However, pathogen proteins may bind to their host target proteins by mimicking the endogenous interacting partners of the host proteins.219,220 This mimicry suggests that despite the lack of sequence homology, known protein complexes from the host may still serve as templates for predicting host-pathogen interactions. Such templates can be found by structural similarities, and whether a pathogen protein can interact with the host target can be evaluated based on energy functions to estimate the binding affinities. For example, P-HIPSTer used this approach to predict and model 282,000 virus-human interactions for approximately 1,000 human-infecting viruses.221 Additionally, protein language models that do not explicitly utilize evolutionary information from MSAs also show promise with these challenges.222,223,224,225 In summary, due to the shortage of evolutionary evidence, predicting host-pathogen PPIs remains challenging. It requires further improvement in the physics-based approaches and AI models that are better at integrating fundamental physicochemical principles.


### Interactions related to human immune responses
  Two crucial types of PPIs in human immune responses, antibody-antigen interactions and T cell receptor-peptide-major histocompatibility complex (TCR-pMHC) interactions, exemplify another scenario with limited evolutionary information. Antibodies bind antigens with their complementary determining regions (CDRs), which display enormous sequence diversity due to the nearly random V(D)J rearrangement and somatic hypermutations.226 The vast sequence diversity and flexibility of CDRs restrict the use of evolutionary information and make it particularly challenging to predict CDR structures and interactions with antigens.227,228,229 Ruffolo et al. described one of the first applications of an AF2-like model to predict antibody structures.230 By analyzing the performance of antibody-antigen targets from CASP16, we discovered that the aforementioned ClusPro server consistently outperforms AF3 in such targets. ClusPro is primarily a PPD approach. It extensively samples different conformations, evaluates them using a combination of statistical and physics-based potentials, clusters favorable conformations, and selects final predictions from the largest clusters.85 Whether the superior performance of ClusPro over AF3 results from massive sampling or demonstrates the power of physics-based approaches remains to be explored.231

  TCR-pMHC interactions pose a greater challenge than antibody-antigen interactions. Similar to antibodies, TCRs bind their target with their CDRs. In addition, the peptide presented by MHC typically consists of 10–20 amino acids, which exhibit high structure flexibility,232 and is not supposed to coevolve with either MHC or TCR. Therefore, modeling the TCR-pMHC interaction is challenging for physics- and evolution-based approaches. To address these challenges, researchers have focused on leveraging the growing volume of experimental data on TCR-pMHC associations obtained from immune profiling.233 Machine learning models have been trained on these data to predict TCR-pMHC interactions directly from sequence information. For example, Lu et al. presented pMTnet, an initial attempt to predict TCR-pMHC binding in a sequence-based fashion.234 Peng et al. adopted a similar approach and was able to predict pairwise residue distances and contacts with a sequence-based machine learning model.235 These methods show promising performance in distinguishing true TCR-pMHC pairs from random pairs at a 1:10 ratio. However, they are likely not accurate enough to predict the true interacting partners of a TCR or a pMHC from billions of pMHC or TCR sequences generated by our immune systems.236 Incorporating AF2/3-like methods to incorporate structural features of TCR-pMHC interactions may be a way to improve the performance.237 For example, Yin et al. developed TCRmodel2, enabling TCR-pMHC complexes modeling from sequences with adaptations from AF2.238 Wu et al. developed tFold-TCR, which leverages a tailored language model to achieve fast and accurate structure modeling of TCR-pMHC complexes.239

  The significance of the human immune response in biomedical applications, combined with the suboptimal performance of existing methods, highlights antibody-antigen and TCR-pMHC interaction prediction as critical challenges for the future. These interactions continue to be a focus of active investigation within the scientific community. For example, in CASP16, several participating groups have developed models tailored for antibody-antigen targets.240 Combining the large amount of data regarding antibodies and TCRs generated in experimental and clinical labs and the ever-improving AI methods to integrate these data with fundamental rules regarding PPIs, we expect a significant improvement in this area in the coming decade.


### Quality evaluation of predicted structures
  To account for the dynamic nature of proteins and the conformational changes upon their interaction, it is a common practice for protein modeling methods to generate multiple predicted structures. Since the release of AF2, multiple groups have worked on adapting such networks to generate more diverse conformations. Methods to diversify the predicted structures include subsampling the input MSAs,241 introducing dropouts in the AF2 network during inference (AFsample by Wallner et al.),242,243 and using diffusion-based methods for structure generation (AF3).118 To facilitate the generation of many predictions, MassiveFold244 was introduced to parallelize structure prediction and drastically improve the sampling speed. It is shown in CASP15 that sampling more models results in better predictions.245 Raouraoua et al. also demonstrated the advantages of sampling for antibody-antigen targets in CAPRI.246

  Our experience in assessing CASP16 suggests that the large number of predicted complex structures generated by MassiveFold using the aforementioned diversification techniques nearly always contains the correct structures, but the challenge lies in selecting the correct ones. For difficult targets without substantial coevolutionary information, only a few groups can predict the correct structure, and none of them performed consistently well on all targets.231 The ability to evaluate the quality of predicted structures and select the best model is, however, critical for biologists to use predicted structures for biological insights. Current protein structure prediction methods typically output five different models,50,118,247,248 which comes from the CASP tradition where predictors can submit five models. With five (or more) structures generated, knowing which model is more reliable is essential for the experimental community.228,249,250 Our analysis of CASP16 results suggests that physics-based methods are promising in ranking predictions and selecting the best models for challenging targets, such as antibody-antigen pairs, without coevolutionary information.231 Therefore, incorporating more physics-based ideas in AI models can be a promising future direction.


### Predicting the effect of mutations at PPI interfaces
  Residues located at PPI interfaces contribute differently to the binding affinity. While a few hotspot residues contribute significantly to the binding free energy, mutations to other residues at PPI interfaces do not interfere with the binding between two proteins.251 Predicting the effect of mutations at PPI interfaces is important for designing experiments to perturb PPIs and for interpreting human genetic variations. AF2 relies on evolutionary data from MSAs to make accurate structure predictions. Through Evoformer, AF2 can modify the input MSAs to improve its compatibility with the predicted pairwise contacts and 3D structure. As a result, AF2 is not sensitive to mutations in query sequences. Buel and Walters provided an example to show AF2’s incapability of predicting the structural effect of mutations.252 Pak et al. suggested that the confidence metric, pLDDT, of AF2 showed nearly no correlation with a protein’s stability.253 In the AF_unmasked pipeline by Mirabello et al., although their original purpose was to increase the importance of templates by reducing the MSA depth used by AF2, they discovered that such modifications could help to predict the structural effect of point mutations, suggesting a potential approach to increase the sensitivity of AF-like methods to point mutations.106

  While AF2 and its derivatives struggle to predict mutation effects, other methods dedicated to this task show better promise. Huang et al. leveraged evolutionary profile and physics-based energy calculation to predict the affinity change upon mutations.254 Rodrigues et al. developed mCSM-PPI2, which uses graph neural networks to predict the effect of mutations on PPIs,255 whereas Liu et al. adopted a geometric learning-based method for this task.256 One challenge in predicting the effect of mutations is the lack of large, high-quality experimental datasets. Commonly used databases to derive training datasets or to evaluate model performances for interface mutation effect predictors include ASEdb,257 SKEMPI,162,258 and Ab-Bind,259 but they typically only contain several thousand data points. Developing high-throughput experimental techniques to measure the effects of mutations on PPI260 can help accumulate training data for better predictors, but the accuracy of such large-scale experimental data remains to be assessed. Overall, predicting the impact of mutations on PPIs remains a challenging task. Looking ahead, we expect the development of more advanced computational methods supported by emerging experimental techniques that produce high-quality data to train more accurate and reliable models.
