# Prediction of Small-Molecule
Partitioning into Biomolecular
Condensates from Simulation


## Abstract
  Predicting small-molecule partitioning into biomolecular condensates is the key to developing drugs that selectively target aberrant condensates. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying small-molecule partitioning remain largely unknown. Here, we first exploit atomistic molecular dynamics simulations of model condensates to elucidate the physicochemical rules governing small-molecule partitioning. We find that while hydrophobicity is a key factor in determining partitioning into condensates enriched in hydrophobic residues, partitioning into more polar condensates is driven by specific interactions that can offset the associated entropic cost of localization. The observed selectivity of condensates toward certain compounds suggests that condensate-specific therapeutics can be engineered. Building on these insights, we develop minimal models (MAPPS) for the efficient prediction of small-molecule partitioning into biologically relevant condensates. We demonstrate that this approach reproduces atomistic partition coefficients in both model systems and condensates composed of the low-complexity domain (LCD) of FUS. Applying MAPPS to various LCD-based condensates shows that the protein sequence can exert a selective pressure, thereby influencing small-molecule partitioning. Collectively, our findings reveal that partitioning is driven by both small molecule–protein affinity and the complex interplay between the physicochemical properties of the compounds and the condensate environment.


## Introduction
  Biomolecular condensates are membraneless intracellular compartments composed of proteins and nucleic acids and encompass structures such as the nucleolus, stress granules, P-bodies, and centrosomes. −     Many condensates are formed and maintained through phase separation, appearing as dynamic, liquid-like assemblies with distinct chemical environments. −      Such organization facilitates efficient biochemical reactions and regulatory processes. For example, condensates play essential roles in RNA metabolism, signal transduction, and cellular stress responses. ,  It is therefore unsurprising that the dysregulation of biomolecular condensates has been linked to several human diseases. In neurodegenerative disorders, pathological phase separation can lead to the formation of insoluble aggregates. For example, TDP-43 mislocalization and aggregation are hallmarks of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and tau protein aggregates are central to Alzheimer’s disease. ,  In cancer, altered phase separation can drive oncogenesis by enhancing signaling pathways, modulating gene expression, or creating environments favorable to cancer progression. Additionally, many viruses exploit cellular condensates to enhance their replication or shield themselves from immune responses, as seen in certain stress granules or nucleolar condensates. ,  Given these adverse effects, condensates represent promising targets for drug development.

  Therapeutic strategies targeting biomolecular condensates are an emerging area of drug discovery, as small molecules can prevent the formation of pathological condensates by disrupting the weak multivalent interactions driving intracellular phase separation. −    The current predominant approach in the field involves a phenotype-based strategy, in which extensive high-throughput screens of small organic molecules are carried out experimentally. ,  Using such screens, certain small molecules have been identified that partition into condensates and modulate their material properties. −     Although potential drug candidates have been identified, the phenotype-based strategies used are inherently agnostic to the mode of action of the drugs. Thus, at present, drug discovery for condensates possesses an inherent blind spot that significantly hampers progress in the field: the molecular mechanisms underlying the preferential partitioning of small molecules into condensates and their consequent effects on condensates remain largely unknown.

  The challenge of targeting condensates is further exacerbated by their inherently dynamic nature. Many proteins involved in condensate formation lack well-defined structures, making them unsuitable for traditional structure-based drug design. −   Furthermore, variability in condensate chemical environments, ,  i.e., distinct from one condensate to another and determined by their unique compositions, poses an additional challenge in drug discovery. For instance, identifying molecules that target one type of condensate without affecting others requires a precise understanding of the unique interactions within them.

  Recent experimental efforts combined with machine-learning-based computational approaches have aimed to uncover the physicochemical factors that govern small-molecule partitioning into condensates. Thody et al. identified key features of small molecules, such as solubility and hydrophobicity, that favor or disfavor condensate partitioning, which varied over nearly 6 orders of magnitude. They concluded that the enrichment is primarily driven by the formation of hydrophobic environments within condensates and emphasized the hydrophobicity of a compound as the dominant driver of its partitioning into condensates.

  Kilgore et al. probed selective small-molecule partitioning into condensates formed by MED1, NPM1, and HP1a, revealing distinct partitioning patterns across these environments. Using machine learning, they further analyzed how molecular properties, such as size, hydrophobicity, flexibility, and number of hydrogen bonds, influence partitioning. Their results suggest that the chemical environments of condensates selectively concentrate small molecules based on a complex interplay of physicochemical properties rather than a single dominant feature, including interactions with a specific condensate environment. Additionally, Li et al. showed that heterogeneities within the microenvironment of condensates significantly affect the potency of small molecules. Their findings suggest that condensates provide a unique chemical environment, challenging the idea that the physicochemical properties of a compound alone dictate partitioning.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations offer a compelling approach to understanding the complex relationships between the properties of small molecules and their selectivity for particular condensates. Crucially, MD simulations enable the capture of the fully disordered nature of condensates while dynamically probing their interactions with small molecules. A recent study by Wang et al. utilized all-atom MD simulations to explore how nonspecific hydrophobic interactions within MED1, HP1α, and NPM1 condensates influence the partitioning of small molecules. Their simulations used systems representative of condensates and revealed that the unique compositions and physicochemical properties of the condensates favor certain small molecules over others. While such studies offer valuable insights into the intricate mechanisms of molecular interactions, modeling a phase boundary of a condensate could provide a broader picture of partitioning mechanisms, which is computationally expensive for large systems at all-atom resolution. In contrast, coarse-grained (CG) models simplify the system representation by grouping atoms into interaction sites or “beads”. This reduction in complexity and computational expense can facilitate the screening of a broader range of small molecules.

  Here, we employ atomistic and CG molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the factors influencing small-molecule partitioning into condensates. At atomistic resolution, we design four model condensates with varying physicochemical properties, specifically differing in their degrees of polarity. These model condensates, composed of peptides, have previously been shown to capture the phase separation behavior of biomolecular condensates. To model hydrophobic environments, we select aromatic residues (tyrosine, Y; phenylalanine, F; and tryptophan, W) and incorporate alanine (A) to lower the density of the condensed phase, thereby enhancing the molecular mobility. To represent polar environments, we substitute hydrophobic residues with polar (glutamine, Q; asparagine, N) and charged (histidine, H) residues. Overall, the model condensates capture a range of distinct chemical environments. Additionally, the selection of model condensates helps balance chemical diversity with the computational demands of all-atom simulations, which constrain the number of different sequences that can be probed. Using these model systems, we examine the partitioning behavior of 17 organic small molecules spanning a range of physicochemical characteristics, including hydrophobicity, solubility, flexibility, dipole moments, and size (Figure  a). These ∼65 systems enable a detailed analysis of the interplay between small molecule properties and the chemical environment in determining localization to condensates (Figure  b). Building on insights from the atomistic simulations, we develop a set of minimal CG models to further elucidate the key properties driving small-molecule partitioning. This approach, dubbed MAPPS for MinimAl models for Prediction of small-molecule Partitioning into biomolecular condensateS, enables the efficient exploration of interactions between small molecules and condensates. Together, our work uncovers key features and empirical relationships that describe interactions between small molecules and condensates: (1) the extent of a compound’s partitioning is driven by the unique characteristics of the condensate environment and is shaped by the physicochemical properties of the compound; (2) greater small-molecule hydrophobicity leads to higher partitioning, and compounds demonstrate preference for condensates enriched in hydrophobic residues; and (3) while condensates enriched in polar residues generally exhibit low partitioning, certain compoundssuch as those with high dipole momentscan engage in specific interactions that enhance localization to condensates, effectively overriding entropic costs.


## Results and Discussion

### Partitioning Is Influenced by the Physicochemical
Properties of Both Small Molecules and Condensates
  To gain atomistic insight into the partitioning of small molecules in various condensates, we simulate ∼65 systems composed of small molecules and model peptide-based condensates: FWAFWF, FYAFYF, YQHQHY, and NYANYN (the letter codes for the amino acids are given in Table S1). We quantify the hydrophilicity of each peptide using the Hopp–Woods scale, with total scores for each sequence as follows: FWAFWF (−14.8), FYAFYF (−12.6), YQHQHY (−5.2), and NYANYN (−4.5). Figure S1 shows the averaged density distributions of the dense and dilute phases for water and peptides in each system. The densities of peptides in the condensates span from ∼0.65 g/cm3 for NYANYN to ∼0.85 g/cm3 for YQHQHY. Overall, the water density within the condensates increases with the intrinsic hydrophilicity of the peptides.

  We then calculated the small-molecule partitioning coefficients (K) for the peptide-rich phase. Figure   shows K values versus (a) the compounds’ log P values (the partitioning coefficient of a solute between octanol and water, obtained from Xlog P3 3.0), (b) the logarithm of solubility (at 25 °C based on ref ), and (c) molecular diameter estimated based on the van der Waals volume using the ProteinVolume 1.3 tool.

  We observe a strong linear relationship between log P and K, particularly for systems composed of more hydrophobic peptides (e.g., FWAFWF and FYAFYF in Figure  a). This result is consistent with the work of Thody et al., who reported that partitioning is primarily governed by compound hydrophobicity. In contrast, our results show an inverse relationship between compound solubility and K (Figure  b), which is intuitive due to the negative correlation between log P and the logarithm of solubility (Figure S2a). Furthermore, compounds with high log P values, such as styrene and butane, partition significantly more into the highly hydrophobic condensates FYAFYF and FWAFWF, with butane deviating from the overall linear trend and reaching K values as high as 24. We attribute this behavior to butane’s relatively small size and high log P value, which together facilitate its strong preference for the condensed phase. In all systems, we also observe slightly higher K values for dioxane and acetic acidboth with low log P valuescompared to molecules such as isopropanol, pyrrole, and cisplatin, which have higher log P values. For acetic acid, a higher K can be attributed to its small molecular diameter of 4.8 Å (Figure  c), which facilitates easy penetration into the condensate. The more flexible cyclic ether structure of dioxane, compared to smaller, higher log P molecules like pyrrolewhose rigidity arises from delocalized π-electronsmay contribute to its higher K values. In contrast, cisplatin, a bulky and rigid metal complex, has limited flexibility due to its platinum center and coordinated ligands, restricting its access to certain regions of the condensate. Although these structural properties have a weaker effect than hydrophobicity and solubility, molecular size and flexibility can still contribute to condensate selectivity, albeit to a lesser extent, consistent with previous findings in ref .

  In general, as condensates become more enriched in polar residues (e.g., NYANYN and YQHQHY systems), the relationship between log P and K weakens. Moreover, in such systems, the partitioning of certain compounds yield unexpected results. For instance, in NYANYN, nitrobenzene exhibits an appreciably greater K than compounds with higher log P. A similar trend is observed in YQHQHY, where nitrobenzene, along with thiophenol and benzonitrile, shows unusually high K values despite being relatively large molecules (Figure  c) with moderate hydrophobicity. We hypothesize that such behavior is driven by specific interactions between compounds and protein residues. Because condensates enriched in polar residues do not provide sufficient enthalpic gain to compensate for the entropic cost of partitioning into a confined polar space, most hydrophobic molecules preferentially remain in the solvent. In contrast, nitrobenzene demonstrates relatively high K values in both NYANYN and YQHQHY condensates, despite its moderate hydrophobicity. This enhanced partitioning likely stems from nitrobenzene’s large dipole moment, which facilitates favorable dipole–dipole and dipole−π interactions with polar and aromatic residues. To investigate this further, we select compounds with log P > 1.5 and plot their dipole moment against their K in each system (Figure S3). For condensates enriched with polar residues, this analysis reveals a strong positive correlation. Notably, YQHQHYcapable of supporting stronger dipole–dipole and dipole−π interactions due to its compositionexhibits a stronger correlation than NYANYN. Overall, these results suggest that condensates enriched in polar amino acids can preferentially recruit less hydrophobic compounds via dipole-mediated interactions. This result underscores the potential to engineer selective small-molecule partitioning into condensates via specific binding mechanisms.

  Overall, molecules exhibit higher K values in more hydrophobic condensates, with partitioning values ranging from 0 to 1.5 in condensates enriched in polar residues and up to ∼24 in those rich in hydrophobic residues. This trend is consistent with previous studies, which experimentally demonstrated that small molecules preferentially partition into condensates with greater hydrophobicity. We also find that the magnitude of the difference in a compound’s partitioning across different condensates is proportional to the compound’s hydrophobicity. To quantify this, we plot the K value of each compound as a function of the hydrophilicity of the peptides comprising the model condensates. We extracted the absolute value of the slope (Figure S2b), which we define here as the compound’s “partitioning sensitivity” (K*). These values were computed for each compound except for acetic acid, toluene, and cisplatin, which showed weaker correlations (r < 0.7) between K and peptide hydrophilicity due to their consistently low partitioning across all systems. We then compared log­(|K*|) against the corresponding log P values (Figure S2c). We observe a strong linear relationship: most compounds show a high correlation, with pyrrole being the only outlier due to generally low K values. These results suggest that sensitivity to the condensate environment increases with a compound’s hydrophobicity.

  Altogether, we conclude that small-molecule hydrophobicity is a major driver of partitioning, with its effect being more pronounced in condensates enriched with hydrophobic residues. In contrast, in condensates rich in polar residues, entropic contributions appear to play a greater role, and specific molecular interactions can override the general dependence of K on log P. These findings highlight the dual influence of hydrophobicity and binding affinity of the condensate environment in governing small-molecule partitioning into biomolecular condensates.

  While peptide-based model condensates serve as useful systems for understanding the partitioning behaviors, it is important to probe these effects in more biologically relevant condensates. All-atom simulations, while detailed, are computationally expensive and inefficient for exploring a wide variety of systems or capturing the effects of small molecules over long time scales. To overcome these limitations, we leveraged atomistic simulations to develop a minimal coarse-grained approach that focuses on the key factors influencing small-molecule partitioning. This method enables the study of small-molecule partitioning for more biologically relevant condensates such as those composed of low-complexity domains of proteins.


### Designing Minimal Models for Predicting Small-Molecule
Partitioning into Biomolecular Condensates
  The interactions between small molecules and condensates are governed by a complex interplay of forces, including dispersive forces, electrostatics, and hydrogen bonding. Accurately modeling these interactions across a wide parameter space and diverse systems can quickly become computationally prohibitive. To efficiently simulate the partitioning behavior of small molecules and their impact on condensate properties, it is beneficial to use an approach that simplifies the system while retaining essential features. Such an approach should, therefore, effectively describe interactions between small molecules (SM) and macromolecules (e.g., proteins) in condensed systems while also considering other physical characteristics of condensates, such as solvent-occupied volume.

  Several chemically specific CG models were previously developed to describe protein–protein interactions in phase-separated systems. −       Here, we adopt the Mpipi model, a residue-resolution approach with implicit solvent that has been successfully employed to characterize the phase behavior of disordered proteins. The Mpipi potential captures both bonded and nonbonded protein interactions, including long-range electrostatics and short-range nonbonded contacts. The nonbonded interactions were parametrized using all-atom potentials-of-mean-force (PMF) calculations of amino acid pairs ,  and bioinformatics data. Adopting Mpipi as a foundational model, we developed a minimal CG approach to predict small-molecule partitioning into condensates (Figure  ). We hypothesize that this approach will provide an efficient means of estimating the partition coefficients of small molecules into biologically relevant condensates while enabling us to identify the dominant physicochemical properties.

  Since in Mpipi the protein–protein interactions are partially derived from PMF calculations, one approach to incorporating small molecules is to compute PMFs (for SM–SM as well as SM–X pairs, where X represents a protein residue). Here, the compounds in our test set are small enough to be represented as a single interaction site (molecular diameter less than 6 Å), and all have a net zero charge. Thus, PMF calculations (Figure  a) can be directly used to determine the parameters representing the relative strength of interactions of the Wang–Frenkel potential, which captures short-ranged nonbonded interactions. We initially employed this approach; however, the resulting model failed to accurately reproduce the partitioning observed in our atomistic simulations (Figure S4).

  There are several reasons why relying solely on SM–SM/SM–X interactions via PMFs is insufficient to capture the observed trends in partitioning. While PMFs can reflect the effective relative interaction strength, they do not effectively capture the effects of a condensate environment, which can affect the available space for the molecules and hence their distribution between phases, factors important for the partitioning of the compounds. From a technical standpoint, computing PMFs between small molecules and residues is nontrivial, as commonly used techniques (e.g., employing positional restraints) are not readily applicable due to the lack of a priori chemical intuition about the dominant interaction mode between a given compound and protein residue. Overall, environmental and interaction-specific factors that modulate a molecule’s preference for the dilute phase or condensate need to be carefully considered to accurately describe partitioning behavior.

  Additionally, by reducing the complexity of a system by simplifying its representation, the chemical specificity of a compound may be lost due to the exclusion of certain degrees of freedom and the neglect of charge distribution. To enhance the chemical specificity of the model, one approach is to increase the resolution of the SM representation. While this would improve accuracy, it would also result in a higher computational cost. Alternatively, chemical specificity can be captured indirectly by accounting for the key factors that drive small-molecule partitioning into condensates.

  Therefore, rather than relying solely on PMFs for parametrization of both SM–SM and SM–X interactions, we design an optimization approach that accounts for both SM characteristics and the physicochemical environment of the condensates. First, as before, we compute PMFs for SM–SM interactions (Figure  a), which are used to determine the parameters of the Wang–Frenkel potential εSM–SM and μSM–SM (see eq   in section  ) representing the SM–SM interaction strength in the CG model (see section  ). However, to determine SM–X interactions, we initially design model (YXY)2 condensates (where Y represents tyrosine and X represents an amino acid) at atomistic resolution. For a given small molecule, we then characterize the partitioning atomistically and iteratively optimize the CG parameters to reproduce the atomistic observables (Table S2), effectively determining the interaction terms for SM–X (similar to Figure  b). Strikingly, the resulting CG model is transferable. When we probe SM partitioning at the CG level for condensates not included in the training set, we observe good agreement with all-atom behaviors. This finding supports our hypothesis that both small-molecule properties and condensate chemical environments are critical for governing the partitioning. We should note, however, that the developed models are designed to reproduce partitioning rather than the bulk-phase thermodynamic properties of the pure compounds. As a result, the optimization strategy prioritized partitioning behavior over accurately capturing the pure-phase properties of the compounds.

  A key drawback of the above parametrization approach is its labor-intensive nature. For each new small molecule, the process requires simulating its partitioning into approximately 20 model (YXY)2 condensates, simulating the corresponding CG model condensates, and performing optimizations across multiple parameter sets. However, motivated by the success of this optimization approach in capturing partitioning trends, we sought to identify empirical relationships that describe SM–X interactions in condensate environments.

### Small-Molecule Partitioning Observed Atomistically
Is Captured via Coarse-Grained Simulation

#### Peptide-Based Condensates
  We first validate the MAPPS approach by characterizing the partitioning of selected small molecules into model condensates. For five peptide-based condensates (FYAFYF, FWAFWF, YQHQHY, NYANYN, and NFWAFS), we simulate a subset of small molecules that exhibit divergent partitioning in all-atom simulations: cisplatin, phenol, 2-methylpropane, styrene, and nitrobenzene. The comparison of the partitioning coefficients obtained from CG simulations via MAPPS with the corresponding values from the atomistic simulations is presented in Figure  a. The CG simulations capture the trends observed in the atomistic simulations for each model condensate, showing increased partitioning with increasing compound hydrophobicity (Figure S9). Overall, we observe better agreement with the all-atom simulations for more hydrophobic condensates: FWAFWF and FYAFYF. For FWAFWF, the CG and all-atom (AA) simulations show good agreement for the majority of the compounds. Using the FWAFWF system, we further analyze the performance of the models for a range of small-molecule concentrations (Figure S10). This analysis revealed the independence of K from the concentration of the compounds, as expected and observed for the atomistic analogues.

  For the larger set of compounds (displayed in the legend of Figure  ), we simulate their partitioning in FYAFYF, FWAFWF, YQHQHY, and NYANYN condensates and compare the results against the values obtained in the corresponding all-atom simulations (Figure  b, c). Additionally, Figure S9 summarizes K versus log P, solubility, and the molecular diameter (size) for the CG systems, similar to Figure  . Consistent with the atomistic simulations, small molecules exhibit higher K values as the hydrophobic content of the condensate increases, ranging from a maximum of 1.2 in NYANYN to 2.8 in YQHQHY and reaching approximately 8 in FYAFYF and FWAFWF. Moreover, AA and CG simulations show closer agreement for partitioning into the more hydrophobic condensates, FYAFYF and FWAFWF (Figure  b). However, the correlation with log P is weaker for these hydrophobic systems than in the atomistic simulations, primarily because styrene partitioning is underestimated in both peptides and toluene in FWAFWF. This underestimation likely stems from an insufficient representation of favorable interactions with F residues (Table S4), potentially due to bias in the single-chain calculations. Improved agreement might be achieved by sampling a broader set of random single-chain sequences to better capture these interactions.

  Additionally, the CG simulations systematically overestimate partitioning in condensates enriched with polar residues, especially for high log P compounds such as styrene. We hypothesize that incorporating additional terms into eq  to better capture polarity effects and solvent entropycould improve these predictions. Furthermore, across all condensates, partitioning of hydrophilic moleculesdioxane, cyclopentanol, furan, and acetic aciddeviates markedly from atomistic predictions. This further underscores the model’s deficiency in accurately capturing polarity-driven interactions.

  In summary, MAPPS captures partitioning trends in hydrophobic environments and offers a quick estimate of partitioning magnitudes across condensates of diverse compositions. However, it overestimates partitioning into more polar condensates. This reduced accuracy in polar environments indicates that small molecule–solvent interactions are not fully captured, underscoring the need for refined parametrization with additional interaction terms to better represent solvent effects.


#### FUS LCD Condensate
  In addition to the peptide-based condensates, we also tested our MAPPS approach against atomistic simulations for condensates composed of low-complexity domains (LCDs). Here, we report our results for small-molecule partitioning into an FUS LCD condensate.

  To simulate the FUS LCD condensate atomistically, we first equilibrate it at CG resolution using Mpipi and then employ a backmapping procedure to generate the corresponding AA configuration (Figure  a). Due to the high computational costs, the all-atom simulations with FUS LCD include only a small set of compoundscisplatin, 2-methylpropane, pyrrole, and thiophenolwhich we also simulated using the CG approach. Specifically, the compounds chosen for testing with FUS LCD gave good agreement between CG and AA predictions for the peptide-based condensates.

  Across the two resolutions, we obtain a good agreement in partitioning coefficients for the compounds tested (Figure  b; r = 0.95), although the CG predictions are systematically higher. The quantitative discrepancy may primarily stem from the longer time scales needed to capture the partitioning in the all-atom simulation, where diffusion is hindered by the presence of explicit solvents and ions. Additionally, since the FUS LCD has a large fraction of polar residues (58%), the consistently higher K values observed in the CG simulations may result from the systematic overestimation of the partitioning into more polar environments, as was shown for the peptide-based systems (Figure  c).

  We also examined whether the interaction landscapes of the compounds in the condensates are consistent at the two length scales. Specifically, we measure the contact probability of each compound with the residues in FUS LCD in the phase-separated systems (Figure  c). Here, we observe a strong linear correlation (r = 0.91) between the frequency of residue contacts at the AA and CG resolutions. This result strongly suggests that the CG simulations can capture key interaction patterns found in the higher-resolution AA system. However, the number of compound–residue contacts in CG simulations is much lower than those at the atomistic level. This behavior likely stems from the coarser resolution of the CG model, which omits the sharper, more specific interactions explicitly captured in AA simulations. Notwithstanding this, Figure S11 shows the distribution of contacts per residue for each compound, illustrating the high Pearson correlation between the interactions across the two resolutions.


### LCD-Based Condensates Exhibit Varying Partitioning
of Small Molecules
  Using CG simulations, we investigate small-molecule partitioning into condensates composed of low-complexity domains (LCDs) of proteins. We focus on FUS, hnRNPA1 (here, A1), EWSR1, and TIA1 LCDs that have been shown to be important for condensate formation in vitro and in cells −     (the sequences are given in Table S8). The small molecule-to-protein molar concentration (in the range of 1.5–4.0) is chosen to be consistent with experimental assays. ,  Figure  a illustrates the residue composition of each LCD. The proportion of hydrophobic (i.e., aliphatic and aromatic) residues in the LCDs decreases in the following order: TIA1, EWSR1, FUS, A1. The corresponding density distributions for the pure protein systems and the small molecules are shown in Figure S12. Among the LCD condensates, TIA1 is the densest, FUS and A1 have similar intermediate densities, and EWSR1 is the least dense; all four are markedly less dense than the model peptide-based condensates.

  Figure  b shows the distribution of the partitioning coefficients in each LCD-based condensate obtained for a set of small molecules at 300 K and 150 mM salt. Additionally, Figure  c–f show the K values as a function of the compound’s log P value for each system. For each condensate, a clear trend is observed, wherein K increases with small-molecule hydrophobicity. For LCDs with a higher proportion of polar residues (TIA1, EWSR1, FUS), we observe a lower partitioning of higher log P molecules such as 2-methylpropane and toluene compared to nitrobenzene, which is consistent with the behavior observed in the model condensates.

  To analyze whether the distributions of partitioning values differ significantly across proteins, we use the Kruskal–Wallis test. This test is a nonparametric method for comparing three or more independent groups and evaluates the null hypothesis that all of the groups share the same underlying distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis test did not indicate statistically significant differences across the proteins (test statistic H = 6.37, p = 0.095, while statistical significance is defined as a p-value below 0.05). We hence follow up with Dunn’s posthoc test, using the Bonferroni correction, to explore potential pairwise differences. Dunn’s test compares all possible pairs of groups while controlling for multiple comparisons. While none of the adjusted p-values reached significance, the comparison between EWSR1 and TIA1 (p = 0.11) yielded the lowest value, suggesting a potential trend. This lack of significant difference likely reflects the relatively narrow range of K values among the LCD proteins, which fall within the broader bounds observed for the peptide systems. Further, as the LCDs share more similar amino acid compositions, they yield comparable interaction landscapes.

  Although small-molecule partitioning, quantified by K values, spans a similar range across multiple LCD systems, there are still demonstrably different partitioning behaviors. Among the LCDs, TIA1 and A1 exhibit the lowest K values (Figure  b, inset). In the case of TIA1, the high condensate density likely offsets the effect of its elevated hydrophobic residue content, resulting in reduced compound partitioning with a median K value below 1. Conversely, A1 displays a relatively moderate condensate density and the lowest fraction of hydrophobic residues, resulting in similarly low K values. EWSR1, which exhibits a relatively low density in combination with a high proportion of hydrophobic residues, shows elevated K values. These observations are in good agreement with trends observed in the all-atom simulations.

  Interestingly, styrene shows a pronounced preference for TIA1, which suggests that when high-affinity interactions are present, they can overcome the transport limitations imposed by high condensate density, leading to elevated K values even in dense environments. Similarly, nitrobenzene displays a strong preference for FUS and EWSR1, with K values exceeding 1.5, likely driven by the specificity of its interactions with polar residues. This behavior is consistent with the all-atom simulations, which showed that nitrobenzene favors polar environments. Additionally, dioxane exhibits a higher K value than furan exclusively in A1, indicating a possible specific interaction between dioxane and residues enriched in A1 condensates.

  Among the LCDs, partitioning coefficients show the greatest variability for high log P compounds. Styrene, the most hydrophobic compound considered, shows the widest variability, with partitioning values spanning from K ∼ 2 in A1 to a maximum of K = 3 in TIA1. Thiophenol, the next most hydrophobic compound, also has notable variation, with K ranging from ∼1 in A1 to ∼2 in EWSR. This trend aligns with the previous conclusion that partitioning sensitivity correlates with a compound’s log P value.

  The analysis of partitioning into LCD condensates, along with the consistent trends observed in the peptide-based systems, reinforces the conclusion that partitioning is governed by several factors, encompassing the chemical specificity of compounds and the physicochemical properties of condensates, including their density. Thus, accurately predicting partitioning trends across different proteins requires considering the interplay between diffusion constraints and high-affinity interactions. Therefore, when molecular potency is evaluated, crowders and other environmental factors shaping the condensed phase must be considered; neglecting them can mislead interpretations of partitioning trends.


## Conclusions
  In recent years, biomolecular condensates have been positioned as therapeutic targets, which have sparked intense interest in elucidating the factors that drive small-molecule partitioning into condensates. In this work, we leverage molecular dynamics simulations to interrogate which factors dictate the localization of small molecules into condensates. At atomistic resolution, we systematically examine partitioning behaviors across ∼75 systems comprising a diverse set of small molecules, peptide-based condensates, and the FUS-LCD system. Our findings show a strong linear relationship between the hydrophobicity of small molecules (log P) and their partitioning into condensates enriched in hydrophobic residues. In more polar condensates, however, the linear trend weakens; chemical specificity, e.g., dipole–dipole and dipole−π interactions, overtakes hydrophobicity as the primary driver of partitioning. Hence, our findings suggest that polar residues can offset the entropic cost of localization for compounds with high dipole moments, resulting in a preferential uptake of compounds with a lower log P. This highlights the critical role of the condensate chemical environment in dictating molecular partitioning within condensates. Overall, our atomistic simulations are consistent with recent studies ,  and point to a complex interplay between small-molecule characteristics and condensate chemical environment.

  Leveraging these insights, we optimized a set of minimal coarse-grained (CG) models (MAPPS) to efficiently predict small-molecule partitioning into biologically relevant condensates. Our approach, built upon a residue-resolution CG model of proteins, captures the chemical specificity of small molecule–protein interaction via an affinity score and accounts for the effective free volume for accommodating small molecules via a derived empirical relation. We show that MAPPS demonstrates good transferability and predictability for different systems, including in predicting small-molecule partitioning into LCD-based condensates, as validated by all-atom simulations of the FUS LCD system. MAPPS effectively captures partitioning among various compounds for condensates enriched in hydrophobic residues and accurately reflects increased partitioning in more hydrophobic condensates. MAPPS, therefore, offers a fast and versatile approach for estimating the partitioning of organic compounds into biologically relevant protein condensates.

  As examples, we demonstrate that MAPPS enables efficient screening of small-molecule interactions with condensates of FUS, hnRNPA1, EWSR1, and TIA1 LCDs, revealing some differences in the partitioning behavior across these proteins. We find that in EWSR1 condensates small molecules show a strong preference for the condensed phase, whereas partitioning is generally lower in TIA1 condensates. However, highly hydrophobic molecules deviate from this trend, displaying increased partitioning in TIA1, likely due to selective interactions driven by the high proportion of hydrophobic residues. The variation in partitioning across different proteins underscores the influence of sequence composition and the chemical specificity of small-molecule interactions. Interestingly, despite being the most hydrophobic LCD, TIA1 does not necessarily promote the strongest partitioning. Our simulations indicate that TIA1 condensates exhibit higher densities under similar conditions, resulting in smaller pore sizes and restricted diffusion. This result suggests that beyond chemical affinity, physical properties such as condensate density impose additional constraints on partitioning. Consequently, experimentalists must account for factors such as crowding agents and condensate density when assessing the selectivity of small molecules for condensates.

  Despite the success of MAPPS in capturing trends in partitioning, we find that as the fraction of polar residues grows, the approach overestimates partitioning relative to all-atom results. Since the LCDs probed here contain a sizable fraction of polar residues, we expect the predictions to preserve the trends in partitioning while representing an upper bound. MAPPS also shows significant deviations for hydrophilic small molecules. These discrepancies highlight opportunities to better account for effects due to solvent and small-molecule representation in future refinements.

  Together, our modeling approach enables the rapid estimation of partitioning trends in biologically relevant condensates, demonstrating good predictability for high log P compounds in hydrophobic condensates. By following the MAPPS procedure, new compounds of interest can be efficiently parametrized for predictive simulations. In the future, we aim to expand MAPPS to a comprehensive library of parametrized compounds, including practical pharmaceuticals, providing a readily accessible resource for screening interactions with LCDs. More broadly, our findings show that small-molecule partitioning depends not only on the compounds’ chemical properties but also on the condensates’ physicochemical characteristics. These findings underscore the importance of considering both molecular interactions and condensate properties when evaluating partitioning behavior.

### Simulations Models

#### Mpipi Model for Protein–Protein Interactions
  The coarse-grained (CG) models for the small molecules are built upon Mpipi, a residue-resolution sequence-specific CG model, which was previously shown to effectively predict phase separation of intrinsically disordered proteins.

  In Mpipi, each amino acid is represented by a single bead, with its corresponding physical properties such as mass, molecular diameter (σ), charge (q), and an energy scale that reflects the relative frequency of planar π–π contacts (ε). The total interaction energy is a sum of bonded interactions within a chain E bond, electrostatic contributions E elec, and short-ranged nonbonded interactions between beads E pair: 4 EMpipi=Ebond+Eelec+Epair

The uncharged nonbonded interactions are modeled via the Wang–Frenkel potential: 7 Epair=∑i,jεi,jαi,j[(σi,jr)2μi,j−1][(rcr)2μi,j−1]2νi,j where 8 α=2νi,j(rcσi,j)2μi,j[1+2νi,j2νi,j[(rc/σi,j)2μi,j−1]]2νi,j+1 σ i,j , ε i,j , and μ i,j  are parameters specified for each pair of interacting beads, and r  c  is the cutoff radius. For most amino acids, in the original Mpipi implementation, μ i,j  = 1 and ν i,j  = 1. r  c  is 3σ i,j  for each pair. All parameters for the Mpipi model can be found in ref .


#### Coarse-Grained Parameters for Small Molecules
  The atomistic potential-of-mean-force (PMF) profiles are used to determine the relative strength of self-interactions of the small molecules (εSM–SM), described by the Wang–Frenkel potential (eq  ). The area of the first well in PMF profiles for a pair of molecules is numerically evaluated and compared with the PMF of tyrosine–tyrosine interactions. This ratio is then used to assess the relative strength of the interactions between the molecules. The parameters εSM–SM and μSM–SM (with fixed νSM–SM = 1 according to the value for most amino acids in Mpipi) are then varied so that (1) the ratio εSM–SM to εY (tyrosine self-interaction from the Mpipi model) deviates by no more than 10% from the ratio of a minimum of the PMF profile of the molecule–molecule interaction to the minimum of the PMF profile of the tyrosine–tyrosine interaction and (2) the ratio of the well areas under the curve of the PMF profile to such area of the Wang–Frenkel potential corresponding to the SM–SM and tyrosine–tyrosine pairs deviates by no more than 10%. The obtained parameters εSM–SM and μSM–SM are assigned to the small molecule represented as a single interaction site (a bead). The bead size σSM–SM is calculated based on the molecule’s estimated van der Waals radius. Each bead is assigned a neutral charge. The σSM–X, which represents the small molecule–X (where X is an amino acid) distance when the potential is equal to zero, is obtained based on the Lorentz arithmetic rule, using the values of the Mpipi model for the amino acids σresidue.


### Test Systems
  We studied a set of small molecules to assess the impact of hydrophobicity, solubility, and molecular size on the partitioning: 2-methylpropane, acetic acid, aniline, benzene, benzonitrile, butane, cisplatin, cyclopentanol, dioxane, furan, isopropanol, nitrobenzene, phenol, pyrrole, styrene, thiophenol, and toluene. These molecules were selected as they are comparable in size, small enough to represent a single bead (estimated molecular diameter less than 6 Å), and have zero net charge. The transferability of the models is validated by calculating their partitioning into a variety of model condensates: FWAFWF, FYAFYF, NYANYN, NFWAFS, and YQHQHY. The number of protein chains, small molecules, and their molar concentration in each system are given in Table S9 for the all-atom simulations and Table S10 for the CG simulations.

  We also examine partitioning of small molecules into condensates formed by the low-complexity domain (LCD) of the proteins FUS, EWSR1, TIA1, and A1 of 12, 7, 20, and 16 mM concentrations, respectively. The concentration of each of the small molecules is 30 mM. The choice of the concentrations is based on the recent experimental works on small-molecule partitioning, where the molar concentration of small molecules was several times higher than the protein, ,  and to ensure a consistent number density of protein residues in a simulation of approximately 1.2 beads/nm3. All systems are modeled by using direct coexistence simulations in the slab geometry.


### Potential-of-Mean-Force Calculations
  PMF calculations are performed with the GROMACS software. The Amber03ws force field with the JC-SPC/E-ion/TIP4P/2005 water and ions model are used to model amino acids and water–ion interactions. The initial structures of the small molecules are optimized with the Restrained Electrostatic Potential (RESP) fitting approach at the HF/6–31G­(d,p) level of theory using Gaussian software. The charges obtained are combined with the GAFF2 force field parameters for bonded and nonbonded interaction parameters of a molecule, generated via AmberTools. As GAFF2 does not incorporate parameters for platinum, the AMBER force field parameters for cisplatin are taken from ref .

  Each pair of molecules is placed in a cubic box of size 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å and solvated with water and ions at 150 mM NaCl concentration. The distance between the centers of mass of the two structures is used as the umbrella sampling coordinate, with a distance of 14 Å taken as the reference state. At least 50 umbrella windows are used for each pair, with additional windows added for certain systems that require extra sampling. Each window is initially energy-minimized by using the steepest-descent algorithm. An umbrella sampling simulation of 10 ns is performed for each window, with the harmonic force constant varying from 2000 to 6000 kJ/mol/nm2. The potential mean force profile is then calculated using the weighted histogram analysis method.


### Frequency of Contacts
  Atomistic simulations are used to estimate the normalized number of contacts, N contacts, between a small molecule and each protein residue. To do this, we simulate a set of 7–8 single chains composed of the 20 amino acids randomly arranged. Each chain and molecule are placed in a cubic box and solvated with water and ions. The Amber03ws force field with JC-SPC/E-ion/TIP4P/2005 force field for water and ions are used. The system is energy-minimized, and a simulation in the NVT ensemble is performed for at least 500 ns. The number of contacts between the nonheavy atoms of a residue and the molecule within 6 Å is calculated from the trajectories. First, contact values are binarized (1 if present, 0 otherwise) and then summed to obtain the total number of frames with contacts. This sum is divided by the total number of frames, yielding a normalized contact frequency between 0 and 1. For each system, we performed three independent runs, placing the molecule in a random position. The mean and standard error of the mean are calculated across the chains and the three sets of runs for each chain.

  In the all-atom simulation of the FUS LCD condensate, the contacts are computed for trajectories from 300 to 600 ns of the simulation time, collected every 10 ns. The values are additionally normalized by the number of occurrences of the residue in the protein sequence and the total chain length, and the error is estimated as the standard error across the values.

  In the CG simulations of the FUS LCD condensate, the number of contacts is averaged across the trajectories collected every 1 ns, calculated within a pairwise cutoff distance for each residue–small molecule pair (r cut = 1.5σSM–X). A contact is defined as any occurrence of the center of mass of a molecule within the cutoff distance from a residue’s center of mass. The normalized contact frequency values are calculated using a method similar to that applied for the all-atom simulation data.


### Simulation of Phase Coexistence and Partitioning

#### All-Atom Simulations of Peptide-Based Condensates
  All-atom simulations of phase coexistence in a solution with small molecules are performed using GROMACS software. Simulation rendering is performed using the Open Visualization Tool (OVITO) software. Small molecules are represented as in the PMF calculations. For protein structures, the Amber03ws force field is used with the JC-SPC/E-ion/TIP4P/2005 force field for water and ions. The initial configurations of the peptides (capped with the NME and ACE groups) are generated using PyMOL.

  For the model peptides, 100–150 chains are simulated. For each system, the peptides are first placed in a cubic box and minimized with the steepest descent algorithm, and the system was relaxed in NPT ensemble at 1–50 bar and 300 K for 50–100 ns with a time step of 1–2 ps to achieve the protein density of approximately 1 g/cm3. The temperature is kept constant using a stochastic velocity rescaling algorithm, and the pressure is kept constant with a Parrinello–Rahman barostat. Bonds involving hydrogen atoms are constrained to their equilibrium lengths using the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions are modeled using the particle-mesh Ewald method with a grid spacing of 0.1 nm. Dispersion interactions and short-range repulsion are described by a Lennard–Jones potential with a cutoff at 0.9 nm.

  The isotropically compressed configuration is then expanded into a slab with one of the axes of the box elongated. The slab is solvated with water and ions, and the systems are energy minimized and further equilibrated in the NVT ensemble, yielding a peptide-rich (condensed) phase and a peptide-poor (dilute) phase in coexistence. For each system, small molecules are added to the box (dilute phase region). The box is further solvated with water and ions at 150 mM concentration. The obtained configurations are energy minimized and simulated in the NVT ensemble, with equilibration for 500 ns and production runs of at least 500 ns to obtain an equilibrated cumulative average of the partitioned molecules (evolution of the values with time is shown in Figure S13). Figure S14 additionally shows the distribution of the center of mass positions for randomly selected molecules in Y6 condensate, demonstrating that the positions of the molecules are adequately sampled over the space, including the phase boundary throughout the simulation.

  For estimation of the partitioning coefficients, we analyze trajectories from the production runs, where data points are collected at 10 ns intervals. The number density profiles across the long axis of the box (divided into 50 slices, which was verified to be optimal) are averaged over the collected trajectories. The location of the interface (and hence the condensate) is determined using the DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) algorithm. The partitioning coefficient K is estimated as the ratio of the number of molecules (per bin on average across the trajectory) within the condensate to the number of molecules in the dilute phase (Figure S15). 9 K=NcondensateNdilute

  The error associated with partitioning is estimated by propagating the uncertainty from the averaged number density profiles (see Supporting Information for details of the error analysis).


#### Coarse-Grained Simulations
  All coarse-grained simulations are conducted using LAMMPS (version 23 June 2022). −    Simulation rendering is performed using OVITO. The protein interactions are modeled with the Mpipi force field. Initially, the protein copies are compressed in the NPT ensemble using a time step of 10 fs and coupled to a Langevin thermostat with a relaxation time of 10 ps. The simulation box is then expanded to a slab configuration while maintaining a volume corresponding to the concentration in the analogous all-atom simulation, achieving a total protein density of approximately 300 kg/m3. Each simulation is subsequently run in the NVT ensemble for 200 ns to obtain pre-equilibrated condensate configurations. Small molecules are randomly added to the slab configuration of the protein (N total = 350 for peptide-based systems and N total = 95–150 for LCDs). An NVT simulation is then performed for 200 ns of equilibration and 500 ns production, over which the average properties and standard errors are computed.

  The phase boundary of the condensate is estimated based on the slope of the protein density profile. Partitioning is then determined by analyzing the density profile of the small molecules relative to the condensate region. We then calculated the total number of molecules within the “condensate boundary”. As in the all-atom simulations, the partitioning coefficient K is calculated as the ratio of the number of molecules (per bin on average across the trajectory) within the condensate boundary to the number of molecules in the dilute phase.


#### All-Atom Simulations of FUS LCD
  The initial structure for all-atom simulations of the phase-separated FUS-LCD system in a slab configuration is mapped from a CG slab simulation with the Mpipi model, which is initially run for 1 microsecond with 64 FUS-LCD chains. To convert the equilibrium CG condensate into fully atomistic systems, we employ a multistep procedure aimed at accurately reconstructing the atomistic details while minimizing steric clashes. The first step involves reconstructing each chain from the final CG structure in all-atom detail using the Pulchra algorithm, a tool for restoring atomic-level connectivity and geometry from CG models. Pulchra operates by interpreting the CG structure’s backbone and side-chain information, generating atomistic models with proper stereochemistry.

  Next, we address the steric clashes that inevitably arise in the reconstructed atomistic structure due to differences in resolution between the CG and all-atom models and the high density of the condensate. To do this, we map the configuration to the Martini2 model using the Martinize script. This step is performed to reduce atomic overlaps while still preserving the explicit representation of backbone and side-chain atoms. In addition, we used standard Martini water and ions to represent the environment.

  The third phase of the backmapping process utilizes the “backward” program, which is designed specifically for mapping the CG Martini configuration back onto the full atomistic resolution. In this step, we exclude the water molecules from the Martini configuration. The result is a refined all-atom configuration. Finally, we filled the simulation box with water molecules and ions at a 150 mM NaCl concentration. The all-atom direct coexistence simulations in the slab geometry are then carried out for 600 ns using the Amber03ws force field with TIP4P water.


### Performance of MAPPS
  The entire MAPPS procedure is described in eq   and in Figure  . Step 1 involves a single PMF calculation for a given small molecule (∼50 GPU hours). Step 3 is the computation of the set affinity scores A SM–X for the small molecule. This can be estimated from about 24 independent (8 chains and 3 random molecule placements) single-chain simulations (total of ∼100 GPU hours). Step 4, which involves the condensate density ratio, does not need to be computed per small molecule. Instead, we provide these values in Table S5 for all X residues. The only expensive part of this method is Step 2 (Figure  b). On average, it takes about 7 μs of aggregate simulation time: ∼1 μs (or 120 GPU hours) for the atomistic K and ∼6 μs (or 2500 CPU hours ≈ 125 GPU hours) in CG for the optimization to get a good estimate of εSM–Y fitted. In combination with 1–4, step 5 can then be run for a biologically relevant condensate to estimate K in a given system, which takes ∼1 GPU hour. With our approach, the entire procedure takes ∼400 GPU hours and allows rapid screening of multiple systems through the CG simulations, while a 1 μs all-atom simulation of LCDs used to estimate partitioning in one system requires more than 1000 GPU hours (starting from an equilibrium condensate configuration obtained through a backmapping procedure).
