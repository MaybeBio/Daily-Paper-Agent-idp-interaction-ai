# Investigating the
Nature of PRM:SH3 Interactions Using
Artificial Intelligence and Molecular Dynamics


## Abstract
  Understanding the binding interactions within protein–peptide complexes is crucial for elucidating key physicochemical phenomena in biological systems. Among the outcomes of these interactions, biomolecular condensates have recently emerged as vital players in various cellular functions including signaling. Complexes such as PRM:SH3 are known to undergo condensation, yet the chemical interactions and governing factors driving these behaviors remain poorly understood. In this study, we combine AlphaFold2 and molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the binding nature of PRM:SH3. Our findings reveal that proline-to-alanine mutations enhance flexibility, weakening the binding affinity, while charge-altering mutations modify the binding mode and influence the binding strength. Notably, the PRM­(H) series shows that binding is primarily driven by local flexibility and the hydrophobic effect. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the root-mean-square deviation and dendrogram height are correlated to experimental dissociation constants. These insights provide a framework for understanding the binding behaviors of protein–peptide complexes and offer an effective approach for studying similar systems.


## Introduction
  Proteins interact with various biomolecules, including other proteins and peptides in living cells. Among them, protein–peptide interactions play a crucial role in many physiological processes, such as signal transduction, drug delivery, and immune response. Unlike canonical protein–protein interactions, which typically involve well-folded protein domains, protein–peptide interactions involve short peptides, usually consisting of 5–20 amino acids. Upon binding, most peptides leverage their flexibility to maximize the binding interface, often forming hydrogen bonds with backbone atoms.

  In the emerging field of biomolecular condensation, understanding protein–peptide interactions provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying condensation. Interactions between multivalent biomolecules can form transient networks, leading to biomolecular condensation both in vitro and in vivo. ,  One of the early model systems involves interaction between the Src homology 3 (SH3) domain and the proline-rich motif (PRM) peptide. The tandem repeats of SH3 and PRM can undergo condensation when mixed in vitro. ,  Another notable example is Rubisco condensation in the pyrenoid, where interactions between the multivalent Rubisco enzyme and the essential pyrenoid component 1 (EPYC1) peptide play a crucial role. ,

  The SH3:PRM system has been used as a model for in vitro condensation experiments. In their pioneering work, Li and colleagues demonstrated that the condensation of the SH3:PRM system can be regulated by controlling the valency of the interacting proteins. Using the same system, Ghosh et al. suggested that macromolecular regulators can either suppress or promote condensation. The PxxP sequence in PRM, which has a hydrophobic surface, was shown to serve as a target binding site for SH3. ,  This motif typically adopts a polyproline type II helical structure, which fits snugly into the binding groove of SH3 domains, forming a stable and specific protein–peptide interaction. This unique property of PRM sequences enables diverse protein–protein interactions, facilitating the assembly of multiprotein complexes with varied functional effects. PRM­(H) is a derivative of PRM, designed as a high-affinity, monovalent SH3 ligand, and is often used as a reference in comparisons with PRM. ,  However, there are several aspects of these interactions that remain beyond our current experimental reach due to the limitations of our experimental resolution.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) provides invaluable atomic intuition for investigating protein–peptide complexes as it enables the observation of both the atomic details of binding interactions and the global conformational changes within the complex. Due to these advantages, MD has been widely used to study SH3:PRM systems. −   Wang et al. analyzed the interactions between the Sem-5 SH3 domain and its ligands, calculating the relative binding free energies between different substitutions. Hou et al. predicted the binding affinity of peptide ligands to the human amphiphysin-1 SH3 domain, along with comparative molecular field analysis and comparative molecular similarity index analysis. Ahmad et al. investigated the mechanism of fast peptide recognition by the SH3 domain and analyzed the time-dependent orientation changes of the peptides. Despite its usefulness, limited structural information on this complex has constrained the exploration of its detailed interactions. Recently, due to the rapid development of artificial intelligence for biological applications, predicting protein–peptide complexes has become more efficient. AlphaFold2, which contributed to the awarding of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to its developers, demonstrated unprecedented accuracy in predicting protein structures. We hypothesize that using AlphaFold2 to predict initial structures could provide a better starting point for MD simulations by offering more reasonable structures for further analysis.

  In this work, motivated by the recently published experimental mutagenesis data, we combined AlphaFold2 and MD simulations to analyze the atomic details of the SH3:PRM interaction. We tested different types of mutations and investigated underlying thermodynamics to explain the effects of mutations. We also investigated the mutational effects of PRM­(H) on the SH3:PRM­(H) interaction and found that while the qualitative behaviors are similar, the driving thermodynamics are different.


## Computational Details
  To generate all initial structures, we used ColabFold, which enables fast prediction of protein complexes within Many-against-Many sequence searching (MMseqs2) ,  and AlphaFold2 framework, based on the sequences of the SH3 protein and PRM peptide provided in ref  (Table  ). For efficient simulations of the protein–peptide complexes, we removed the tails of the SH3 protein, as these regions remained disordered even after simulations with the full sequences and apparently did not contribute to the binding mode. Using these truncated sequences, we predicted the initial structures from AlphaFold2 and selected the top-ranked structures as the initial models for MD simulations. For the PRM­(H) P7AP9A, however, we chose the second-ranked structure because a significantly different binding structure was predicted for the first-ranked structure (Figure S1). The structures of the unbound PRM or PRM­(H) peptides were generated by using only their sequence information and AlphaFold2.

  MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.3 with the CHARMM36m force field (March 2019). ,  Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm. For NVT equilibration, 100 ps simulations were conducted at 300 K using the velocity rescaling thermostat with a time step of 2 fs. Subsequent NPT simulations were also performed at 300 K for an additional 100 ps to further equilibrate the system, employing both the Berendsen barostat and the velocity rescaling thermostat. During both NVT and NPT equilibration processes, positional restraints were applied to the heavy atoms of the protein and peptide with spring constants of 1000 kJ/mol·nm2, allowing the solvent to equilibrate around the protein. After NPT equilibration, production MD simulations were carried out without restraints for 100 ns. The first 10 ns were considered as the equilibration period and were discarded from the analysis. To ensure robust results, all simulations were repeated 10 times under the same computational conditions for all complexes (total 0.9 μs per system). All simulations were initiated from the same AlphaFold2-predicted structure. Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent method, ensuring that the structures across all 10 simulations were identical at this stage. The velocity distributions were generated using the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at 300 K, with distinct random seeds. Following this, each NVT equilibrium simulation was started using the generated velocity distribution. The simulations of the unbound PRM were performed using only the PRM structure, while those of the bound PRM structures were carried out using the PRM:SH3 complex. The molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method was used to calculate the solvation free energy (G sol) of the peptides in their bound states.

  To obtain contact maps and distance maps, we calculated the inter-residue distances using the positions of the α carbon atoms and set the contact cutoff at 1 nm. Trajectory analysis was performed using MDTraj. Potential of mean force (PMF) in Ramachandran plots was calculated by applying the natural logarithm to the populations in the plot and then multiplying the logarithm of the populations by −1. For the zero bin, we assigned a value of 0.5 to avoid issues when applying the natural logarithm. Pairwise RMSD was calculated by pairing each frame with every other frame, aligning one structure to another, and then computing the RMSD. This produces a square matrix of RMSD values, where the dimensions correspond to the square of the number of frames in the trajectory. The average root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was calculated by averaging all pairwise RMSD values. We used two types of RMSD values depending on the alignment reference. The RMSD of PRM aligned with respect to SH3 refers to the RMSD of PRM after aligning the structures to minimize the RMSD of SH3. The RMSD of PRM aligned with respect to the PRM refers to the RMSD of PRM after aligning the structures with respect to PRM. For clustering, the Ward algorithm was applied using the RMSD of PRM aligned with respect to SH3.


## Results and Discussion

### PRM WT Analysis
  Figure  a shows the representative structures of the PRM wild-type (WT) peptide in its unbound and bound forms. Thermal fluctuations are observed across all regions in the unbound form, while in the bound form, the C-terminal region exhibits smaller fluctuations due to its interaction with SH3. The average RMSD is significantly reduced upon binding (Figure  b), while the radius of gyration (R g) remains comparable (Figures S2 and S3).

  We used a contact frequency map to quantitatively investigate the interface between the protein and peptide (Figure  c). Three main contact regions were identified: (1) PRM residues 6–10 with SH3 residues 7–10, (2) PRM residues 10–14 with SH3 residues 34–36, and (3) PRM residues 7–13 with SH3 residues 48–52. Region 1 is extended to include transient interactions between the N-terminus of PRM and SH3 residues 8–13. Interestingly, the electrostatic interactions between acidic and basic residues are not always significant, particularly between PRM residues 1–5 and SH3 residues 13–17.

  We also investigated the dihedral angle distributions of PRM in its unbound and bound forms (Figure  d). It is notable that the C-terminal half shows a population shrinkage to specific regions in the Ramachandran space. For example, Lys12 can occupy all of the αR, β, PPII, and even αL regions in its unbound form, but upon binding, it predominantly populates the PPII region. This suggests that the C-terminal half of PRM is relatively strongly bound to SH3 and that the binding restricts the movement of the peptide, thereby reducing its fluctuations and conformational entropy.


### Effects of Charge-Changing Mutations
  Electrostatic interactions between charged residues are one of the main types of interactions in proteins, particularly in peptides and disordered proteins. Based on this fact, we hypothesized that charge-changing mutations could significantly affect the binding thermodynamics. The four consecutive lysine residues (KKKK) in the N-terminal region of PRM (residues 2–5) are notable in the sequence, so we replaced this basic tract with either an acidic tract or a neutral hydrophobic sequence. The basic-to-acidic mutant, denoted as PRM +/–, was created by changing KKKK to ELEL. In the basic-to-neutral mutant, called PRM +/0, KKKK was replaced by FIFI.

  The structural ensembles of PRM +/– and PRM +/0 are shown in Figure  a and Figure b, respectively. The R g values indicate that binding does not significantly affect the chain dimensions (Figures S2 and S3). We also obtained their contact maps (Figure S4) and compared them with the contact map of PRM WT (Figure  c,d). They both show globally similar patterns. The electrostatic interactions between the KKKK region of PRM (residues 2–5) and the acidic tract of SH3 (residues 13–17) are diminished, while interactions between the mutated region of PRM and residues 7 and 8 of SH3 are enhanced. This can be explained by the synergistic effect of electrostatic interactions between the glutamates of PRM (Glu2 and Glu4) and the lysine of SH3 (Lys7) and hydrophobic interactions between the hydrophobic residues of PRM (phenylalanine and isoleucine) and the phenylalanine of SH3 (Phe8). These new interactions are stronger, resulting in a conformational change that stretches the peptide.


### Effects of Proline-to-Alanine Mutations
  We analyzed the effects of mutations in the proline-rich region. To investigate the role of proline residues, we mutated two proline residues (Pro8 and Pro11) to alanine. We tested the single mutants P8A and P11A, as well as the double mutant P8AP11A. As shown in Figure  a–c, the mutants exhibited larger fluctuations than the PRM WT even in their bound conformations (Figure  a). This implies that the proline-to-alanine mutations increase flexibility, presumably due to the increased degree of freedom in the dihedral angles. To quantitatively analyze the mutational effects, we examined the contact maps (Figure  d and Figure S4) and their differences compared to the WT map (Figure  e). All three mutants display similar behaviors, and here, we use the P8A mutant as an example. Apparently, the proline-to-alanine mutation significantly alters the interaction mode. One of the most notable changes is that Thr9, which is next to the mutated residue, begins to form new strong contacts in three core binding regions of SH3. This can also be attributed to the increased degree of freedom in the dihedral space.

  We next investigated the nature of the protein–peptide interface. Previous studies have suggested that the hydrophobic effect can play a significant role in determining binding behavior. −   To assess the extent of the hydrophobic contribution, we calculated the G sol of the peptides in bound states. As shown in Figure  , the PRM WT exhibited G sol comparable to that of the proline-to-alanine mutants. Despite this, PRM WT binds more strongly to the SH3 domain than the mutants. Note that PRM +/0, the most hydrophobic of the protein–peptide complexes that we studied (Figure S5), displays an unmeasurably weak binding affinity in the experiment. These findings suggest that electrostatic interactions are the primary driving force behind the binding behavior in the PRM series rather than hydrophobic interactions.


### PRM­(H) Systems
  We extended our analysis to PRM­(H) systems. The binding affinities of PRM­(H) WT and its proline-to-alanine mutants were experimentally determined using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) and were found to be notably stronger than those of the PRM WT motifs. Unlike in the PRM systems, the strong binding of the peptide to SH3 in PRM­(H) does not diminish even after proline-to-alanine mutations. Figure  a–d shows the binding structures of PRM­(H)-based protein–peptide complexes. In all complexes, the peptides are firmly bound to the protein, adopting dumbbell-like shapes; the middle of the peptide is strongly bound and the termini fluctuate. Importantly, the contact mode, dispersion, and distance map of PRM­(H) change marginally upon the mutations (Figures S4, S6, and S7).

  A possible explanation for the stronger binding affinity of PRM­(H) compared with PRM is the hydrophobic effect, which was not the primary driving force in the PRM series. The PRM­(H) systems generally exhibit less favorable G sol than the PRM systems (Figure S5). This reduction in the energetic cost implies enhanced hydrophobicity. The ratio of charged residues to hydrophobic residues differs significantly between the PRM and the PRM­(H) systems. For example, in PRM WT, the charged residues make up approximately 36%, while hydrophobic residues account for 29%. In contrast, PRM­(H) WT has 27% charged residues and 47% hydrophobic residues. This suggests that hydrophobic interactions play a more prominent role in protein–peptide interactions in PRM­(H), potentially surpassing electrostatic interactions as the dominant driving force.

  However, even when considering hydrophobic interactions, it remains challenging to explain why the P9A and P7AP9A mutants of PRM­(H) exhibit significantly weaker interactions than WT and P7A. The hydrophobic interactions are similar across all variants of PRM­(H), as indicated by G sol (Figure   and Figure S5). We observed that the intrapeptide interaction energies correlate with the binding affinities of the PRM­(H) series (Figure  e). To investigate the structural origin of the weaker intrapeptide interaction energies, we analyzed the intrapeptide contact maps and distance maps. Figure  f presents the intrapeptide distance map of PRM­(H) WT in the bound state, while the differences in the intrapeptide distance maps between the bound states of PRM­(H) mutants and PRM­(H) WT are shown in Figure  g–i. The mutated residue shows significant distancing from the protein surface, which implies that the mutation itself reduces the interaction between the proline residue and SH3. However, this is not the end of the story, as the mutational effect is not an additive (Figure  e).

  It is generally observed that upon each proline-to-alanine mutation, the N-terminus and C-terminus make more frequent intrapeptide contacts as the structural constraint because the imine nature of proline has been relaxed. If this intrapeptide interaction is significant, then it will reduce the binding affinity between the peptide and SH3. We found that the position of the mutation determines the efficiency of the intrapeptide interaction. The C-terminus (residues 13–15) makes more global contacts with the other part of the peptide in the P9A system than in the P7A system. As the “stapling” position determines the ratio between the N-terminal and C-terminal halves, we suggest that the split that P9A makes is more effective in inducing transient interactions between the N-terminus and C-terminus, which indicates a reduction of the competing interaction, i.e., the protein–peptide interaction.

  Collectively, in the PRM­(H) series, the proline-to-alanine mutations play two roles: (i) it reduces the interaction between the mutated residue and the protein surface (direct effect), and (ii) the increased flexibility enhances transient intrapeptide interactions, which compete with the peptide–protein interaction (indirect effect). The mutational position determines the mode and extent of intrapeptide interactions, hence leading to the position dependence of the mutational effect. In the case of the double mutant P7AP9A, which experimentally showed similar binding affinity with P9A, although the direct effect is doubled, the intrapeptide interactions are not as efficient as P9A (Figure  i) and presumably compensate for the increase in the direct effect.


### Comparison of RMSD and Dissimilarity
  Based on our analysis of the mutation effects, we found that the binding mode of PRM to SH3 involves both strong binding and transient binding and that the subtle interplay of the two interactions shapes the observed binding affinity. We hypothesized that fluctuations play a crucial role in determining the binding affinities of systems with similar binding modes (Figure  a). As a global measure of fluctuations, we calculated the average RMSD values across all variants used in this study (Figure  b and Figure S8). When the structures were aligned with respect to SH3, the average RMSD values generally followed the trend of experimentally determined dissociation constants, implying that weaker binding is associated with increased fluctuations.

  Next, we divided the structural ensemble of the protein–peptide complexes obtained from our MD simulations into two groups using hierarchical clustering based on pairwise RMSD values. We generated the hierarchical structure using dendrograms generated by the Ward method. The highest linkage height, which represents the dissimilarity between two clusters, is shown in Figure  c. Interestingly, the height, which reflects the dissimilarity between clusters, generally followed a trend similar to that of the RMSD with respect to SH3. This result suggests that when protein–peptide complexes are clustered into two structurally distinct groups, their dissimilarity, which is a consequence of collective fluctuations, is a good estimator of the binding affinity.

  As fluctuations are related to the transient interactions, it should be noted that our measures cannot explain the cases involving a change in strong interactions. For example, PRM WT and PRM +/0 have different natures of interactions; the former is dominated by electrostatic interactions, but the latter lacks the positive charges. In this case, the nature of the N-terminal-SH3 interaction has totally changed, and our measures are incapable of detecting this change. A more delicate case is the PRM­(H) series. As we discussed above, their interactions are an intricate combination of strong interactions and transient interactions, and it is not easy to disentangle one from the other. Thus, it is unsurprising that our RMSD and dissimilarity parameters do not reproduce the binding affinities of the PRM­(H) series.


## Conclusions
  Using AlphaFold2 and molecular dynamics simulations, we discovered that proline-to-alanine mutations enhance flexibility, leading to a reduction in binding affinity, while charge-altering mutations modify both the binding mode and strength in PRM:SH3 systems. The PRM­(H) series demonstrated that binding is primarily driven by local flexibility and the hydrophobic effect. Additionally, we found a correlation between the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and dendrogram height with experimental dissociation constants. These findings provide important insights into the binding mechanisms of protein–peptide complexes, highlighting key factors such as flexibility, electrostatic interactions, the hydrophobic effect, and intrapeptide interactions. Consequently, we anticipate that our work offers a deeper understanding of the chemical interactions governing protein–peptide binding and provides a robust framework for studying similar systems.
