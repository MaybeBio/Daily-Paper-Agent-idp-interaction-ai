# Noncovalent Lasso Entanglements are Common in Experimentally Derived Intrinsically Disordered Protein Ensembles and Strongly Influenced by Protein Length and Charge


## Abstract
  Noncovalent lasso entanglements are conformations in which a protein backbone segment forms a loop closed by noncovalent interactions and that loop is threaded one or more times by either the N- or C-terminal segment of the backbone or both. While these entanglements are common in globular proteins, their presence in intrinsically disordered proteins or regions (IDPs/IDRs) remains largely unexplored. Here, we examine whether IDPs/IDRs in their monomeric form populate these conformations and how sequence length and charge composition influence entanglement prevalence. Using experimentally derived IDP/IDR ensembles from the Protein Ensemble Database, we find that 48% (199 of 416) of its entries contain subpopulations with entangled conformations, with 25% of entries having conformational ensembles in which 50% or more are entangled. This includes IDPs such as nuclear pore complex protein Nup153, nonstructural protein V of Hendra virus, and Eukaryotic initiation factor 4F subunit p150. Using molecular simulations, we find that (i) entanglements are most prevalent in weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes, and strong polyampholytes but rare in strong polyelectrolytes; (ii) entanglement populations increase with IDP length; (iii) entanglement probability positively correlates with chain compaction; and (iv) most IDPs/IDRs in the human proteome exhibit entangled conformations. A GO enrichment analysis reveals that the entanglement probability correlates with IDP/IDR function and subcellular localization. Thus, these findings indicate that noncovalent lasso entanglements are a widespread structural feature of IDPs/IDRs and have the potential to be biologically relevant.


## INTRODUCTION
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) are widespread in all organisms1,2 and play crucial roles in various biological processes, including signal transduction,3-5 molecular recognition,6-9 and phase separation.10,11 Unlike structured proteins and domains, IDPs/IDRs do not adopt a single, well-defined three-dimensional structure.12 Instead, they exist as dynamic ensembles, sampling a broader conformational landscape. This structural flexibility allows them to interact with multiple partners13 and perform diverse functions, but it also introduces challenges in understanding their physical and functional properties.

  In 2017, a new class of topological entanglements, termed noncovalent lasso entanglements, was identified to be present in 49 to 71% of domains of globular proteins,14-18 depending on the organism.14 These structures arise when a protein backbone forms a loop closed by noncovalent interactions between two residues, with one or more segments of the backbone threading through the loop (Figure 1a,b). Unknown is how widespread this class of entanglements is in IDPs/IDRs and what, if any, functional significance they may have.

  Polymer theory would suggest that IDPs/IDRs can sample such entanglements;19-22 however, their population should be influenced by the length of the protein and the persistence length of backbone segments, which, in turn, is dependent on the protein’s sequence and interactions. Due to this complexity, it is difficult to anticipate a priori the extent to which noncovalent lasso entanglements are populated in IDP/IDR ensembles and how this depends on such sequence properties.

  Here, we systematically investigate the prevalence and characteristics of noncovalent lasso entanglements in IDPs/IDRs using both experimentally derived conformational ensembles and coarse-grained molecular simulations. First, we analyze experimentally derived IDP/IDR structures from the Protein Ensemble Database23,24 (PED) to quantify the occurrence of these entanglements in real biological systems. Second, we employ coarse-grained simulations to examine how the sequence length and charge content influence the probability of self-entanglement. Finally, using simulation data of IDPs/IDRs across the human proteome, we assess how widespread entangled states are in human IDPs/IDRs and explore their potential functional implications.

  By integrating experimental and computational approaches, we provide the first comprehensive examination of non-covalent lasso entanglements in IDPs/IDRs. Our findings indicate that these entanglements are common in IDP/IDR ensembles and depend sensitively on the sequence charge composition and length. We suggest that these entanglements may have important implications for IDP/IDR function, stability, and interactions.


## METHODS

### Selection of IDP Ensembles from the PED Database.
  To determine whether noncovalent lasso entanglements are populated by IDPs/IDRs, we applied a detection algorithm for these conformations to the Protein Ensemble Database,23,24 which contains 461 experimentally derived structural entries for 299 proteins (as of September 14th 2023). Many of these ensembles were informed by NMR, SAXS, and FRET data. In the PED database, an entry may consist of a single protein or a complex of multiple proteins. For our purposes, we are specifically interested in individual protein self-entanglement. Therefore, we treat entries with multiple chains as separate entries, where each chain is considered a single entry that we rename and organize as entry_chain format, e.g., entry PED00002 contains two heat shock protein β-6 chains A and B, the separated entries named PED00002_A and PED00002_B. Additionally, we only focus on entries that are experimentally derived. We further exclude any entries shorter than 20 residues or entries that are classified as “fully structured” according to PED as these correspond to ordered proteins that lack IDRs.

  This preprocessing step yielded a total of 416 entries of interest. We categorize the remaining database into three categories: fully IDP (77 entries), long IDR (191 entries), and short IDR (148 entries), which we define below. The sequences in PED were cross-referenced to the disorder information obtained from MobiDB,25 which combines manually curated, homology-derived, and predicted data from the literature. Due to the limited curated data from MobiDB, we combine multiple sources of disorder information from this database; specifically, the following categories will be used: curated-disorder-priority, homology-disorder-priority, derived-missing_residues-priority, prediction-disorder-priority, and derived-mobile-th_90. For the IDP classification, we applied a rule proposed by Necci et al., which says proteins are classified based on the number of consecutive disordered residues:26 short IDR is defined as protein with 5–19 consecutive disordered residues. Long IDR is defined as protein with ≥20 consecutive disordered residues. And fully IDPs are those with more than 50 consecutive disordered residues, and the disordered regions account for more than 95% of the sequence length.

  Proteins were considered to be fully structured if there are no disorder regions or only contain regions that are shorter than 5 disordered residues. If MobiDB does not have disorder information on a certain protein, we visualized the structural ensemble and manually assigned the above categories.


### Entanglement Analysis.
  We used the Gaussian double integral27 to determine the linking number in a protein conformation, which is the linking number between a closed loop and threading segment.

  For a given protein structure, with a contact present at residues (i1, i2), the coordinate Rl and the gradient dRl of the point l on the curves were calculated as  (1) {Rl=12(rl+rl+1)dRl=rl+1−rl}  where rl are the coordinates of the Cα atom in the residue l.

  For a loop γi closed by contact between residues i1 and i2, we calculate the linking number between a closed loop γi and the subchain γj (where γj is the other segment of protein range from residue j1 and j2 and γi∩γj=∅), and their Gaussian entanglement is given by  (2) Gij=14π∑i=i1i2∑j=j1j2Ri−Rj∣Ri−Rj∣3⋅(dRi×dRj)

  A mutual Gaussian entanglement number in a conformation is defined as the largest absolute value of Gij found for all possible pairs (γi,γj)  (3) maxG=max[i1,i2],[j1,j2]∣Gij∣

  A conformation is said to be entangled if maxG≥0.6. The selection of the maxG=0.6 threshold is based on a thorough manual examination of a substantial number of conformations.28 In our analysis, we require that the loop and threading segments must be longer than 10 residues.

  For an entangled conformation, the entangled region is defined as the portion of the structure comprising the loop (i1 to i2) and threading (j1 to j2) segments, which gives the maxG in structure.


### Coarse-Grained Simulations of Synthetic Polymers.
  To investigate the influence of the charge content on the population of entanglements in IDPs, we generated synthetic sequences with controlled charge compositions. Synthetic sequences were generated for three sets of sequence lengths of 100, 200, and 300 residues. For each set, these sequences vary in the fraction of positively charged residues (arginine and lysine), negatively charged residues (aspartic and glutamic acids), and neutral residues (all other canonical amino acids).

  The residue composition for each charge group was derived from the DisProt database.29,30 For positively charged residues, the proportion of R and K was fixed at 38 and 62%, respectively. Similarly, for negatively charged residues, the proportion of D and E was fixed at 37 and 63%, respectively. Neutral residues were distributed across the remaining 16 amino acid types with probabilities reflecting their natural occurrence in disordered proteins.29,30 These probabilities were normalized to sum to one. For each sequence length, charge fractions were systematically varied using two independent variables: the fraction of positively charged residues (fP) and negatively charged residues (fN), with an increment of 0.05. Neutral residue fractions (f0) were computed as f0=1−(fP+fN). Sequences were constructed such that the number of residues in each charge group matched their respective fractions and residues were randomly distributed within the sequence. This procedure generated a total of 21 × (21 + 1)/2 = 231 sequences for each sequence length. As an example, consider a sequence length of N = 200 residue with fP=0.5 and fN=0.5. The number of positively charged residues is NP=0.5×200=100, with arginine and lysine counts as NR=0.38×100=38 and NK=0.62×100=62, respectively. Similarly, the number of negatively charged residues is NN=0.5×200=100, with aspartic acid and glutamic acid as ND=0.37×100=37 and NE=0.63×100=63 residues, respectively. In this example, the fraction of neutral residues was zero (f0=1−0.5−0.5).

  Molecular dynamics simulations were performed in the canonical (NVT) ensemble at a constant temperature of 298 K, maintained by using Langevin dynamics for temperature coupling. An integration time step of 10 fs was employed to ensure the numerical stability and accuracy. Proteins were modeled using the Urry hydropathy scale,31 employing a one-bead-per-residue representation to capture residue-specific hydrophobic properties. Simulations were conducted using OpenMM32,33 (version 8.0). Each simulation trajectory was 3 μs of simulation time, with the first 1 μs discarded to allow for equilibration and the subsequent 2 μs used for analysis.


### IDPs/IDRs in Human Proteome Analysis.
  The data on IDPs/IDRs in human proteome was obtained from Tesei et al. using pLDDT criteria for selection.34 In our analysis, we exclude 435 sequences that exceeded our computational limits (requiring more than 6 days of wall time to calculate Gaussian Linking Numbers for 1000 frames of a trajectory). The final data set comprises 27,623 sequences from 15,297 unique proteins, including 1413 full-length IDPs and 26,210 IDR regions from 13,884 unique proteins, which were used for entanglement analysis and Gene Ontology analysis.


### Gene Ontology Analysis.
  Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted following the approach of Tesei et al.34 with modifications. GO analysis was performed on our data set of IDPs/IDRs, categorizing them under Cellular Component, Molecular Function, and Biological Process. GO annotations for each UniProt entry in our data set were retrieved from UniProtKB35 (January 24th, 2025). The GO terms assigned to full-length proteins were inherited from their respective IDR segments. We filtered and retained only those GO terms associated with at least 200 IDPs/IDRs, yielding 96, 94, and 113 GO terms for the Cellular Component, Molecular Function, and Biological Process, respectively.

  For each GO term, we performed a Brunner–Munzel rank-order test36,37 to assess whether the entanglement probabilities of the corresponding IDPs/IDRs were significantly higher or lower compared to IDPs/IDRs not associated with that GO term. To control for false discovery rate, adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction,38,39 separately for Cellular Component, Molecular Function, and Biological Process categories. A GO term was considered significantly enriched for high or low entanglement probabilities if its adjusted p-value was below 0.05.

  To estimate the magnitude of differences in entanglement probabilities between two groups, we calculated Cliff′s δ effect size40 (suitable for the Brunner–Munzel nonparametric test) as  (4) Cliff′sδ=N+−N−n1n2  where N+ represents the number of times a value in group 1 exceeds a value in group 2, and N− represents the number of times a value in group 1 is less than a value in group 2. n1 and n2 are the sample sizes of groups 1 and 2, respectively.


## RESULTS

### Entanglements are Common in Experimental IDP Structural Ensembles.
  To measure whether noncovalent lasso entanglements are populated by IDPs/IDRs we applied the Gaussian double integral detection algorithm to the IDP/IDR conformations reported in the Protein Ensemble Database.23 Specifically, our analysis only focused on ensembles generated through experimental techniques, such as NMR, SAXS, and FRET, and excluded computational prediction ensembles. Applying filtering criteria (see Methods section), we obtained 416 entries, corresponding to 248 unique proteins, in our final data set (Table 1). These entries can be further categorized: 77 were fully IDP, 191 were long IDR, and 148 were short IDR class, where fully IDP is defined as proteins with more than 50 consecutive disordered residues and the disordered regions account for more than 95% of the protein’s primary structure; long IDR is defined as proteins having a region with more than 20 consecutive disordered residues; and short IDR is defined as a protein with 5 to 19 consecutive disordered residues along the primary structure.

  For each IDP/IDR structural ensemble, we compute the fraction of conformations that contain an entanglement and refer to this as the entanglement population. We found that entanglements are a widespread feature of experimental IDP/IDR ensembles. Specifically, 199 out of the 416 entries populate entangled conformations (i.e., at least one conformation in the ensemble has an entanglement), constituting 48% of all entries (Table 1 and SI DATA 1). A subanalysis based on the IDP categories reveals that entanglements are present in 52, 58, and 32%, respectively, of the fully IDP, long IDR, and short IDR categories. We next asked which percentage of entries have entanglements present in at least 50% of their ensembles of reported structures. We find that 9, 33, and 24% of entries meet this condition, respectively, for fully IDP, long IDRs, and short IDRs.

  Since proteins containing IDRs also have ordered, globular domains, the previous analysis could be confounded by entanglements present only in the ordered domains and not the IDR segments. To address this, we next asked if it is the disordered region taking part in the entanglement or the ordered, globular portion of the protein. By defining the entangled region as the portion of the structure comprising the loop and threading segments that gives maxG (see Methods section), we determined whether entanglements involve intrinsically disordered or structured regions. (By definition, the Fully IDP entries do not have ordered domains and therefore any of their entanglements must be arising from the disordered segments.) We find that in long IDR entries with entanglements, 93.7% (104 out of 111 entries) have conformations in which the disordered region takes part in constituting the entanglement. For short IDR entries with entanglements, 81.3% (39 out of 48) have conformations in which the disordered region takes part. We conclude that for those IDPs and IDRs with entanglements, the vast majority have noncovalent lasso entanglements whose components are composed of disordered regions.


### Charge Content Influences the Population of Entanglements.
  The global compaction of IDPs/IDRs (often characterized by the ensemble averaged radius of gyration, Rg) is modulated by the fraction-of-charge residues (FCR=fP+fN, where, fP, fN are fraction of positively and negatively charged residues, respectively) and net-charge-per-residue41-43 (NCPR=∣fP−fN∣). To systematically investigate how charge composition affects entanglement formation, we carried out coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations on synthetic IDPs of fixed length (200 residues). We generate 231 synthetic sequences that are 200 residues in length and preserve the amino acid usage frequency observed in natural IDPs29 but vary in their charge content (see Methods section). We then ran simulations at 298 K for each of the synthetic IDPs for 3 μs, excluding the first 1 μs to allow for equilibration, and computed their population of entangled conformations. As is common in the IDP field, we then compiled and displayed this information as a phase diagram as a function of the fraction of positively and negatively charged residues (Figure 2). Superimposing these heatmap with the state diagram derived by Das et al.,42,44 which consists of five regions numbered I through V, we observe that the probability of an IDP being entangled is most probable when the fraction of positive and negative charges is nearly similar (i.e., corresponding to polymers in Weak Polyampholytes and Polyelectrolytes (region I) or Strong Polyampholytes (region III) regions and the boundary separating these regimes (region II) in Figure 2a). For IDPs in the Strong Polyelectrolyte regime (IV and V), in which the sequence is highly charged and dominated by one charge type (positive or negative), the probability of being entangled goes to zero. Comparing the entanglement probability (Figure 2a) to their radius of gyration (Figure 2b), we found that the more compact polymer, the higher the probability that polymer will populate entangled conformations. This observation is expected since lasso entanglements require a loop to be closed by noncovalent interactions; greater compaction leads to a higher probability of forming those contacts and selfentangling. Thus, the charge content strongly influences the entanglement probability of polymers.


### Protein Length Influences the Population of Entanglements.
  Given that longer polymer chains have greater flexibility and a higher probability of forming self-interactions, we next asked if protein length influences the probability of entanglement, which we expect to be the case, since longer polymers can more easily bend back on themselves and bring backbone segments closer together in space and increase the chance of them intertwining. To test this, we performed the simulations of two additional sets of synthetic proteins that were either 100 or 300 residues in length. Our results (Figure 3) confirm that protein length significantly impacts the probability of entanglement formation. Overall, we observed a consistent trend: entanglements preferentially populate specific charge-phase regions (region I–III, weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes and strong polyampholytes, and in the boundary separating them), while remaining absent in strong polyelectrolyte regions (IV and V). However, as sequence length increases, the overall entanglement probability increases. This effect is particularly pronounced in IDPs that fall within weak polyampholyte and boundary-phase regions, where the likelihood of entanglement increases substantially with increasing length. Specifically, shorter sequences (N = 100) exhibit a relatively low entanglement probability (Figure 3a), likely due to limited chain flexibility and steric constraints that prevent effective loop formation and threading. The longest sequences (N = 300) show the highest frequency of entanglements (Figure 3c). Longer chains have more degrees of freedom, allowing more intrachain contacts that can close the loops required for entanglement; and longer IDPs allow segments of the protein to have more opportunities to thread through those loops, leading to an increased likelihood of lasso-like topologies. These results indicate that protein length is a critical factor influencing entanglement formation.


### Estimating How Widespread this Phenomenon is in the Human Proteome.
  To estimate the fraction of IDPs/IDRs in the human proteome likely to exhibit subpopulations of entangled states, we reanalyzed coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulation trajectories of all human IDPs/IDRs, as published by Tesei et al.34 This data set includes simulations of 28,058 sequences, with lengths ranging from 30 to 1500 residues, derived from 15,424 unique proteins. This data set allows us to comprehensively examine the influence of sequence properties on entanglement probability in IDPs/IDRs of the human proteome. We excluded 435 sequences exceeded our computational limits (requiring more than 6 days to calculate Gaussian entanglements), this resulted in a final data set of 27,623 sequences from 15,297 unique proteins for entanglement analysis.

  Within this data set, we found that 23,530 out of 27,623 of IDPs/IDRs (85.18%) populate at least one entangled conformation during their simulated trajectories, and only 4093 sequences (14.82%) do not populate any entanglement conformation in their ensemble trajectories (Figure 4a). And 3.58% of the IDPs/IDRs (989 out of 27,623 sequences) populate entangled states for at least half their simulation trajectory. This high prevalence of entanglements among IDP/IDR sequences can be attributed to the distribution of IDPs/IDRs across the phase diagram. Specifically, IDPs/IDRs occupying weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes (region I), and strong polyampholytes (region III), as well as the boundary between these regions (region II), collectively account for 99.51% of the data set. This means they contain sequences with a balanced fraction of positively and negatively charged residues, allowing for many transient intrachain interactions that facilitate loop closure and threading events leading to high probabilities of entanglement (SI DATA 2). These findings indicate that entanglements are present in most human IDPs/IDRs. And again confirms that charge content plays a critical role in determining entanglement propensity, with balanced charge fractions favoring compacted and self-entangled conformations.

  We next investigate the correlation between entanglement and chain compaction, as characterized by the apparent Flory scaling exponent ν, a metric describing chain expansion in polymer physics,45,46 by plotting the entanglement probability as the function of ν (Figure 4b,c). A lower ν value indicates a more compact ensemble, while a higher ν value suggests an expanded conformation. Our analysis shows a strong correlation between entanglement probability and chain compaction, with highly entangled sequences exhibiting smaller ν (Figure 4b,c). Taken together, these results demonstrate noncovalent lasso entanglements are a common feature of IDPs/IDRs in the human proteome. The widespread presence of these entangled conformations opens the possibility that entanglements could play a role in modulating molecular recognition, interaction specificity, and phase separation behaviors in disordered proteins and regions.


### Gene Ontology Analysis Reveals Functional Enrichment of Entangled IDPs/IDRs.
  Given the observed correlation between entanglement probability and IDP/IDR compaction, which, in turn, plays a role in protein function,47-49 we asked whether certain functional categories of IDPs/IDRs are enriched in highly or lowly entangled populations. We performed a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis to examine whether specific cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes are associated with IDPs/IDRs exhibiting high entanglement probabilities. To do this, we retrieved GO terms associated with each full-length protein in our data set from UniProtKB35 and focused on terms with at least 200 associated proteins to ensure statistical robustness. We applied the Brunner–Munzel rank-order test36,37 to assess whether entanglement probability of proteins belonging to a given GO term is stochastically larger or smaller than the entanglement probability of proteins that are not associated with that GO term. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction,38,39 with a significance threshold of 0.05.

  Overall, the Cliff′s δ effect size (eq 4, Methods) is small (∣Cliff′sδ∣<0.33). Nevertheless, we identified 61, 42, and 56 GO terms related to cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes, respectively, that are significantly enriched in highly or lowly entangled populations relative to IDPs/IDRs not associated with these GO terms (Figure 5 and SI DATA 3). Our results show that IDPs/IDRs are localized in nucleus or involve in transcription activity are significantly enriched in higher entangled populations, suggesting that entangled conformations may influence molecular recognition and nucleic acid interactions. Conversely, IDPs/IDRs associated with cytoplasmic localization, G protein-coupled receptor activity or signaling pathway, and GTP binding tend to have lower entanglement probability. This suggests that proteins requiring extensive structural flexibility or long-range conformational changes may be under selective pressure to minimize entanglement, as it could impose constraints on their dynamics and functional interactions.


### Examples of Specific IDPs That Populate Entangled Conformations.
  Certain IDPs have been extensively studied due to their critical roles in cellular processes and their involvement in diseases such as neurodegeneration and cancer. We find that several widely studied IDPs exhibit a significant fraction of entangled conformations. For example, the nuclear pore complex protein Nup153 (PED00145, segment from residue 884–993) exhibits entangled conformations in 70% of its ensemble, suggesting that these topological features may contribute to its structural organization and functional interactions within the nuclear complex.50 Similarly, the nonstructural protein V of Hendra virus (PED00182), which is crucial for inhibiting the host immune response,51 displays entangled conformations in 77% of its ensemble. This suggests that entanglement may influence its interactions with host immune factors, potentially modulating its inhibitory function. Another example is the N-terminal domain of eukaryotic initiation factor 4F subunit p150 (PED00225), which interacts with the mRNA cap structure and serves as a key assembly point for the translation apparatus.52,53 In this case, 61% of its conformations are entangled, indicating a potential role in modulating interactions during translation initiation.

  The prevalence of entanglements in these well-characterized IDPs/IDRs suggests that this structural phenomenon may be more widespread and functionally relevant than previously recognized. Furthermore, the presence of noncovalent lasso entanglements imposes structural constraints due to their intertwined topology. We speculate that this could influence the IDP/IDR function and stability, potentially altering their interaction networks and aggregation propensities.


## DISCUSSION
  The compaction and structural organization of IDPs/IDRs have been extensively studied due to their strong correlation with function47-49,54 and interaction dynamics.2 For instance, charge segregation in the poorly conserved RAM disordered region of the Notch receptor, which mediates interactions with the transcription factor CSL, has been shown to influence the global dimensions of the RAM region. This, in turn, regulates binding affinity and transcriptional activity48 as the highly compact RAM region restricts its access to the CSL binding site. Another example is the cooperative binding of single-stranded DNA binding protein, which is disrupted if the C-terminal disordered tails are removed or altered.54 Instead of adopting a globular conformation, this tail forms coils or hairpin structures, impairing its positive cooperativity in single-stranded DNA binding. Our findings demonstrate that noncovalent lasso entanglements are a prevalent feature of IDP/IDR structural ensembles. In the Protein Ensemble Database, we found that nearly half of the examined IDPs/IDRs contain entangled conformations, and a significant portion of these proteins have ensembles in which more than 50% of conformations are entangled. These results suggest that entanglements, due to their topology, could impose structural constraints that affect IDP/IDR dynamics, interaction mechanisms, and functional properties.

  From our coarse-grained simulations, we identify two major determinants of entanglement probability, namely sequence length–longer IDPs/IDRs are more likely to populate entangled states due to increased chain flexibility and a higher likelihood of intrachain interactions; and charge composition–entanglements are most prevalent in weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes and strong polyampholytes, where positively and negatively charged residues are nearly balanced. In contrast, strongly charged polyelectrolytes, which remain more extended, rarely form entanglements. Since at least 75% of known IDPs are polyampholytes,30 this suggests that a major fraction of known IDPs will populate entangled conformations. Consistent with this, we observed that in the human IDP/IDR proteome simulation data set, 85% populate at least one entangled conformation. Overall, these observations align with previous studies on IDP compaction,42 where electrostatic interactions play a dominant role in determining conformational properties. The correlation between the entanglement probability and radius of gyration suggests that compaction promotes the formation of closed loop structures necessary for entanglement. This finding is particularly intriguing, because entangled states may contribute to functional specialization by modulating molecular recognition and interaction interfaces.

  Our Gene Ontology (GO) analysis suggests potential functional relevance of entanglements, revealing that IDPs/IDRs localized in the nucleus or involved in nucleus-related activity tend to have a higher prevalence of entangled conformations. This suggests the possibility that entanglements may influence molecular recognition, transcriptional regulation, and phase separation, which are key processes involving disordered proteins. On the other hand, IDPs/IDRs associated with cytoplasmic localization, or involved in G protein-coupled receptor activity and signaling pathway, show a lower propensity to populate entangled states, potentially due to functional constraints requiring a more extended and flexible conformation.

  Given that entanglements could affect self-assembly and aggregation kinetics, a potentially fruitful future research direction could be to investigate their potential role in amyloid formation, phase transitions, and biomolecular condensates.
