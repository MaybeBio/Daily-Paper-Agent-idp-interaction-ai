# Resolving the structure and dynamics of the disordered C terminus of human cardiac troponin T and effects of cardiomyopathic mutations


## Significance
  The C-terminal tail of cardiac troponin T (C-cTnT) is a hotspot for cardiomyopathic mutations and has been proposed to inhibit thin filament activation, however the mechanism by which it regulates activation is poorly understood. This is due, in part, to its inherently flexible and disordered nature as it remains structurally unresolved. To overcome this, we triangulated its position in biochemical conditions reflecting the three-state model of activation and examined its intermolecular interactions in a reconstituted cardiac thin filament. Disease-causing mutations perturbed the wildtype structure and altered intermolecular interactions in a manner that altered both its average position and flexibility. We suggest these changes alter the energy barrier required to shift tropomyosin from the blocked position, contributing to altered actomyosin interactions.


## Results
  To first triangulate the position of the flexible C-cTnT with respect to cTnC we performed TR-FRET experiments using time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC). This approach yielded discrete distances (angstroms, Å) and distance distributions (Å), as measured by the full width at half-maximum (FWHM). We selected cTnT residues V274C and V283C and labeled these sites with the donor probe IAEDANS. We selected cTnC residues T53C and G125C to represent the N- and C-lobes respectively and labeled them with the acceptor probe DABMI. In total, we used four independent probe pairs (see schematic in SI Appendix, Fig. S1), 1) cTnT-V274C with cTnC-T53C, 2) cTnT-V274C with cTnC-G125C, 3) cTnT-V283C with cTnC-T53C, and 4) cTnT-V283C with cTnC-G125C. Experiments were performed in fully reconstituted WT or mutant cardiac thin filaments under different biochemical conditions (−Ca/−S1, +Ca/−S1, or +Ca/+S1) to mimic the blocked, closed, and open states of thin filament activation respectively. An NADH-coupled myosin activity assay was performed to assess for the expected calcium-based regulation of cysteine substitutions, with probes bound (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We found no differences in myosin activity when cysteine-substituted proteins were bound with our FRET probes, suggesting the presence of bound probes do not contribute to the changes seen in our FRET data.


### Structure and Dynamics of WT C-cTnT in Relation to cTnC.
  A graphical representation of mean distances and distributions of the WT cTF data is shown in Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2 include a summary of the numerical mean ± SEM for WT and D270N or WT and K273E respectively. Last, a graphical representation of the individual data points and statistical analysis and comparisons can be found in Figs. 2 and 3 for WT and D270N or WT and K273E respectively.

  We found that cTnT-274C was closer to the N-lobe (T53C) than the C-lobe (G125C) of cTnC in the absence of calcium (45.5 ± 0.9 Å and 52.8 ± 1.5 Å) and this was similar for cTnT-283C (49.3 ± 1.0 Å and 59.0 ± 2.9 Å) (Fig. 1). This also demonstrated that cTnT-274C is closer to the troponin core, specifically cTnC, than cTnT-283C. We measured a FWHM of 34.5 ± 1.3 Å and 36.7 ± 4.4 Å from cTnT-274C to the N- and C-lobes respectively in the absence of calcium. The FWHM was similar for cTnT-283C (29.1 ± 2.2 Å and 26.2 ± 9.5 Å to the N- and C-lobes respectively). Taken together, this demonstrated the full length of the C-cTnT is inherently flexible and disordered in the absence of calcium.

  We note that the structures presented in Fig. 1 are average structures, but may be somewhat misleading because they show a fixed location for the flexible region. To do this we perform a dimensional reduction on the RMSD of the frames of the MD simulation. To analyze the range of possible structures, we first calculated the RMSD (17, 18) between all frames for C-cTnT found in our 30 ns MD simulation data. In order to ensure that RMSD comes from true dynamic variation for clustering (and not simply orientation differences of the same static structure) each pair of frames were overlapped to minimize difference. Next, we applied the metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) (19–21) method in order to generate a low-dimensional representation of the 2D RMSD data. This method is related to the more standard PCA method, but it ensures that the distances between the output points are as close as possible to the input data, and this eliminates many of the artifacts found in standard PCA. We project the data on 2 dominant MDS components and use a density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) (22–24) method with four different parameter settings and selected one of them that provided the best clustering results. Finally, we chose the frame closest to the centroid of each cluster as the representative structure for that cluster. The representative conformations of each cluster are shown in Fig. 4. We have included such analysis for all states and mutations in SI Appendix, Figs. S18–S25. The purpose of this is to find the dominant locations of the less structured region in addition to the average structure. We also calculate RMS fluctuation (RMSF) for residues in cTnC and cTnT, which are presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S17, to indicate the contribution of motion of the individual proteins involved in the FRET measurement. Panel D of Fig. 4 shows how all structural clusters found for the calcium-free results fit in the density reported by Risi et al. (25). SI Appendix, Figs. S26–S28 show all our cluster results for both calcium saturated and depleted states.

  In calcium saturated thin filaments, the distance from cTnT-274C to the N-lobe was unchanged, while the distance to the C-lobe decreased by 5.7 Å, demonstrating calcium binding leads cTnT-274C to move closer to the C-lobe. This differed at cTnT-283C which was not affected by calcium as distances were not different for either the N- or C-lobes. Similar trends were found regarding the FWHM as calcium had no effects on the flexibility of either cTnT-274C or 283C. In the aggregate this suggests that calcium has minimal effects on C-cTnT structure and flexibility.

  In cardiac thin filaments (cTFs) with both saturating calcium and nucleotide free myosin S1, the distance between cTnT-274C and the N-lobe decreased 7 Å (relative to calcium free), but did not affect the distance to the C-lobe. For cTnT-283C, myosin had no effect on distances to the N- and C-lobes. However, myosin dramatically decreased the FWHM of cTnT-274C to both N- and C-lobes of cTnC (15.4 ± 2.2 Å and 8.3 ± 1.9 Å respectively) without any effects on cTnT-283C to either the N- or C-lobes. This suggests myosin binding stabilizes C-cTnT near 274C, decreasing flexibility, likely through increased stable intermolecular interactions. Thus, WT C-cTnT undergoes a disordered to ordered transition as the thin filament transitions from the blocked to the open state, near the IT arm (cTnT-274C), while the terminal tail (cTnT-283C) stays disordered and flexible regardless of biochemical state.

  The inherent assumption in all of these measurements is that cTnC presents a fixed location for measure, and that is not necessarily true. Recent experiments have found multiple configurations of cTnC in cryo-EM data (25, 26). These must, in fact, be slowly interconverting states or the individual densities would not be visible in the cryo-EM images. To investigate how important such contributions might be to the distance measurements, we used long classical MD runs on the wildtype protein. For example, studying how cTnC T53C (one labeled location) moves relative to a fixed location on actin 21, we find this distance has a mean of about 60 and varies at most 5 Å on either side. So, this is about a 10% variation. When the same computation is done for G125 (not on a loop) we see motion of the residue relative to the assumed fixed actin no more than 2 Å. Details of these computations are presented in SI Appendix, Figs. S40–S42.


### Atomic Resolution of WT C-cTnT.
  A summary of interaction frequency in the WT cTF is shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S5–S7.

  Measured TR-FRET distances were used to perform constrained molecular dynamics to inform our computational model with experimental data. After integration of the FRET distances, average structures from three, 10 ns production runs of the C-cTnT for the blocked, closed, and open states were obtained and the results are summarized in SI Appendix, Figs. S14–S16. Using these production runs, we examined the intermolecular interactions of WT C-cTnT. These studies revealed direct interactions between C-cTnT (residues 270 to 288) with other thin filament proteins including cTnI, cTnC, actin, tropomyosin, and myosin when present. These interactions are based on the percentage of time the residues interact within the production run (SI Appendix, Figs. S5–S13).

  These coupled computational—in-vitro studies, in the absence of calcium, identified frequent interactions between C-cTnT with both strands of the nearby tropomyosin dimer (denoted TMCA and TMCB). Interestingly, the region closer to the IT-arm (residues 272 to 276) interacts with TMCA while residues more distal from the IT-arm (residues 280 to 288) interact with TMCB (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Interactions with actin were primarily with one monomer (denoted actin 21) as an adjacent monomer (actin 23) included one interaction present for about 0.2% of the trajectory. While interactions were observed between cTnT and cTnC, these interactions were only present for ≤5% of the trajectory, suggesting these interactions are likely weak or transient (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Interactions with cTnI were minimal and included only two interactions that were present for ≥10% of the trajectory (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

  In calcium saturated filaments, the cTnT-cTnC interactions become more frequent, with multiple residues interacting for ≥50% of the trajectory, specifically cTnT residues 287 and 288 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), myosin further alters the C-cTnT:cTnC interface where only cTnT-D270 interacts with cTnC (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Interactions with tropomyosin are also dynamic, in the calcium bound state interactions with TMCB are completely lost, and when myosin bound only one interaction was observed (cTnT288-TMCB194) (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7). Of note however, Tm-E192 (a known mutation site, Tm-E192K) interacts frequently with cTnT-K280 (≥70% of the trajectory) in the calcium bound state. Upon myosin binding, Tm-E192 interacts with both cTnT-K280 and R278 (a cTnT mutational hotspot, cTnT-R278P/C). Taken together, these data suggest that these interactions with Tm-E192 could significantly impact thin filament activation and consequently, disruption of these interactions via Tm-E192K or cTnT-R278P/C could lead to dysregulation and contribute to pathogenic remodeling at the whole-heart level.

  The terminal end of C-cTnT interacts with the nearby myosin head (SI Appendix, Fig. S7, Panel G). As these interactions occur primarily with cTnT residues 282 to 288, they likely do not contribute to the increased stability seen at cTnT-274. However, increased interactions with cTnC, cTnI, and actin are likely the driving force for increased stability in the open state (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These dynamic interactions likely represent the transition from the less stable, blocked and closed states to the more stable, open state. These data provide a base from which specific interactions or residues can be interrogated to determine the significance of specific intermolecular interactions as well as how disease-causing mutations alter these interactions and thus the inherently dynamic structure–function relationship.


### Structural and Dynamic Effects of DCM-Associated cTnT-D270N.
  A summary of average distances and FWHM (distributions) in the cTnT-D270N cTF is shown in Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1 and a representation of the Gaussian distributions and average distance for each biochemical condition is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S3.

  In the absence of calcium, the presence of D270N moved cTnT-274C (12.3 Å) and 283C (14.2 Å) closer to the C-lobe of cTnC with no effect relative to the N-lobe. D270N also decreased the FWHM near the IT arm (274C) relative to N- and C-lobes of cTnC by 4.5 Å and 17.1 Å respectively, while not affecting cTnT-V283C. Thus, D270N repositions C-cTnT closer to the cTnC C-lobe, stabilizing C-cTnT near the IT arm and remaining flexible further away (283C) compared to WT, suggesting D270N alters C-cTnT interactions.

  Compared to D270N calcium free thin filaments, the only calcium effect observed was a 4.8 Å increase of the FWHM between cTnT-274C and cTnC-53C, suggesting a slight increase in flexibility. However, as compared to WT calcium saturated thin filaments, D270N moved cTnT-274C 6.5 Å away from cTnC N-lobe and 6.9 Å closer to cTnC C-lobe, while cTnT-283C also moved closer to C-lobe by 7.8 Å. Thus, we observed similar trends of D270N moving C-cTnT closer to the C-lobe relative to WT.

  In myosin-bound cTFs, D270N resulted in cTnT-274C moving 5.7 Å closer to the C-lobe while cTnT-283C moved 15.7 Å closer to the C-lobe relative to WT. Of note, D270N increased the FWHM of C-cTnT near the IT arm (274C) by 14.6 Å relative to the N-lobe and 12 Å relative to the C-lobe, supporting an increase in C-cTnT flexibility relative to WT (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), while D270N decreased the FWHM of cTnT-283C relative to the cTnC C-lobe by 22.4 Å, suggesting the more distal portion of C-cTnT becomes less flexible and thus more stable than WT (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This highlights an interesting finding where both D270N (with respect to the C-lobe) and K273E (with respect to the N-lobe) exhibited decreased flexibility at cTnT-283C. Although they exhibited similar trends on a more macroscale, the intermolecular interactions (discussed below) can account for this differential flexibility relative to the different lobes of cTnC. Overall, it appears D270N alters the distance and flexibility (FWHM) seen in the WT C-cTnT, suggesting it never reaches the expected activated position when myosin is bound.


### Atomic Resolution of the Effects of DCM-Associated cTnT-D270N.
  A summary of interaction frequency in cTnT-D270N cTFs is shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S8–S10.

  The DCM mutation cTnT-D270N, in the absence of calcium, exhibited a similar number of interactions with actin. Interactions with tropomyosin decreased in frequency (≤15% of the trajectory, SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Interactions with cTnC were infrequent in the WT cTF (≤5% of the trajectory), whereas they were all but eliminated in the presence of cTnT-D270N (≤0.1% of the trajectory, SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S8). Similar trends were seen regarding interactions with cTnI, but to a lesser degree, where D270N reduced the number and frequency of interactions with cTnI (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Interactions with cTnC and cTnI in the presence of calcium were also reduced (no interactions with cTnC) compared to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Thus, it is likely that cTnT interactions with cTnC play a major role in C-cTnT stability, with and without calcium, as the reduction/loss of these interactions coincides with increased flexibility seen in our TR-FRET data. With myosin bound, D270N reduced C-cTnT interaction frequency with cTnC, cTnI, and myosin (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). This suggests interactions with cTnC, cTnI, and myosin are important to stabilize the activated, myosin-bound state, and the loss of these interactions likely contribute to the increased FWHM near the IT-arm seen in the open state (Fig. 2, Panels B and D).


### Structural and Dynamic Effects of HCM-Associated cTnT-K273E.
  A summary of average distances and FWHM (distributions) in the cTnT-K273E cTF are shown in Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Table S2 and a representation of the Gaussian distributions and average distance for each biochemical condition is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S4.

  Compared to WT, in cTFs containing K273E, cTnT-274C was 8.4 Å closer to the N-lobe of cTnC without calcium, 5.6 Å closer with calcium, with no change in the myosin-bound state (Fig. 3). This trend was similar regarding the FWHM, which decreased between cTnT-274C and cTnC-53C by 20.9 Å in the absence of calcium, 18.6 Å in the presence of calcium, and was unchanged with bound myosin (Fig. 3). Taken together, these data demonstrate 274C is closer to and less flexible relative to the N-lobe in mutant cTFs than WT. Thus, the mutation appears to move C-cTnT to an activated position like that seen in WT, when it should be in an inactivated state. This suggests K273E prevents C-cTnT from reaching an inactivated position, likely resulting in a more activated filament, even in the absence of calcium.


### Atomic Resolution of the Effects of HCM-Associated cTnT-K273E.
  A summary of interaction frequency in cTnT-K273E cTFs is shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S13.

  In the absence of calcium, cTnT-K273E containing cTFs exhibited reduced interactions with actin and increased interactions with cTnC contributing to the increased stability compared to WT cTFs (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). This likely explains why cTnT, near the IT-arm (cTnT-274C), exhibited a smaller FWHM, and thus is less flexible. The substitution of lysine to glutamic acid represents a charge change and creates new, more frequent interactions with Tm-R182 and cTnC-R147.

  When cTnT-K273E mutant cTFs were bound with myosin, C-cTnT exhibited reduced interactions with actin, cTnC, and cTnI, and increased interactions with Tm (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Overall C-cTnT stability was minimally affected by mutation K273E in the open state, suggesting that the increased interaction with Tm may “offset” the reduced interactions with actin, cTnC, and cTnI. This explains why cTnT-K273E cTFs were similar to WT when bound to myosin in our TR-FRET data (Fig. 3).


### Effects of cTnT Mutations on Thin Filament Thermodynamics.
  To determine whether the local structural changes detected for the C-cTnT mutations can be propagated to other crucial regulatory regions of the cTF we measured the thermal unfolding of Tm bound Tn (Tm-Tn) away from actin as we have previously published (27, 28). Briefly, we used differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) to measure the heat capacity (kJ/mol*K) of the cTF as it unfolded over a range of temperatures. This provided information on the thermal stability (Tm) and cooperativity (full width half max, FWHM), a surrogate of flexibility, of distinct structural domains in the cTF as they unfold (Fig. 6, panel A) (27, 28).

  In the absence of calcium, WT cTFs had a thermal stability of 48.1 °C that was reduced to 47.8 °C when calcium was added (Fig. 6). This may suggest reduced interactions between Tm-Tn and actin, contributing to tropomyosin’s ability to move across actin as it transitions to the closed state. At baseline the FWHM of the WT cTF was 1.7 °C and this was not changed by the addition of calcium.

  The C-cTnT mutations D270N and K273E exhibited a baseline reduction in thermal stability in the absence of calcium to 47.7 °C and 47.3 °C, respectively, that was unaffected by the addition of calcium (47.6 °C and 47.4 °C) (Fig. 6). This suggests that these mutations propagate their structural effects through the Tm-overlap, a critical region for cTF regulatory function, reducing the thermal stability of the interactions between Tm-Tn and actin. These data may indicate that in the blocked state these mutations weaken interactions between Tm-Tn and actin.

  The C-cTnT mutation K273E had no effect on the FWHM (a surrogate of flexibility) of the Tm–Tn complex unfolding. As demonstrated in Fig. 6, the FWHM of the cTnT-K273E complexes was 1.6 °C in the absence of calcium and 1.8 °C in the presence of calcium compared to WT calcium free (1.7 °C) or calcium bound (1.7 °C) cTFs. However, cTnT-D270N cTFs exhibited an increase in FWHM in the absence of calcium to 1.9 °C suggesting that the presence of D270N in C-cTnT increases the flexibility of the Tm-Tn chain via propagation through the Tm-overlap region. These data suggest that C-cTnT mutations may propagate their local structural changes to other functional domains of the filament effecting the stability of interactions between the regulatory proteins and actin as well as the flexibility of the filament.


### Effects of cTnT Mutations on Myosin Enzymatic Activity.
  To determine how C-terminal cTnT mutations may influence thick-thin filament interactions, we performed myosin activity assays in cTFs in the absence of calcium containing either D270N or K273E. We found no differences in cTFs containing cTnT D270N (9.35 ± 0.48 micromoles ATP/min) compared to WT filaments (8.43 ± 0.71 micromoles ATP/min) (Fig. 7). However, cTFs containing cTnT-K273E exhibited increased enzymatic activity (10.32 ± 0.34 micromoles ATP/min) compared to WT cTFs as shown in Fig. 7. This suggests that in the inactivated state, D270N does not alter C-cTnT structure or stability in a manner in which myosin enzymatic activity is affected. However, as K273E increased this activity in the absence of calcium, we would predict this is primarily due to the altered structure and stability of intermolecular interactions detected in our TR-FRET data. Specifically, if cTnT-K273E is indeed able to reduce the energy barrier required to shift tropomyosin out of its blocked position, this could result in increased myosin attachment and thus increased activity or possibly increase the probability of myosin transitioning (once bound) from the weakly bound (nonforce producing state) to the strongly bound state.


## Discussion
  The C-cTnT is a disordered, flexible region whose structure is currently unresolved likely due to these inherent characteristics (11, 12, 29). This domain exhibits a high prevalence of HCM-associated mutations and variants of unknown significance, including multiple hotspots (R278, R286) (1). Interestingly, there is only one mutation/variant within this region of cTnT linked to DCM, D270N, near the terminal end of the IT-arm, a region of cTnT known to strongly interact with cTnI and cTnC (30, 31). Although clinical data on mutations and variants within this region are limited, several mutations are well supported to be causative of disease such as cTnT-K273E and Intron 15 G1→A, as large, unrelated, multigenerational families were genetically screened and demonstrated a strong link between patients who carry the mutation and exhibit the clinical phenotype (32–34). Therefore, this is a clinically and physiologically relevant region of the cardiac troponin complex. Consequently, resolving the structure of this disordered region is vital to understanding the mechanism(s) by which mutations in this region result in severe disease. Additionally, understanding this disordered domain will allow interrogation with variants, which are known to be difficult to interpret in clinical settings.

  To structurally resolve the disordered C-cTnT, we used high-resolution TCSPC to perform TR-FRET to understand the structure and dynamics of C-cTnT of the human adult WT cardiac thin filament. This is of particular interest as some prior studies used mixed species regulatory proteins or have performed experiments which do not include a fully regulated cardiac thin filament (6, 13, 14, 35–37). Our use of human regulatory proteins for in-vitro experiments complements our computational modeling which also utilizes the human regulatory proteins. Additionally, we aimed to identify intermolecular interactions using our atomistic computational cTF model.

  We triangulated the C-cTnT position in relation to the troponin core, specifically the N- and C-lobes of cTnC as the structure of cTnC is well understood in the context of the troponin complex alone and within a regulated thin filament (11, 12, 38–46). In agreement with Zhu et al. who recently reported a distance of approximately 39 Å between cTnT-275 and cTnC-53 in both calcium free and calcium bound thin filaments (14), we measured a distance of 45 Å and 44 Å between cTnT-274 and cTnC-53 for calcium free and calcium bound thin filaments respectively. Additionally, as we incorporated myosin into our TR-FRET experiments, we found that myosin binding results in C-cTnT moving closer to cTnC. As we utilized TCSPC to perform our TR-FRET experiments, our system provides higher resolution as we measure fluorescence lifetime and use global waveform analysis to examine fluorescence decay traces, which can resolve distinct structural states, while the prior study utilized steady state FRET measuring fluorescence intensity, reflecting a global average (47). While Zhu et al. found no changes with the addition of calcium between C-cTnT and the N-lobe of cTnC (14), which was similar for our study, we found calcium does indeed have effects on local C-cTnT structure, as we detected changes in distance between C-cTnT and the C-lobe of cTnC.

  As our knowledge about cardiac thin filament structure has greatly expanded with a number of high-resolution cryo-EM structures, this technique is limited to static structures and gives limited information regarding the dynamics of the system. Therefore, the understanding of flexible, disordered regions in the cTF was limited. Our experimental system overcomes this limitation and we demonstrate that C-cTnT structure is dynamic and is altered in the presence of calcium (Figs. 1 and 8 and SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). Additionally, when cTFs are bound with myosin S1, C-cTnT becomes less disordered, as evidenced by the sharp decrease in the FWHM between cTnT-274 and cTnC-53, and cTnT-274 and cTnC-125, suggesting a more stable, less flexible C-cTnT in the open state (Fig. 1). Interestingly, decreased disorder was only observed in the portion of C-cTnT near the IT-arm (cTnT-274), as no changes in disorder were found near the terminal end (cTnT-283), demonstrating that it remains flexible. This supports a disordered to ordered transition occurring as the thin filament becomes activated as myosin binds. Therefore, not only is the average structure different between the three states, which is not surprising, but the dynamics also dramatically change and this is varied between different regions of C-cTnT.

  MD simulations, using our TR-FRET data as initial constraints, identified complex and highly dynamic intermolecular interactions between C-cTnT with actin, tropomyosin, cTnI, cTnC, and myosin (when present) in the WT cTF and are summarized in SI Appendix, Figs. S5–S7. WT C-cTnT in the absence of calcium exhibited a high degree of flexibility, ~30 Å FWHM. The addition of calcium increased the number and frequency of interactions between C-cTnT with both cTnC and cTnI (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), however, the FWHM remain approximately 30 Å, suggesting that newly formed interactions between C-cTnT with cTnC and cTnI are not sufficient to alter overall flexibility. It was only upon the addition of calcium and myosin S1 together that we detected a decrease in the FWHM of C-cTnT, suggesting that C-cTnT interacts with the myosin head directly or that myosin alters other intermolecular interactions in such a manner that forms a more stable C-cTnT and indeed we found several high frequency interactions between cTnT and myosin (Fig. 8 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7) as well as altered interactions with other thin filament proteins (SI Appendix, Figs. S5–S7).

  We found several high frequency interactions between C-cTnT (residues 282 to 288) and myosin (residues 405 to 410) (Fig. 8 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Interestingly, there are several well-established pathogenic mutations and variants of unknown significance within this region of myosin such as V406M and G407V/C (48–51). Although myosin R403 was not explicitly detected to interact with C-cTnT in our interaction data, the proximity of this well-studied mutational hotspot (R403Q/W/L/G) (50, 52, 53) could potentially cause local structural changes that may very well impact the interactions that we did detect. However, as we found a decrease in the FWHM at cTnT-274 and not at the more distal cTnT-283, the interactions with myosin likely do not exclusively lead to the increased stability that we see in our TR-FRET data but certainly cannot be excluded from contributing to the increased stability as the cardiac thin filament is known to exhibit molecular allostery.

  Our computational data suggest the number and frequency of interactions increased in WT C-cTnT in the open, myosin-bound state compared to the blocked state. These data corroborate the TR-FRET data, in which we found a more stable average structure in the open state relative to the blocked state, as indicated by the decreased FWHM. Our TR-FRET data on its own did not suggest C-cTnT is directly interacting with cTnC, as we measured cTnT residues 274 and 283 were between 44 Å and 59 Å away from cTnC-53 or 125. This finding was in agreement with Zhu et al., who found no evidence of direct interactions between C-cTnT with cTnC based solely on the distance between cTnT-275 and cTnC-53 (14). Our MD simulations, however, identified several interactions in the absence of calcium between cTnT-276 and 277 with cTnC residues 109, 111, 143, 144, 145, and 147; however, these interactions were only present in ≤5% of the trajectory, suggesting these are likely transient interactions (Fig. 8 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). When WT cTFs were bound with calcium, several high frequency interactions were found between C-cTnT and cTnC, specifically cTnT-287 and 288 with cTnC-147 and 111 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Johnston et al. detected direct interactions between cTnT-K282 and cTnC-K6 via chemical cross-linking followed by mass spectrometer analysis on the WT cardiac troponin complex in the presence of calcium (13). Although we did not detect the same interaction, the use of troponin complex alone versus the full cardiac thin filament, as used in both the TR-FRET and MD, likely explains this difference as the thin filament is known to form extensive electrostatic interactions (1, 54).

  These data provide a high-resolution starting point that facilitates the ability to interrogate how perturbing the system can alter these intermolecular interactions and thus the structure of this region. Therefore, we introduced mutations of interest into this region of cTnT to test this approach. First, we examined DCM-associated mutation cTnT-D270N. As this mutation results in the loss of a negative charge within the end of the IT-arm we predicted it would have significant effects to not only the average structure, but also the intermolecular interactions. Indeed, we found that cTnT-D270N not only alters the average structure, as seen in SI Appendix, Fig. S3, but has effects on C-cTnT intermolecular interaction frequency. As demonstrated in Fig. 8 and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9, this mutation essentially eliminates interactions with cTnC and cTnI in both the absence and presence of calcium. It was not until cTFs containing D270N were bound with myosin did we detect only a few interactions with cTnC and cTnI. This suggests that D270N results in a destabilization of C-cTnT with other troponin subunits, but once bound with myosin, can interact with the myosin head in a manner that appears to be indistinguishable from WT, likely explaining why we found no change in myosin activity in the presence of D270N in the absence of calcium (Fig. 7). Thus, in a more biologically complex system we would predict this mutation alters the kinetics of thin filament activation.

  When we examined the interactions in cTFs containing the HCM-associated mutation cTnT-K273E, we found opposite effects regarding interactions with cTnC, compared to that of cTnT-D270N. In the absence of calcium in K273E thin filaments, interactions with cTnC increased, compared to WT, from 8 interactions all present for less than 5% of the trajectory to ~20 interactions for at least 10% of the MD trajectory, 6 of which were present for ≥40% of the trajectory (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). A summary of each protein–protein interaction map for each biochemical condition and mutation is included in SI Appendix, Figs. S29–S39. Interaction frequency increased, suggesting decreased flexibility due to increased interactions between C-cTnT with cTnC; indeed, that is what we observed in our TR-FRET data (Fig. 3). Although interactions with cTnC were decreased upon the binding of calcium, interactions with tropomyosin, actin, and cTnI increased, likely accounting for the increased stability seen in the TR-FRET in the closed state and this stability was maintained in the presence of myosin (Fig. 3). Our findings suggest that cTnT-K273E induces C-cTnT into a position that closely resembles the activated WT state even in the absence of calcium. This is supported by the increased MgATPase activity of myosin seen in reconstituted cTFs (Fig. 7). The charge change from a positive lysine to a negative glutamic acid clearly alters the WT structure and interactions. Note that others have demonstrated that the introduction of negative charges near the IT helix stabilizes the activated state of actin (36), and our TR-FRET data support this finding. However, we would also suggest that the intermolecular interactions would be differentially altered depending upon the location of the altered charge and may not always result in increased stability. Interestingly, Venkatraman et. al. performed a myosin ATPase activity assay in the presence of cTnT-K273E and found no differences in myosin activity, contrary to our findings (55). Several differences in experimental methodology could possibly explain this discrepancy. First, our assay utilized the NADH-coupled activity assay, where NADH oxidation is coupled to the hydrolysis of ATP by myosin, whereas the other assay measured the inorganic phosphate released. Another possibility is our use of human ala-ser alpha-tropomyosin and rabbit skeletal myosin compared to the porcine cardiac tropomyosin and myosin. Last, our experiments were performed at room temperature using HMM, whereas the other group performed experiments at 37 °C and presumably with full-length myosin, as no mention of digested myosin were made.

  Considering all our WT data together, we hypothesize a flycasting mechanism, like that of the C-terminal tail of cTnI, is occurring in this region of cTnT (56). In cTnI, the tail electrostatically “searches” for tropomyosin, whereby it helps to hold tropomyosin in the blocked state. As cryo-EM experiments did not resolve C-cTnT, it is possible C-cTnT is not interacting with Tm at only one or two specific sites but rather searches for any interactions with Tm, resulting in numerous structural conformations as suggested by the large FWHM found in the absence and presence of calcium (Fig. 1). Unlike the C-terminal tail of cTnI, which when truncated by 17 residues, results in the displacement of the average position of Tm on actin (57), the C-cTnT when truncated (∆28+7), does not have an effect on the average position of Tm on actin (16), suggesting more subtle effects. This led to the hypothesis that C-cTnT functions by regulating the energy barrier required to shift Tm from the blocked to closed to open states (16). This is supported by several studies of the HCM mutation Intron 15 G1→A, which results in two predicted truncated protein products (cTnT ∆14 or ∆28+7), and demonstrated the inability to prevent actomyosin cross-bridge formation and force production using mutant troponin exchanged into skinned porcine cardiac muscle preparations (58), reduced actomyosin S1 ATPase rates in the absence of calcium (58, 59), and diastolic dysfunction in intact mouse hearts expressing <5% mutant protein (9). While we do not disagree that this is likely a primary mechanism by which C-cTnT regulates thin filament activation, we propose that the intermolecular interactions between C-cTnT and other thin filament proteins dictates what this energy barrier is. Coupling our all-atom computational model with experimental TR-FRET data now gives us a starting point where in the future we can perform computational metadynamics studies to examine and measure the energy required to shift tropomyosin out of the blocked position (28, 60).

  To further assess the biophysical effects of these mutations, we performed DSC to examine cTF unfolding events which can inform us of thin filament stability. First, we found the WT cTF undergoes a decrease in thermal stability (Tm) of the Tm–Tn complex (domain 2, D2) upon the addition of calcium, which is indicative of reduced interactions between Tm-Tn and actin. This is consistent with tropomyosin moving across actin to partially reveal underlying myosin binding sites upon the addition of calcium when transitioning from the blocked to closed states, as well as cTnI releasing its contacts with actin which also aids in tropomyosin’s movement. Taking this into account, we found not only the HCM-associated mutation K273E, but also the DCM-associated mutation D270N, exhibited reduced Tm of this Tm–Tn complex in calcium free conditions, while K273E also reduced Tm in calcium saturated conditions (Fig. 6). This could explain why cTFs containing cTnT-K273E exhibit increased myosin enzymatic activity in the absence of calcium, as weakened interactions between Tm-Tn and actin would likely make it easier for Tm to shift from the blocked to closed to open state, shifting the equilibrium distribution to favor a more activated state, as even in the absence of calcium Tm can be found in blocked, closed, and open positions on actin (61). It is possible D270N does not alter Tm–Tn complex interactions with actin to a large enough degree to affect myosin activity, as the Tm between cTnT-K273E and cTnT-D270N cTFs was different in the absence of calcium (Fig. 6). This suggests cTFs are able to “tolerate” mutational effects to a degree before altered function occurs, which has been previously proposed (62).

  In the main, these data presented here facilitate the interrogation of molecular mechanisms at an atomistic level of clinically significant cardiomyopathic mutations in the C-terminal tail of cTnT. As the thin filament undergoes transitions from blocked to closed to open and back to blocked during the contractile cycle, understanding the dynamics of flexible, disordered regions is paramount to understanding how pathogenic mutations and variants of unknown significance result in genetic cardiac disorders. Of note, we defined the intermolecular interactions of WT C-cTnT as well as the impact of HCM mutation cTnT-K273E and DCM mutation cTnT-D270N on inherent intermolecular interactions, thin filament stability, and thick-thin filament interactions. This high-resolution coupling of TR-FRET and MD simulations provides a framework for investigating disordered, flexible regions of proteins and protein complexes that are unable to be detected with classic structural techniques such as X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM, or cryoelectron tomography. Indeed, as the field is constantly increasing the resolution of these techniques as well as capturing data in more intact systems including both thin and thick filaments simultaneously (63), capturing information about the dynamics of these systems at high-resolution is also necessary as it is clear that the interplay of complex intermolecular interactions are inherent to myocardial function and physiology and may, in fact, provide new targets for clinical intervention.


## Limitations
  In our present study, we utilized TR-FRET to interrogate the structural dynamics of the C-cTnT in human troponin. Due to these proteins (Tm and Tn) being bacterially expressed, posttranslational modifications are absent as obtaining and utilizing human proteins would be complicated and the amount of protein would likely be limited. As the central hypothesis of thin filament activation mediated by C-cTnT is through regulating the energy barrier required to shift tropomyosin from the blocked to closed to open states, we did not directly measure this in our current study; however, we are currently exploring the use of advanced computational techniques, such as metadynamics, to measure this directly in-silico. As in-vitro ATPase assays lack sarcomeric structure, this was necessary as no mouse models of cTnT-K273E or cTnT-D270N, to our knowledge, currently exist. We are currently assessing generating these mouse models or performing troponin exchange experiments in myofibrils to assess thick-thin filament interactions in a more intact system in future work.


## Materials and Methods
  Detailed methods can be found in SI Appendix.


### Protein Expression, Purification, and Reconstitution.
  cDNA containing sequences for human cardiac TnT (hcTnT), human cardiac TnI (hcTnI), human cardiac TnC (hcTnC), and Ala-Ser α-tropomyosin™ were inserted into pET3d vectors provided by J.D. Potter (University of Miami). Actin was obtained from rabbit skeletal muscle as previously described (64). Myosin was purified from cryoground rabbit skeletal muscle obtained from Pel-Freez Biologicals (Rogers, AR) and was digested into heavy meromyosin (HMM) or S1 as needed.


### Protein Labeling.
  Cysteine-substituted hcTnT was labeled with the donor probe N-(iodoacetaminoethyl)-1-naphthylamine-5-sulfonic acid or IAEDANS (Setareh Biotech, Eugene, OR) and cysteine-substituted hcTnC was labeled with the acceptor probe 4-dimethylaminophenylazophenyl-4’-maleimide or DABMI (Setareh Biotech, Eugene, OR).


### Time-Resolved Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer.
  Lifetime data were obtained using a ChronosBH fluorescence lifetime spectrometer from ISS (Champaign, IL) with a K428 microwell plate reader using TCSPC. The instrument response function was collected from the emission of TR-FRET working buffer. Data were analyzed using FargoFit, a global waveform analysis software and was aided with the help of Brett Colson (University of Arizona). The Förster distance was experimentally determined for our unique protein system as previously described (64).


### Differential Scanning Calorimetry.
  Experiments were performed as previously described (65, 66), briefly, samples were heated from 0 to 100 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min using the NanoDSC (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE) and the obtained calorimetric profile was analyzed using NanoAnalyze software provided by TA Instruments.


### Myosin Activity Assay.
  An NADH-coupled enzymatic activity assay was performed using the cysteine-substituted proteins used in FRET to assess for the expected calcium-based regulation of the substitutions with probes bound in reconstituted cardiac thin filaments. We also performed this assay to investigate the mutational effects of actomyosin interactions in reconstituted human cardiac thin filaments.


### Molecular Dynamics.
  Simulations were performed as previously described (64). Three 10 ns production runs with random initial velocities were conducted and the average structures obtained from the production ensembles were obtained for further analysis. All simulations were conducted in the NanoScale Molecular Dynamics (67) version 2.13, with the Chemistry at HARvard Macromolecule Mechanics 36 force field (68, 69).
