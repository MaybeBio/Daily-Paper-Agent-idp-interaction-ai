# Structural
and Functional Relevance of Charge-Based
Transient Interactions inside Intrinsically Disordered Proteins


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) perform diverse biological functions without adopting stable folded structures, instead existing as dynamic ensembles of flexible conformations. While these conformations were traditionally attributed to weak, nonspecific interactions, emerging evidence emphasizes the role of transient, specific interactions. Here, we investigate how charged amino acids within IDP sequences influence the prevalence of these interactions. Using model peptides, we establish an empirical relationship between the fraction of transient interactions and a novel sequence metric, the effective charge patch length. Extending this analysis to IDP ensembles with varying levels of transient interactions, we uncover heteropolymeric structural behaviors, including network formation in phase-separated condensates. A large-scale analysis reveals that approximately 20% of disordered regions in the human proteome exhibit charge-driven transient interactions, contributing to heteropolymeric conformational ensembles. Finally, we explore the functional enrichment of these interactions, underscoring their potential role in mediating diverse biological processes.


## Introduction
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) play critical roles in diverse cellular processes, including transcription and signal transduction, formation of membraneless organelles, −   and generation of functional or pathological aggregates. ,  Unlike folded proteins, where structure often serves as an intermediate in the sequence-structure–function paradigm, −   IDPs lack a well-defined three-dimensional structure, disrupting this classical framework. ,  Instead, their sequences generate dynamic ensembles with numerous degrees of freedom, −     and biophysicists often turn to quantitative structural properties to describe them in a smaller dimensional space and to mechanistically explain specific functions. ,  The challenge then is to identify which of the many possible structural properties of a given conformational ensemble are functionally relevant. Since these properties are encoded by specific sequence grammars, −       doing so would allow us to effectively bridge the gap between sequence and function. −

  Advances in experimental technologies have enabled direct measurement of numerous structural properties of IDPs. These methods often target specific properties, restricting hypotheses about functionally relevant features to observable data. For example, the overall size of an IDP, typically quantified by the radius of gyration or hydrodynamic radius, is a common metric. The polymer scaling exponent, which relates these size measurements to protein chain length, ,  has effectively interpreted experimental data ,  and explained phenomena like liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS). This approach assumes IDPs act as homopolymers, governed by nonspecific, weak interactions. Such assumptions are valid for low-complexity sequences, where a few amino acids dominate or repetitive fragments occur, allowing average sequence properties to predict behaviors like LLPS.

  Emerging evidence shows that IDPs are influenced by more than nonspecific, weak interactions, and overall size alone is insufficient to capture their conformational behavior. Specific, transient interactions between amino acids or distant sequence fragments, observed through techniques like paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE), ,  Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), ,  and photoinduced electron transfer (PET) ,  measurements, reveal deviations from homopolymer models. These interactions can be driven by charged ,  or hydrophobic amino acids. ,,  Their presence in various transcriptional activation domains suggests a functional role in transcriptional regulation. ,−   In a recent article, homologous proteins were shown to recover their molecular function when they shared similar separated charged patches in their disordered domain, potentially creating transient interactions. Additionally, transient interactions may shape IDP condensate microstructures, fine-tuning membraneless organelle formation. ,  While experimental methods like site-specific labeling probe these interactions, they face challenges such as label-induced perturbations and the need for prior knowledge of optimal labeling sites. Moreover, interpreting these methods can be complex as they depend on both the lifetime of the transiently formed interaction state and its population within the ensemble. ,,,  Computational ensemble generation methods might provide ensembles matching diverse sources of experimental measurements − ,,  but involve uncertainties and depend on the accuracy of the underlying physics-based models.

  In this work, we develop a novel computational framework to investigate the structural and functional relevance of transient interactions, with a particular focus on those involving charged amino acids, especially between charged patches. We focus primarily on charged patches as they have proven to be a promising strategy for modulating disordered protein behaviors. ,  Previous studies suggest that these charged patches can induce two-state (i.e., open and closed) behavior in a conformational ensemble. ,  In addition, well-separated charge blocks can actively regulate condensate formation and selectively recruit similarly blocky clients into condensates. To fully explore the variation of transient interactions within the charge-based sequence space, we simulate a series of model peptides at different temperatures and ionic strengths using a coarse-grained (CG) model, creating a large ensemble database that captures a range of transient interaction levels. From this database, we derive a new sequence metric that predicts charge-driven transient interactions directly from amino acid sequences. This relationship allows us to identify transient interactions across IDPs in the human proteome, examine their structural and functional significance, and explore whether they represent an overlooked structural property crucial for formulating the sequence-structure–function paradigm of IDPs.


## Modulation of Transient Interactions
through
Sequence and Environmental Factors
  Our initial goal was to establish a model system to quantitatively modulate transient interactions within an IDP. In prior work, we studied DR-N, a model peptide with N aspartic acid residues at the C-terminal, N arginine residues at the N-terminal, and a glycine linker. Using CG simulations with the HPS model, we observed two interconverting states: an open state resembling an IDP without specific interactions and a closed state where the charged patches interact. This behavior, observed in the patch-to-patch distance distribution (Figure  a), was described using the SAW-ν-tr model, which integrates two distance distribution functions: one for the short-distance peak, characterizing the charge–patch interactions, and the other for the long-distance peak, reflecting the overall size of the IDP. The subsequent behavior as well as the analytical form of the two distributions is shown in the right-hand side of Figure  a. P t(r) approximates the short-range magenta peak, while P IDR(r, ν) captures the long-range cyan peak. We previously demonstrated that our formulation of P(r) can flexibly model a range of behaviors by adjusting f t as a free parameter to shift the distribution between the two peaks. Detailed expressions for the constituent components are provided in SAW-ν-tr Model. In this work, we extended the DR-N model to include nonconsecutive charged amino acids, commonly seen in biologically relevant IDPs. Charge content was characterized by total patch length (N) and fraction of charged amino acids (f c), allowing f c < 1. Nonconsecutive patches were created by replacing charged residues with glycine to adjust f c (e.g., swapping every third residue yields f c = 2/3 in Figure  a). To examine patch separation (s), we varied peptide lengths instead of the fixed 100 residues from prior work. This approach aligns with the sequence charge decoration (SCD) descriptor, ,  which includes charge separation and is important for IDP characterization. ,,,

  We first performed single-chain CG simulations (see Coarse-Grained (CG) Model) on DR-N peptides with varying N and f c and fixed s = 100 across temperatures (T) and salt concentrations (I), yielding 1242 combinations (Table S2). The patch-to-patch distance distributions were then calculated and fit to the SAW-ν-tr model (see SAW-ν-tr Model) with four parameters: the transient interaction fraction (f t), the center r c and width (σc) for the short-range peak, and the polymer scaling exponent ν for the long-distance peak (see Figure S1 for fitting representative cases). We note that when f t is close to zero, the ν obtained from fitting the SAW-ν-tr model closely matches the traditional ν derived from directly fitting the intrachain distance function along sequence separation or using a homopolymer model like the SAW-ν model. However, as f t increases, the ν determined here deviates from the homopolymer interpretation, reflecting how the remaining weak, nonspecific interactions influence the conformational ensemble. This process generated a comprehensive data set of f t and ν as functions of charge patch parameters and environmental conditions. We found that f t increases rapidly with higher N or f c and is highly sensitive to charge content (Figure  b). Environmental parameters such as ionic strength (I) strongly influence f t due to the electrostatic screening effect, while temperature (T) has a smaller effect (Figure  d,e). The behavior of f t with respect to T is affected by two factors within the simulation model. First, at higher temperatures, the entropically favored open state becomes more prevalent, reducing the likelihood of contact between the two patches. Second, the HPS model incorporates an experimentally parameterized adjustment to amino acid hydrophobicity, which, within the temperature range considered, slightly weakens pairwise hydrophobic interactions between charged amino acids. In contrast, ν showed weaker variation across N and f c (Figure  c), and temperature had a greater effect on ν than ionic strength (Figure  f,g). For the range of ionic strengths simulated, the model peptides’ conformational behavior should primarily be influenced by electrostatic screening, although the HPS model also incorporates an empirical parameterization for the salting-out effect. Electrostatic screening is implemented in our CG model by the Debye–Hückel approximation, ,  which is expected to be reasonable for systems with moderate salt concentrations as considered here. ,  We emphasize that for conformational ensembles with f t = 1, only a single short-distance peak is observed, which prevents the determination of the scaling exponent ν. As a result, ν values are not shown for these cases. Since f t depends primarily on N, f c, and I, we expect an empirical relationship between these sequence parameters and f t, independent of T.


## Empirical Estimation of Transient Interactions
Driven by Sequence Factors
  As previously shown, f t exhibits a rapid transition from 0 to 1 with small variations in sequence factors N and f c (Figure  b). We propose modeling f t as a sigmoid function using a sequence metric that combines these factors. From Coulomb’s law, the natural choice for this metric is the number of charged amino acids q = f c N. However, quantifying electrostatic interactions using q assumes that all oppositely charged amino acids are always in direct contact, which holds for a rigid structure but not for IDPs, where charge patches adopt flexible conformations. Consequently, the effective number of charged amino acids contributing to electrostatic interactions may be smaller than q. To account for this flexibility, we borrow a concept from polymer theory, introducing a scaling exponent β to adjust N, as described by the equation 1 Neff=fcNβ where N eff represents the effective number of charged amino acids within the charge patch, hereafter referred to as the effective charge patch length. We then optimized β by fitting the relationship between f t and N eff using a sigmoid function (eq  )­ 2 ft=1−exp⁡[b1(Neff2−b2)]−1 where b 1 and b 2 are free parameters varying with T and I, and β is a common free parameter across all conditions.

  We first tested a case with β fixed at 1. While this fit worked well for extreme f t values, as shown in Figure S2 at three representative temperatures, deviations appeared in the transition region. We then performed a global fit of the f t curves across all simulations, allowing β to vary, which yielded an optimal scaling parameter of β = 0.76. Though the precise conformational implication of this β value remains uncertain, if it is comparable to the polymer scaling exponent, then the optimal scaling exponent of 0.76 suggests that the charge patch adopts a more expanded conformation than a random coil. This may result from electrostatic repulsion within the patch. Representative fitting curves for simulations at 300 K are shown in Figure  a, with those for other temperatures in Figure S3. The values for b 1 and b 2 across all temperatures and ionic strengths are provided in Code Availability. A key question is whether β is robust to environmental factors. We found that β was minimally affected by ionic strength but showed slight variation with temperature, as shown in Figure  b. This behavior aligns with expectations that ionic strength only influences the electrostatic interactions quantified by N eff, while temperature impacts the peptide size and scaling behavior. Given that the optimal β for each temperature falls within a narrow range of 0.7 to 0.8, and varying β did not significantly improve the fit, we have adopted β = 0.76 as a constant for all conditions.

  We used N eff to examine how charge content affects transient interactions, consolidating the two original sequence metrics (N and f c) into one. This allowed us to simultaneously monitor N eff, T, and f t (Figure  c) or N eff, I, and f t (Figure  d). In both cases, we observed a rapid transition of f t as a function of N eff, consistent with using a sigmoid-shaped function (eq  ) to describe this behavior. The transition point for f t shifts with T from roughly N eff of 2 to 4, while I causes a more pronounced shift from 2 to 7.5. A quantitative analysis of these N eff thresholds at varying T and I is detailed in Figure S4. Notably, a small threshold N eff value of approximately 3 achieves 50% transient interactions at 300 K and 0.1 M, corresponding to three consecutive charged amino acids. Minor changes in I instead of T significantly affect the N eff threshold, indicating f t can be fine-tuned through small sequence adjustments.

  The sequence metric s, defined as the number of amino acids between the centers of two charged patches, also influences transient interactions. To investigate, we conducted additional simulations at 300 K and 0.1 M ionic strength, varying s from 30 to 300 residues (Table S3). Determining f t at very low s is infeasible due to overlapping peaks for open and closed states in P(r). Thus, the smallest s in our data set is 30. The globally determined scaling value β = 0.76 can still be utilized to fit f t for these new sequences across our range of s values (Figures  e and S5). Corresponding values of b 1 and b 2 for the resulting curves are included in Code Availability. For fixed N eff, f t decreases with increasing s. However, when f t = 1, the interactions do not decay with s anymore, resembling native protein contacts. We therefore fitted f t as a function of s to an exponential function as 3 ft=ft′exp[−c(1−ft′)(s−s′)] where c is a shared free parameter at all s with an optimal value of 0.019, s′ = 30 is the reference patch separation, and f t ′ is the transient interaction fraction at s = 30 (Figure  f). This model, combined with eq   for s = 30, enables f t predictions of any sequences based on the sequence metrics N eff and s.


## Influence of Transient Interactions on Conformational
Behaviors
  To explore how transient interactions influence single- and multichain structural behaviors, we performed two additional types of simulations: slab coexistence and cubic box simulations. The slab simulation protocol, detailed in Slab Simulation Method and prior work, was conducted at 280 K, slightly below the critical temperatures for most sequences (Table S4). Cubic box simulations were conducted at slightly higher concentrations than the dense phase (Table S5). These simulations simplified the analysis of separating low- and high-density phases using slab simulations, enabling the characterization of f t impact on the microstructure within the liquid droplet. Structural observables are illustrated using a unified color code based on the sequence metric N eff, which correlates with f t (Figure  a).

  Density profiles from slab simulations (Figure S6) showed that only sequences with N eff > 3.8 formed stable condensates at 280 K and 0.1 M ionic strength, with two coexisting phases. The saturation concentration (low-density phase) and dense phase concentration (inside the droplet) were plotted on a phase diagram (Figure  b). Notably, f t was fully saturated in single-chain simulations for N eff > 3 (Figure  a), yet the dense phase concentration keeps increasing with N eff. The critical ionic strength I c below which LLPS occurs was determined from phase diagram fits (Slab Simulation Method, Figure S7). As N eff increased, I c and dense phase concentrations rose, while saturation concentrations dropped, reflecting enhanced interactions favoring phase separation. However, higher ionic strength screened electrostatic interactions, eventually collapsing the two phases into one.

  We investigated whether microscopic structures, such as peptide networks, exist within condensates, a phenomenon known as percolation-coupled phase separation. ,  Due to the coexistence of two phases in slab simulations, peptide clusters were analyzed only in cubic box simulations following Das and Muthukumar. Clusters were defined by peptides sharing contacts within oppositely charged patches (Cubic Box Simulations). The distribution of cluster size (C) was analyzed using a distance cutoff value of 0.7 nm (Figure  c), with additional results for varying cutoff distances shown in Figure S8. For lower N eff values, we observed a broad range of cluster sizes, but as N eff increased, the distribution shifted toward larger clusters. This trend aligns with previous studies, ,,  which demonstrate that multivalent interactions between peptides drive the formation of network structures. In our model, these interactions arise from specific transient interactions between charged patches and are effectively captured by N eff.

  We further examined the relation between the radius of gyration (R g) and N eff and its implications for network structures within condensates. In single-chain simulations, R g decreased with N eff as f t saturated (Figure  d). However, slab and cubic box simulations revealed two notable trends. First, R g in condensates was less sensitive to variations in N eff compared to single chain cases, consistent with previous computational −    and experimental studies. Second, R g slightly increased with N eff in condensates, contrary to the collapse observed in single chain simulations. To explore this further, patch-to-patch distance distributions (Figure  e) showed more open states in condensates compared to single chains. Additionally, both intra- and interchain contacts, as recently highlighted, ,  increased linearly with N eff (Figure S8). This suggests that stronger interchain contacts, alongside large condensate-spanning clusters, contribute to an expanded R g that is less affected by environmental factors or sequence variations.

  Finally, we analyzed intra- and interchain dynamics (see Time Correlation Analysis). For freely moving chains, the relaxation time (τ) is governed by transitions between open and closed states, evident from the large peak in τ when f t is near 50% (Figure  f). For sequences dominated by a single state (f t = 0 or 1), τ reflects relaxation within that state and is faster than transitions between states. Intrachain motions within condensates relax twice as slowly as in single chains, with τ increasing further as N eff rises due to slower state transitions. Interchain relaxation cannot be analyzed using the distance between a single pair of charge patches as each patch may interact with multiple oppositely charged patches simultaneously. We therefore analyzed the relaxation of coordination numbers, the number of charge patches contacting a given patchto capture node formation. τ increased linearly with N eff, similar to intrachain relaxation, but was an order of magnitude faster. This indicates robust network nodes with rapid relaxation and flexible linkers with slower dynamics due to partial restraint by the nodes.


## Exploration of the Functional
Relevance of Transient
Interactions
  To explore the functional relevance of transient interactions, we adapted our f t prediction algorithm to accommodate arbitrary IDP sequences. Given that biological sequences may lack a well-defined “charge patch”, we introduced a patch scanning algorithm. Using the disordered part of the SLC13A4 protein as an example, we obtained N eff as a function of both sequence position and window size N from 3 to 15 amino acids (Figure  a). The algorithm identified dominant positive and negative charge patches as those with the most positive (N eff+) and negative (N eff–) values (shaded areas in the figure), along with their sequence separation (s). Overlapping patches were excluded from f t determination.

  We first predicted f t using eqs   and , substituting N eff 2 with the product of N eff+ and N eff–. This assumption reflects our understanding that the electrostatic interaction can be captured by the product of the effective charge patch length within the positive and negative charge patches. We decided to test this assumption by performing simulations using several new model peptides featuring asymmetric charge patches, a summary of which is given in Table S6. We assessed the accuracy of our assumption by predicting f t for each new peptide using eqs   and  and the product of N eff+ and N eff– and comparing it to the simulated value. We found that our empirical equations consistently overestimate f t and that the relative error increases with charge asymmetry (see Figure S9a). To incorporate this effect into our algorithm for estimating f t, we fit the relative error of predicted versus observed f t with respect to the absolute value of the difference between N eff+ and N eff– to a power law function as shown in Figure S9a. This new equation, combined with eqs   and , returns a more accurate estimation of f t for IDPs with asymmetric charge patches. It is expected to improve f t predictions in the subsequent analysis of biologically relevant disordered sequences, with distribution of |N eff+ – N eff–| shown in Figure S9b.

  This algorithm has two limitations. First, sequences may include charged residues outside the identified patches. Although smaller patches may influence f t, the sigmoid relationship between f t and N eff suggests they play a secondary role. Second, when the same type of charged amino acids cluster within a short fragment, their effective charge may further be reduced due to charge regulation or renormalization. ,  Our algorithm could overestimate f t, but it does not compromise our qualitative analysis of the functional relevance of transient interactions in subsequent steps.

  We applied this algorithm to the IDRome database, analyzing a total of 27,562 disordered sequences with a chain length of at least 30 amino acids. This analysis identified 11,822 sequences with distinct positive and negative charge patches. The patch separation s varied widely, reaching up to 1000 residues, although most were under 400 (Figure  b). The distribution of N eff+ and N eff– was uneven with more prevalent negative patches (Figure  c). Most IDPs (approximately 79.7%) showed little to no transient interactions (f t < 0.1, Figure  d), aligning with the traditional view that IDPs generally lack specific interactions. However, approximately 20.3% of IDPs show f t values larger than 0.1, suggesting that transient interactions are not uncommon in IDPs and may play roles in their biological functions.

  To determine which biological functions depend on transient interactions, we performed GO enrichment analysis on sequences grouped by their f t values (Gene Ontology Analysis). The IDP sequences, with their f t values determined, were classified into five groups based on f t ranges, and GO enrichment analysis was conducted on each group. The dependence of fold enrichment on f t was quantified using the Spearman correlation coefficient, allowing us to classify GO terms into four categories: ascending (enrichment increases with f t), descending (enrichment decreases with f t), turning (enrichment peaks at medium f t), and none (unclear dependence). Representative examples of these classes are illustrated in Figure  e. Our analysis focused on two distinct categories of GO terms: (1) high-level terms, which are immediate child nodes of the broader molecular function category, and (2) low-level terms, which are the terminal nodes in the GO tree map. High-level GO terms were distributed relatively evenly across the ascending, descending, and turning classes, although the turning class comprised the largest group overall. This may suggest that transient interactions of varying strengths (low, medium, or high f t) may influence broad biological processes (Figure S10). Though most high-level terms were able to be sorted into a class, the same could not be said for low-level terms. This may be due to a lack of sampling for such specific molecular functions, however. Notably, in classification, low-level terms showed a significant preference for the turning class. Additionally, among turning class terms, considering both high- and low-level terms, it is more common for the highest fold enrichment to occur in the 0.4 ≤ f t < 0.6 bin. Both findings underscore the crucial role of a medium fraction of transient interactions in enabling precise and dynamic molecular functions. Finally, calculated linear fitting slopes (S +, S –, and |S +| + |S –|) for each term’s fold enrichment and highlighted representative terms from the high- and low-level groups, shown in Figure  f. While these results do not directly prove that specific functions require transient interactions, they reveal intriguing correlations. For instance, in the ascending class, the general transcription initiation factor emerged as the highest-ranking term. Proteins in this category are known to mediate RNA polymerase holoenzyme assembly and contain clusters of charged amino acids.


## Discussion
  In this study, we examined how charged amino acid patches contribute to transient interactions in IDPs, uncovering fundamental principles governing these dynamic interactions. By analyzing model peptides with varying charged patches, we built a comprehensive database of transient interaction levels under different sequence and environmental conditions using coarse-grained simulations. This analysis yielded several key findings. First, charge-driven transient interactions are primarily affected by ionic strength, with temperature playing a minimal role. Second, these interactions decay exponentially with increasing patch separation unless stabilized as permanent interactions. Lastly, transient interactions can be quantitatively predicted using empirically derived equations based on the effective charge patch length, a newly introduced sequence metric incorporating a scaling parameter (<1) to the patch length. This metric enables predictions of transient interaction fractions directly from IDP sequences.

  Building on this framework, we explored the structural and functional implications of transient interactions in IDPs. During liquid–liquid phase separation, we observed that increasing transient interaction levels promote the formation of network structures within condensates, driven by the shift from intramolecular to intermolecular interactions. Our functional analyses revealed a strong enrichment of molecular functions associated with medium-strength transient interactions. These dynamic interactions provide a crucial balance, avoiding the extremes of overly weak interactions that reduce IDPs to random coils or overly strong ones that enforce folded conformations. This balance likely underpins the ability of IDPs to adopt diverse conformations and execute a wide range of biological functions.

  Despite these advances, our understanding of transient interactions remains incomplete. While our work focused on charge-based interactions, transient interactions might arise from other sequence characteristics such as hydrophobic and aromatic residues. Furthermore, IDPs often interact with a variety of partners, suggesting that heterotypic interactions may also depend on specific transient interactions. Our functional analysis, while providing intriguing correlations, does not yet offer a mechanistic explanation for why charge-based transient interactions are critical for certain functions. Nonetheless, our findings indicate that over 10% of IDPs exhibit charge-driven transient interactions, underscoring their prevalence and biological significance. Continued research in this area will illuminate additional structural elements within the IDP sequence-structure–function paradigm.


## Methods

### Coarse-Grained (CG) Model
  The CG HPS model considers each amino acid as one bead, described by three types of interactions: bonded, electrostatic, and short-range pairwise interaction terms characterized by amino acid hydropathy. Bonded interactions are modeled by a harmonic potential with a spring constant of 10 kJ/Å2 and a bond length of 3.8 Å. Electrostatic interactions are modeled using a Coulombic term with Debye–Hückel electrostatic screening, in which κ is the Debye screening length and D = 80, the dielectric constant of the solvent. The short-range pairwise interaction follows the Ashbaugh–Hatch functional form. Amino-acid specific parameters of the model are shown in Table S1. The hydrophobic scales (λ) of the 20 amino acids were further adjusted to include both the temperature dependence and salting-out effect. The interaction strength ε is set to 0.2 kcal/mol based on parameterization from previous works. The HOOMD-Blue software v2.9.3 together with the azplugins were used for the molecular dynamics simulations. All simulations were run using a Langevin thermostat with a friction coefficient of 0.01 ps–1, a time step of 10 fs, and a temperature of 298 K for 5 μs. The first 0.5 μs trajectories were dumped for equilibration, and the remaining trajectories were used for further analysis. To calculate distance distributions, we recorded the distances between the centers of the two charged patches across each simulated CG trajectory. The histogram was made using 100 bins and a distance range of 0 to 20 nm.


### SAW-ν-tr
Model
  The self-avoiding walk model with varying scaling exponents and transient interactions (SAW-ν-tr) describes the transient interaction fraction f t within an IDP by a combination of two distance distribution functions  4 P(r)=ftPt(r)+(1−ft)Ppolymer(r,ν)

  The first term is a Gaussian distribution function to match the short-distance peak 5 Pt(r)=1σt2πexp(−r−rt2σt2) in which the parameters r t and σt in the Gaussian function were fit empirically via CG simulations of a series of DR-N peptides. In this work, we also allow these two parameters to vary during the fitting process, enabling adaptation to the changes in the short-range peak caused by charge patches. The second term is a self-avoiding walk model with a varied scaling exponent ν (SAW-ν model) to match the long-distance peak. The distance distribution function of the model was initially developed to interpret the experimental measurements of an IDP in relation to its conformational properties, as shown below 6 Ppolymer(r,ν)=a14πR(rR)2+gexp[−a2(r/R)δ] in which g = (γ – 1)/ν and γ = 1.1615 in three dimensions, δ = 1/(1 – ν), and a 1 and a 2 are normalization factors so that ∫0 ∞ P polymer(r,ν) dr = 1 and ∫0 ∞ P polymer(r,ν)r 2 dr = R 2. The combined model therefore contains four free parameters: f t, ν, r t, and σt. This model reflects the metastable nature of transient interactions by considering the final p(r) function to be built from two statistically weighted parts. f t then characterizes the probability of finding the two charged patches in contact with one another and ranges from zero to one. For scenarios with low f t, the open state is still prominent in the overall ensemble. We expect that the conformational ensemble should be relatively undisturbed by the presence of transient interactions and well described by a homopolymer model, like the SAW-ν model. The scaling exponent ν for such an IDP ranges roughly from 0.45 to 0.65. For cases with large f t, the homopolymer interpretation and the ν value become less relevant as the transient interactions dominate the structural properties of the conformational ensemble. We would like to note that in most cases, an initial guess of f t = 0.5 for fitting is robust. However, for extreme cases in which only one peak exists in the distance distribution function, fitting to a two-peak model is ill-defined. For such cases, we manually adjust the initial guess of f t and the bounds of the minimization process to be either 0 or 1 based on the peak position. If the peak position occurs below the empirically observed cutoff distance of 2 nm, we adjust the initial guess of f t to be 1 and the allowed values of f t to be close to 1. This ensures that only the short-distance function (eq  ) dominates the fitting. If the peak position occurs above the cutoff, we adjust the initial guess f t to be 0 and the allowed values of f t to close to 0 so that only the SAW-ν polymer model (eq  ) is present.


### Slab Simulation Method
  Slab coexistence simulations were conducted in three steps. First, a large cubic box was populated with 100 peptide chains. The simulation box was then gradually compressed over 10 ns to achieve a high peptide concentration while avoiding steric overlaps. In the final step, the simulation box was extended along the z-axis to 280 nm, much larger than the x- and y-axes, creating a slab-like structure in the center of the simulation box to mimic a droplet with an effectively infinite surface area. All slab simulations were then run for 5 μs, with the first 1 μs designated for equilibration, and data analysis was conducted on the remaining 4 μs trajectories. To determine the dense phase and saturation concentrations of each slab simulation, we first centered each slab frame to z = 0. We then calculated the density profiles with 100 bins, averaged over the last 4 μs simulations. The dense phase concentration was taken as the average density at z = 0 (the slab center), while the saturation concentration was calculated from the average density across the 10 bins furthest from the center. After obtaining the concentrations for all sequences and conditions, we discarded any cases where the difference between saturation and dense phase concentrations was below a cutoff. The cutoff (approximately 25 mg/mL) was established based on the dense and dilute phase concentrations from the slab with the lowest charge content (N = 3, f c = 1/3), which was inspected and determined not to form a condensate. To determine the critical ionic strength, we fitted the dense (ρH) and dilute phase concentrations (ρL) to the equation below with two free parameters, the critical ionic strength I c and a protein-specific factor A  7 ρH(I)−ρL(I)=A(Ic−I)0.325 The critical exponent 0.325 is taken from a universal class of the 3D Ising model. For a meaningful fit, only sequences that exhibited phase separation across at least three different ionic strengths were considered, and any slabs that dissociated too quickly with respect to ionic strength were excluded.


### Cubic Box Simulations
  Cubic box simulations were conducted to create an environment at a slightly higher concentration than that of the high-density phase, ensuring a single-phase box suitable for calculating relevant structural features. To achieve this, we first calculated the high-density phase concentration for each sequence at 0.1 M salt and then determined the cubic box size needed to contain 100 chains at this concentration. The actual simulated box was set to be 0.1 nm smaller on each side than this calculated size. For each frame of the cubic box simulations, we applied a cluster-searching algorithm to identify peptide clusters. Starting from an initial chain, we identified all other chains in contact with it based on interactions between their positive and negative patches, where contact was defined as at least one residue from a positive patch contacting a residue from a negative patch on the initial chain. Various distance cutoffs were tested to define contacts (see Figure S8). For each contacted chain, this search process was iteratively applied to find additional contacting chains, continuing until no new contacts were found. All chains identified through this process were classified as a single cluster. The process was repeated for unclustered chains until all chains were assigned to clusters.


### Time Correlation Analysis
  To determine the relaxation times of various collective variables, we first calculated the autocorrelation function for each variable and fit the resulting relaxation curve to an exponential function. We analyzed patch-to-patch distances as collective variables in three types of simulations: single-chain, slab coexistence, and cubic box simulations. In slab coexistence simulations, the relaxation times reported represent the average over 100 chains. For cubic box simulations, we also examined the coordination number of charged patches, defined as the number of negatively charged patches within a 0.7 nm cutoff of any given positively charged patch. The reported relaxation times in this case represent the average over all 100 positive patches in the simulation.


### Gene
Ontology Analysis
  We classified IDP sequences into five groups based on their f t value ranges and collected their GO terms. Due to the tree structure of GO terms, we scanned the list of molecular function GO terms for each sequence and always added back the corresponding parent terms. Each sequence therefore contains, on average, four to eight GO terms, varying between different f t ranges. With this finalized, we calculated each term’s fold enrichment throughout the f t groups with respect to the whole IDRome database as N grp T ref/(N ref T grp), in which N grp is the number of times a term is annotated within a group, N ref is the number of times a term appears within the reference database, and T grp and T ref are the sizes of the f t group and reference set, respectively. Each term, therefore, has a series of five values representing its fold enrichment in different f t ranges. We used these values to create a metric which separated the GO terms into four classes based on their dependence with respect to f t. Since sampling GO terms within a specific f t range might be limited, we only classified terms that are represented in at least four out of the five f t ranges. First, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient to determine if the term’s fold enrichment monotonically increases or decreases with respect to f t. Terms whose correlation coefficient is greater than 0.8 or smaller than −0.8 fall into one of the first two classes, “ascending” or “descending”. The strength of the dependence between the GO term and f t can be quantified by the linear fitting slopes S + and S – for these two classes, respectively. Terms which do not meet this criterion are subject to a second test. We obtained the maximum value of fold enrichment within different f t ranges and again calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient on the two sides of the maximum. This test determines which terms have a nonmonotonic behavior with respect to f t. Terms whose first and second Spearman coefficients are above 0.8 and below −0.8 fall into the final class, “turning”. The strength of the dependence between the GO terms and f t in this class can be quantified by |S +| + |S –|, where S + and S – represent the linear fitting slopes from either side of the maximum. All other GO terms which were not classified into the previous classes were sorted into the “none” class.
