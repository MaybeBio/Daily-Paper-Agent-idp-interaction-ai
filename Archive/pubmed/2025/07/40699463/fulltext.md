# A phase separation-related gene signature for prognosis prediction and immunotherapy response evaluation in gastric cancer with targeted natural compound discovery


## Background
  Aberrant phase separation (PS) has emerged as a pivotal pathogenic mechanism in cancer development. However, its prognostic significance and influence on the tumor immune microenvironment in gastric cancer (GC) remain largely unexplored. This study aimed to develop a PS-related risk model for predicting clinical outcomes and immunotherapy response, and to identify potential natural small-molecule compounds targeting proteins within this PS-related network.


## Methods
  We integrated transcriptomic data from the TCGA-STAD and GSE62254 datasets with four PS-related databases (including DrLLPS, PhaSepDB, PhaSePro, and LLPSDB) to identify candidate signature genes. The prognostic model was developed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and validated in both cohorts. Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response between PS-related high- and low-risk groups was evaluated using TIDE algorithm scores. Potential therapeutic agents targeting signature genes were screened via Connectivity Map and HERB database analyses, followed by molecular docking validation.


## Results
  By Integrating analysis of the differential expression dataset from TCGA-STAD (n = 407, 375 tumor/32 normal) with prognosis-related dataset and PS-related dataset, we identified 78 candidate genes and developed a robust 21-gene risk prediction model. The model effectively stratified GC patients into high-risk and low-risk subgroups, with consistent performance in the independent GSE62254 validation cohort (n = 300, tumor). Compared to low-risk patients, high-risk patients exhibited poorer survival, a more immunosuppressive microenvironment, and a reduced response to immunotherapy. Moreover, computational screening identified 18 potential therapeutic natural compounds, 13 of which showed high-affinity binding to signature genes (docking scores > 6.0).


## Conclusions
  Our study developed a novel PS-related risk model that predicts GC outcomes, characterizes tumor immune microenvironment, evaluates immunotherapy response, and identifies targeting small molecules. These findings advance the understanding of PS regulation in GC and provide a framework for personalized therapy.

## Introduction
  Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most common malignant tumor and the third most frequent cause of cancer deaths worldwide, representing a significant public health challenge, especially in China, which accounted for 44.0% of global cases (479,000 diagnoses) and 48.6% of deaths (374,000 fatalities) in 2020 [1, 2]. GC often develops with subtle early symptoms, leading to advanced-stage diagnosis (III/IV) in over 70% of patients. In these cases, surgery is no longer curative, and the median survival time is less than 12 months [3]. Current treatments for GC face major limitations. Conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy cause severe side effects and lose effectiveness due to intrinsic tumor heterogeneity [3]. Targeted therapies against HER2 or VEGFR show restricted clinical benifits because of rapid acquired drug resistance and adaptive tumor evolution [4]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1 blockade) have demonstrated certain efficacy in a subset of GC patients, their effectiveness is frequently compromised by the immune-suppressive environments and aggressive tumor growth in GC [5]. These clinical challenges underscore the urgent need to delve into the molecular drivers of GC and innovate its therapeutic strategies.

  Phase separation (PS) is an essential biophysical mechanism by which proteins and nucleic acids spontaneously partition into distinct liquid phases, forming membraneless organelles termed biomolecular condensates. These condensates exhibit a wide range of biological functions including transcriptional and translational regulation, intracellular signaling cascades [6–8]. Dysregulation of PS can lead to aberrant condensate and amyloid formation, which has been shown to promote tumor initiation and progression, therapy resistance, and immune evasion [9, 10]. YES-associated protein 1 (YAP) forms oncogenic condensates that enhance resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy by modulating immune checkpoint pathways in GC [11]. As an RNA-modifying enzyme, NAT10 has been shown to regulate YTHDF1 splicing through PS-dependent condensate formation, driving the progression of GC [12]. Furthermore, pharmacological disruption of PS has demonstrated promise in suppressing tumor metastasis and overcoming chemotherapy resistance [13, 14]. The biophysical properties of PS condensates can also influence the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of antitumor drugs, offering new avenues for drug design and delivery optimization [15, 16]. These findings collectively underscore PS as a central regulatory node in GC pathogenesis and a promising target for therapeutic intervention. Several studies have developed risk prediction models based on PS-related gene signatures to systematically investigate the diagnostic and therapeutic implications of these molecular features in various malignancies including hepatocellular carcinoma, prostate cancer, and glioma [17–19], no relevant studies have been reported in GC.

  This study developed an effective prognostic model for GC patients based on PS-related gene signatures, which can accurately stratify patients into distinct risk subgroups. High-risk patients exhibited significantly worse clinical outcomes, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, lower tumor mutational burden (TMB), and reduced response to immunotherapy. Meanwhile, we identified several potential natural compounds targeting key genes in this model, which not only provide promising therapeutic options for high-risk GC patients but also offer potential treatment strategies for GC therapy.


## Materials and methods

### Data preparation
  The PS-related datasets were obtained from four publicly available databases: DrLLPS (http://llps.biocuckoo.cn/download.php), PhaSepDB (http://db.phasep.pro/), PhaSePro (https://phasepro.elte.hu/), and LLPSDB (http://bio-comp.org.cn/llpsdb/home.html). Transcriptional expression profiles were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) databases, including the GSE62254 dataset (Asian cancer research group, ACRG, n = 300) and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) data (375 tumor and 32 non-tumor samples).


### Candidate gene screening and PS-related dataset construction
  Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from TCGA-STAD data were identified using the ‘limma’ package, with a|fold change| >1.3 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 set as the significance thresholds. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted using the ‘survival’ package, with log-rank tests and univariate Cox proportional hazard regression employed to evaluate prognostic significance (P < 0.05). To construct the candidate PS-related gene set, PS-related genes, DEGs, and prognosis-related genes were intersected using the ‘Wu Kong’ platform (https://www.omicsolution.com/wkomics/main/).


### PS-related risk model construction and validation
  The prognostic model was constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression with the ‘glmnet’ package, employing an L1 penalty (α = 1) to prioritize sparse selection of PS-related genes. Key parameters included optimization of the regularization strength (λ) via 10-fold cross-validation, a maximum iteration threshold of 1 × 10⁶ to ensure convergence stability in high-dimensional genomic data, and z-score standardization of all features to eliminate scale-dependent bias. External validation cohorts underwent non-negative expression thresholding to align with platform-specific normalization protocols.   Patients were then divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score.

  We compared overall survival (OS) between subgroups through KM survival analysis (log-rank test) and assessed model performance via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Independent prognostic value of the risk score was further confirmed by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis using the ‘survival’ packages. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated clinical net benefit across various risk thresholds. These analyses utilized the ‘pec’, ‘ggDCA’ and ‘survival’ packages.


### Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and TMB
  Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was employed to evaluate biological pathway activities between the high- and low-risk groups, utilizing gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). These gene sets encompass hallmark collections, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and immunologic signatures. The somatic mutation profile for STAD were downloaded from the TCGA database (http://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/). The mutation landscape of candidate PS-related genes was analyzed, including gain-of-function and loss-of-function mutations. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated and compared between the high- and low-risk groups based on the PS-related risk scores. The correlation between TMB and PS-related risk scores was also evaluated. The prognostic significance of both TMB and PS-related risk scores was evaluated through survival analysis employing these R packages: ‘limma’, ‘GSEABase’, ‘GSVA’, ‘pheatmap’, ‘survival’, ‘survminer’, ‘ggpubr’, ‘reshape2’, ‘maftools’, and ‘RCircos’.


### Immune infiltration analysis and immunotherapy
  Single-sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed to quantify infiltration levels of 23 tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations per sample, with subsequent investigation of their association patterns with PS-related risk scores. Expression profiles of 41 chemokines, 18 chemokine receptors, 46 immunostimulators, 24 immunoinhibitors, and 21 major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules were compared between the risk stratification groups. Data on the responsiveness of each TCGA-STAD sample to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy were extracted from the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) server (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu). The following R packages were used for analysis: ‘reshape2’, ‘ggpubr’, ‘limma’, ‘GSEABase’, ‘GSVA’, ‘corrplot’, and ‘ggplot2’.


### Connectivity map (CMap) analysis and small molecule screening
  The CMap (https://clue.io/data) was utilized to identify potential existing drugs targeting the candidate genes and to elucidate their mechanisms of action (MoAs) on relevant molecular pathways using the ‘Query’ model and ‘Touchstone’ mode. Candidate molecular compounds and their corresponding MoAs were matched and visualized on a heatmap using the Wei Sheng Xin online platform (http://113.44.3.163). Interactions between potential PS-associated genes and small molecules were predicted using the HERB database (http://herb.ac.cn/). MoAs for the candidate compounds were further investigated using data from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Selleck (https://www.selleckchem.com/), and MedChemExpress (https://www.medchemexpress.cn/). All interactions were mapped and visualized using Cytoscape version 3.9.0 (http://www.cytoscape.org).


### Molecular docking
  The three-dimensional (3D) structures of potential target proteins were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://www.alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The two-dimensional (2D) structures of small-molecule compounds were downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Blind docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0) with a large docking box that covered the entire protein surface. Parameters were set to ensure comprehensive sampling of potential binding sites. Specifically, the exhaustiveness was set to 8 to enhance search accuracy, and the number of output binding modes was set to 20 [20]. Molecular docking was used to evaluate binding affinities, with docking energy values ≤−6.0 kcal/mol considered as strong binding interactions. All molecular docking results were subjected to three-dimensional visualization and interaction analysis using PyMOL software (version 2.4.0, https://pymol.org/).


### Cell culture and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
  Human GC cell lines (MKN45, AGS, HGC27) were obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Resource at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (http://crcpumc.com/) and the China Center for Type Culture Collection at Wuhan University (http://cctcc.whu.edu.cn). The SGC7901 cell line was kindly gifted by the Gansu University Key Laboratory for Molecular Medicine and the Chinese Medicine Prevention and Treatment of Major Diseases. The human gastric epithelial cell line-1 (GES-1) was purchased from Servicebio Technology (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, NY, USA). cDNA synthesis was conducted using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan), incorporating gDNA Eraser for genomic DNA removal. qPCR amplification was then performed with SYBR Green chemistry (SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ II, Takara, Japan) on a QuantStudio Dx real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were obtained from Harvard PrimerBank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/index.html) and are documented in Additional File 1.


### Statistical analysis
  Differences in relative gene expression levels between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism Software (version 9; San Diego, CA, USA). All other statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Identification and construction of PS-associated gene signatures in GC
  The workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1A. First, we systematically integrated data from four authoritative PS databases. Following removal of duplicates, we obtained a refined set of 3,687 PS-related genes. Subsequently, we performed transcriptomic analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort, identifying two distinct gene sets, 11,141 DEGs and 913 prognosis-associated genes. By intersecting these three datasets, we ultimately derived a candidate PS-related gene set consisting of 78 genes (Fig. 1B).

### Construction of a PS-related risk model in GC
  To establish a robust risk model associated with PS for outcome prediction, we subjected the 78 candidate genes to LASSO regression analysis. This process identified 21 genes based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance criterion (Fig. 1C, D). Among these, 18 genes exhibited upregulated expression, while 3 genes showed downregulated expression (Fig. 1E). The selected genes included ecto-5’-nucleotidase (NT5E), annexin A5 (ANXA5), aldo-keto reductase family 1 member B1 (AKR1B1), musashi 2 (MSI2), lanthionine synthetase C-like protein 2 (LANCL2), glutathione transferase omega 1 (GSTO1), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), glutamate dehydrogenase 2 (GLUD2), synuclein-gamma (SNCG), apolipoprotein A-I (APOA1), small ubiquitin-like modifier 4 (SUMO4), vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 35 (VPS35), clathrin heavy chain-like 1 (CLTCL1), G protein subunit alpha I 1 (GNAI1), von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor (VHL), fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), G protein alpha subunit (GNAS), PTPRF interacting protein alpha 3 (PPFIA3), family with sequence similarity 53 member B (FAM53B), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase kinase 4 (MAP4K4), and cell division cycle 37 (CDC37). The network analysis showed that among the 21 genes in the model, six served as independent protective factors, while the remaining 15 were independent risk factors (Fig. 1F). Individual risk scores were computed by combining prognostic coefficient of each gene (Additional File 2) with its mRNA expression level through the following formula: risk score = (0.1×ANXA5)– (0.195×CDC37)– (0.125×FAM53B) + (0.0113×GLUD2) + (0.025×GNAS) + (0.108×GSTO1) + (0.093×LANCL2) + (1.950×MAP4K4)– (0.376×MSI2)– (0.191×NT5E)– (0.0002×PPFIA3) + (0.009×RUNX2)+ (0.088×SNCG) + (0.224×SUMO4)– (0.046×VHL) + (0.660×VPS35) + (0.220×AKR1B1) + (0.017×APOA1) + (0.005×CLTCL1)– (0.133×FAAH) + (0.0853×GNAI1). Using the median risk score as threshold, patients were stratified into high-risk (n = 167) and low-risk (n = 167) groups. Comparative analysis revealed distinct patterns in risk score distribution, survival status, and gene expression profiles between subgroups (Fig. 1H). This risk stratification system provides a reliable prognostic model for GC, demonstrating the clinical relevance of these PS-related genes in GC progression and outcome prediction.


### Prognostic performance of the PS-related risk model in GC
  To evaluate the prognostic performance of the PS-related risk model, KM survival analysis was performed using both the TCGA-STAD and Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) dataset (GSE62254). In the TCGA-STAD cohort, patients in the high-risk group showed significantly shorter OS compared to those in the low-risk group (P = 6.3 × 10⁻⁹, hazard ratio [HR] = 2.96, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.01–4.34; Fig. 2A). Consistent results were observed in the ACRG cohort, where high-risk patients also exhibited poorer OS (P = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴, HR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.33–2.55; Fig. 3A). The diagnostic accuracy of the PS-related risk model was further validated using ROC analysis. In the TCGA-STAD cohort, the model demonstrated strong predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS, with AUC values of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.69–0.82), 0.78 (95% CI: 0.70–0.87), and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.65–0.91), respectively (Fig. 2B). Although the predictive efficacy of the model was slightly reduced in the- ACRG cohort, it still performed well for 3- and 5-year survival, with AUC values of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.55–0.71) for both time points (Fig. 3B). Cox regression analysis consistently demonstrated the risk score as an independent prognostic indicator for OS in both TCGA and ACRG cohorts (Figs. 2C, D and 3C, D). The analysis showed significant predictive value in univariate models (both P < 0.001), with results remaining robust in multivariate models after adjustment for clinical covariates including age, gender, tumor stage, and histological grade (TCGA-STAD: P < 0.001; ACRG: P = 0.002). Further validation using nomogram analysis with calibration curves demonstrated excellent prediction accuracy (Figs. 2E, F and 3E, F). Time-dependent ROC analysis confirmed consistent prognostic performance across multiple timepoints (Figs. 2G and 3G). DCA provided additional evidence for clinical utility by showing favorable net benefits across various risk thresholds (Figs. 2H and 3H). These findings collectively establish the PS-related risk model as a reliable prognostic tool for GC patients.

### Pathway enrichment and immune microenvironment characterization by PS-related risk stratification
  To investigate the biological characteristics associated with PS-defined high- and low-risk groups, we performed GSVA using data from both TCGA and ACRG cohorts. Hallmark pathway analysis revealed activation of pro-tumorigenic pathways in high-risk patients, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), KRAS signaling, angiogenesis, and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 4A). Further, KEGG and GO analyses identified significant enrichment of pathways related to pyrimidine metabolism, homologous recombination, and DNA repair, along with dysregulation of cytoskeletal remodeling and platelet activation processes in the high-risk group (Figs. 4B, C). These aberrantly activated pathways and biological processes may represent key mechanisms underlying the aggressive tumor behavior and poor clinical outcomes observed in high-risk patients. Immune-related pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that CD8+ T cells in high-risk patients showed more marked exhaustion characteristics, along with significant dysregulation in T cell development/activation signaling pathways (Fig. 4D). These findings collectively establish the PS-risk model as a comprehensive molecular framework connecting oncogenic pathway activation, genomic instability, and immune microenvironment remodeling with aggressive GC progression.

### Immune landscape and ICI therapy response by PS-related risk stratification in GC
  To comprehensively characterize the immune microenvironment in GC, we performed ssGSEA to quantify the infiltration levels of 23 tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations across high- and low- risk subgroups. Our results revealed that high-risk patients exhibited significantly greater immune cell infiltration than low-risk patients, with marked enrichment of immunosuppressive subsets, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (P < 0.001; Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis further confirmed strong associations between high-risk scores and immunosuppressive populations, particularly immature dendritic cells, immature B cells, and Tregs (Fig. 5B), reinforcing the link between risk stratification and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Next, we systematically evaluated differential expression of MHC molecules, chemokines, chemokine receptors, immunostimulators, immunoinhibitors between risk subgroups. The results showed that high-risk patients exhibited significant upregulation of MHC molecules (TAP1, TAP2, TAPBP, and B2M; Fig. 5C), indicating enhanced antigen presentation. We also observed coordinated overexpression of chemokines (CCL2, CCL5, CCL7, CCL8, CCL17, and CCL19) and their receptors (CXCR4, CCR1, CCR2, and CCR5; Figs. 5D, E), consistent with active immune cell recruitment. Although elevated expression of several immunostimulators (TNFRSF9, ICOSLG, ICOS, and CD70) was detected (Fig. 5F), the immunological profile was overwhelmingly dominated by upregulated immunosuppressive checkpoints (VTCN1, TIGIT, TGFBR1, TGFB1, PDCD1LG2, KIR2DL3, CSF1R, and CD96; Fig. 5G), revealing a markedly immunosuppressive TME. This immune landscape favors immune evasion and tumor progression in high-risk GC patients.

  The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionized contemporary cancer treatment paradigms. Our TIDE analysis revealed significantly higher TIDE score in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group (P < 0.001, Fig. 5H), suggesting enhanced immune evasion capacity and a potentially diminished response to ICI treatment. Given the critical role of microsatellite instability (MSI) status in cancer treatment, we conducted a comparative analysis of risk scores across MSI subgroups. Notably, tumors with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) exhibited significantly lower risk scores than other subtypes (P = 1.5 × 10−6), consistent with their established sensitivity to immunotherapy. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between microsatellite stable (MSS) and low-level microsatellite instability (MSI-L) groups (P = 0.86, Fig. 5I). These collective observations substantiate that high-risk patients predominantly characterized by MSS/MSI-L status, show substantially reduced responsiveness to immunotherapy.

### Genetic variation landscape of PS-related risk model genes
  To comprehensively characterize the genetic alterations associated with PS-related risk model genes, we analyzed both the somatic mutation landscape and copy number variations (CNVs) across the 21 PS-related genes in TCGA-STAD cohort. 75 cases harbored mutations in 433 samples (17.32%), with GNAS showing the highest mutation frequency at 4% (Fig. 6A). CNV analysis demonstrated generally low CNV rates among the model genes, while RUNX2, GNAS, AKR1B1, and SNCG showed higher CNV gains or losses, suggesting potential unique genomic instability at these loci (Fig. 6B, C). We further analyzed the effects of gene mutations on their own expression levels. The results demonstrated that only VPS35 and FAM53B expression was affected by their corresponding mutations (Additional File 3). TMB analysis showed that the low-risk group had significant higher TMB compared to the high-risk group (P = 1.0 × 10−8, Fig. 6D). Furthermore, TMB showed a significant negative correlation with the risk scores (r = − 0.35, P = 1.0 × 10−10, Fig. 6E). Integrative survival analysis combining risk scores and TMB assessment revealed that patients with both high TMB and low-risk scores had significantly longer OS compared to all other subgroups (P < 0.001; Fig. 6F). These findings highlight the interplay between genetic variations and risk stratification, indicating that the combination of high TMB and low-risk scores represents a favorable prognostic marker in GC.

### Drug repurposing and identification of small-molecule compounds targeting key proteins in PS-related risk model
  To identify potential therapeutic agents targeting the PS-related risk model genes, we performed a CMap analysis to screen existing drugs capable of modulating the expression of candidate genes. Our analysis revealed multiple drugs with the potential to reverse the expression patterns of genes in the PS-related risk model (Fig. 7A). To further explore the therapeutic implications, we performed a drug sensitivity analysis using the CellMiner database. Notably, AKR1B1 expression showed a positive correlation with the IC50 value of imatinib, while CDC37 expression was positively associated with the IC50 of 5-fluorodeoxyuridine (Additional File 4), suggesting that elevated expression of these genes may reduce the sensitivity of GC cells to conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapies, potentially contributing to drug resistance.

  We further utilized the HERB database to screen for potential small molecules targeting the genes in the risk model. The results identified 18 potential small molecules targeting 13 genes (Fig. 7B). The specific results are as follows: adenosine (GNAS and NT5E), apigenin (CDC37), berberine (BBR, target: ANXA5), capsaicin (SUMO4), celastrol (CTL; targets: ANXA5 and CDC37), coumarin (GNAI1), coumestrol (GNAI1, MAP4K4, and NT5E), curcumin (AKR1B1), daidzein (APOA1), emodin (EMD; target: RUNX2), gallic acid (APOA1), lupeol (AKR1B1), oleic acid (APOA1 and FAAH), progesterone (FAAH), quercetin (GLUD2, GNAI1, NT5E, and VHL), resveratrol (RES; target: RUNX2), salicylic acid (APOA1), and serotonin (GNAI1 and GNAS). These small molecules exhibit antitumor properties through mechanisms such as anti-proliferation, anti-inflammation, and immune homeostasis regulation (Fig. 7B).

## Discussion
  We successfully developed a novel risk stratification model for GC based on PS-related signature genes. This model effectively classifies GC patients into two distinct subgroups with significantly different immune cell infiltration patterns, immunotherapy responsiveness, TMB, and prognosis. Furthermore, we identified several promising existing drugs and natural bioactive compounds that specifically target key components of the risk model. These findings not only advance understanding of PS-mediated mechanisms in GC but also provide a robust framework for developing effective and personalized therapeutic strategies.

  GC demonstrates marked intratumoral heterogeneity, with tumor cell subpopulations and the immune microenvironment synergistically driving disease progression and therapy resistance [21]. Accumulating evidence highlights the central role of PS in driving tumor progression, remodeling immune microenvironment dynamics, and modulating therapeutic responses [22–24]. In contrast to conventional prediction models based on lipid metabolism-associated or angiogenesis-associated genes that primarily reflect downstream molecular events [25, 26], PS represents a biologically fundamental event that precedes the regulation of gene expression. Consequently, the establishment of PS-related risk model contribute to earlier mechanistic insights and prognostic assessment in GC.

  The PS-associated signature genes in the model demonstrate functional convergence with classical oncogenic pathways in GC and other malignancies [27–29]. Previous studies reveal that NT5E drives tumor progression through promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition and inducing CD8-positive T cell exhaustion [30]. RUNX2 coordinates microenvironment remodeling through transcriptional regulation of EGFR signaling [31]. ANXA5 enhances immunotherapy resistance via angiogenesis [32]. VPS35 promotes GC proliferation and metastasis through integrin-FAK-SRC signaling cascades and EGFR recycling [33, 34]. Moreover, PPFIA3 [35], SNCG [36], and APOA1 exhibit potential diagnostic value in both tumor tissues and serum [37]. GLUD2 plays a key role in several risk model [38]. GNAI1 participates in competing endogenous RNA network regulation [39]. The PS-associated risk model integrates multiple genes to comprehensively investigate the underlying mechanisms of GC pathogenesis and therapeutic response, offering a more holistic understanding compared to conventional single-gene approaches and providing a biophysical foundation for precision oncology strategies.

  Natural small molecules constitute a primary source of anticancer therapeutics, with compounds including berberine (BBR) [40], celastrol (CTL), resveratrol (RES), quercetin, curcumin, and apigenin demonstrating multimodal antitumor activity across various malignancies [41]. Published studies have demonstrated that CTL derivatives disrupt oncogenic signaling by interfering with Cdc37 expression and Cdc37-Hsp90/Hsp70 complex formation [42]. Apigenin induces myeloma cell apoptosis via CK2, CDC37, and HSP90 inhibition [43], while quercetin suppresses NT5E activity in bladder cancer and modulates UBE2S/VHL pathways in cervical cancer [44, 45]. RES displays context-dependent regulation of β-catenin and RUNX2, with opposing effects on osteogenic transdifferentiation under differential TNF-β conditions [46, 47]. Emodin demonstrates antiangiogenic and antimetastatic activity through RUNX2 inhibition in breast cancer [48], and Curcumin demonstrates multifunctional antitumor effects in GC, including angiogenesis suppression, proliferation inhibition, metastasis blockade, and apoptosis induction, with additional anti-Helicobacter pylori activity [49]. Its derivative PGV-1 shows promise in glioblastoma treatment by targeting AKR1B1 and overcoming bevacizumab resistance [50]. Additional compounds including daidzein (APOA1 activation) [51], progesterone (endocannabinoid regulation via FAAH interaction) [52], and coumestrol (multipathway regulation in breast cancer) [53], also demonstrate anticancer efficacy. These findings provide indirect validation for the efficacy and accuracy of the screening methodology employed in our study.

  The polygenic risk stratification model developed in this study demonstrates robust predictive performance for GC prognosis. However, the requirement of the model for coordinated modulation of multiple molecular targets presents significant challenges to conventional single-target drug development paradigms. Notably, current research has validated that traditional Chinese medicine formulations can exert anti-tumor effects through multi-component synergistic mechanisms [54]. This network pharmacology approach shows high compatibility with the pathobiological complexity of polygenic risk models, thereby providing a novel paradigm for developing multi-target therapeutic regimens and demonstrating potential clinical application value in GC treatment.


## Limitations
  The study has several limitations. Firstly, while the current model effectively identifies PS-associated gene signatures, it does not incorporate critical regulatory mechanisms such as post-translational modifications or RNA-protein interactions. Future studies should employ artificial intelligence to integrate multi-omics data to enhance diagnostic performance, with subsequent validation in population cohorts. Secondly, the therapeutic potential of the identified natural compounds targeting PS-related genes requires preclinical validation through pharmacokinetic optimization and comprehensive toxicity assessment using patient-derived orthotopic models. Such investigations are crucial for translating computational findings into clinically applicable precision oncology strategies for GC.


## Conclusions
  The PS-associated risk model developed in this study establishes a clinically actionable biomarker stratification system by systematically delineating subgroup-specific immune profiles, mutational signatures, and survival outcomes in GC patients. This model not only provides mechanistic insights into GC malignant progression but also offers a novel theoretical foundation and therapeutic strategies for optimizing personalized combination treatment regimens in clinical practice.
