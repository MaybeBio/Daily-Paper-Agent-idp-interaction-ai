# Hidden protein disorder: Deciphering the structural organisation and dynamics of a non‐canonical CP12 from the diatom Thalassiosira pseudonana


## Abstract
  The chloroplastic protein CP12 from the diatom Thalassiosira pseudonana exhibits atypical and enigmatic structural properties that have so far hindered our understanding of its functions. Here, we used AlphaFold to generate a three‐dimensional (3D) model of the structure of the protein. However, this model did not accurately describe the small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) data previously obtained. We have therefore undertaken a study using site‐directed spin labelling combined with electron paramagnetic resonance (SDSL‐EPR) to characterise the structural dynamics of this atypical CP12 and to investigate its dimeric organisation using double electron–electron resonance (DEER). We then performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, constrained by SAXS and DEER data, to refine the AlphaFold model and take into account the flexibility and disordered propensities of this protein. The combination of the experimental techniques together with the in silico AlphaFold and MD simulations reveals that the dimer is organised in an antiparallel arrangement of each monomer and that the C‐terminal regions are highly flexible and partly disordered. Additionally, this diatom CP12 contains four structured domains likely to bind phosphoribulokinase regardless of their redox state. Our structural data therefore provide insights into the function of this protein in the regulation of the Calvin cycle and of photosynthesis in diatoms, whereas its structural organisation is completely different from any of its homologous counterparts from Plantae and cyanobacteria.


## Introduction
  The chloroplastic protein CP12 is a ubiquitous protein in photosynthetic organisms [1, 2, 3]. This 80 amino acid, nuclear‐encoded protein has been described in Plantae and cyanobacteria as a conditionally disordered protein, whose disordered/folded states oscillate upon the day/night cycle and the redox conditions [4, 5, 6, 7]. As many intrinsically disordered proteins [8] CP12 is a moonlighting protein with several functions [1]. The most documented one is the regulation of the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle, responsible for CO2 assimilation. During the night, when oxidised, CP12 is partly folded [9] and sequesters two key enzymes of this cycle, glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and phosphoribulokinase (PRK) by forming an inactive ternary complex. Alternatively, in light conditions, CP12 is reduced and fully disordered, and the ternary complex is dissociated [10, 11, 12]. Many other functions are suspected for CP12, as deletion mutants of CP12 in various photosynthetic organisms lead to very different and unexpected phenotypes [13, 14, 15].

  One of the hallmarks of CP12 is a highly conserved motif AWD_VEEL together with one and sometimes two pairs of cysteine residues, one in the N‐terminus and the second one in the C‐terminus [2, 3]. Despite the ubiquitous presence of CP12, very few studies could identify a CP12 from diatoms, and only three reports are available. Two reports mention a CP12 from the fresh water pennate diatom Asterionella formosa [16, 17]. A third report accounts for a new CP12 from the marine centric diatom Thalassiosira pseudonana [18]. Diatoms are ecologically widespread and constitute the major component and the most diverse group of phytoplankton. They are responsible for 40% of marine primary productivity and as such, are major actors of the biological carbon pump. They have a complex evolutionary history, in particular with their chloroplast that originates from a second endosymbiosis with a red microalga [19]. Regulation of the CBB cycle thus significantly differs in diatoms compared to other photosynthetic organisms and remains enigmatic [20, 21].

  A first study on a T. pseudonana CP12 protein (XP_002286772, henceforth noted tp‐CP12) [18] showed that this CP12 is overexpressed when the diatom is submitted to stress conditions such as low CO2 or nutrients, and its expression level is decoupled from the expression levels of GAPDH and PRK and from dark/light conditions. Furthermore, it exhibits unusual properties. Contrary to all other CP12 studied so far, this one is twice as long as other CP12 (163 vs 80 amino acids in C. reinhardtii for example), contains two homologous copies of the motifs AWE_VEEL, and a single pair of cysteine residues located at the C‐terminus. More intriguingly, unlike all other CP12, it is dimeric with a putative coiled‐coil in the middle of the sequence. This CP12 always elutes at a single peak on size‐exclusion chromatography column, indicating that the dimeric state is the only stable form and that the dimerisation constant is very high. NMR data suggest that it also contains disordered regions, or at least dynamic interconverting helices, that are stabilised by trifluoroethanol. In addition, according to small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS), its structure occupies a rather compact cylindrical shape with kinks. For all these reasons, T. pseudonana CP12 appears to be atypical and needs to be further investigated.

  With the recent advances in artificial intelligence, combined with experimental approaches [22, 23], deciphering the detailed structural properties of this odd CP12 is now within reach. An open question concerns its dimeric organisation, in particular the orientation of the unusual coiled‐coil domain (parallel or antiparallel) and the structural dynamics of some regions.

  To address such a question, site‐directed spin labelling combined with Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (SDSL‐EPR) spectroscopy is the technique of choice. It is based on the selective grafting of paramagnetic spin labels (mainly nitroxide radical derivatives) classically on cysteine residues followed by EPR analyses. Room temperature (rt) continuous wave (cw) EPR spectral shapes of nitroxide‐labelled proteins are sensitive to molecular motion that contains the contributions of both the local dynamics of the spin label side chain and the global motion of the entire protein (non‐negligible for proteins of MW < 50 kDa which is the case for the dimeric T. pseudonana CP12 of 39 kDa). The latter contribution being constant for a given protein, nitroxide spin labels are described as ideal reporters of their local environment, and the technique is well‐suited to reveal local structural properties [24, 25, 26]. Complementarily, distances measurement between spin pairs (here nitroxide‐nitroxide) by pulsed EPR is nowadays an established technique, most often obtained by double electron–electron resonance (DEER) experiments [27, 28]. Even though well‐defined distances can be obtained in some cases, most of the time DEER experiments lead to distance distributions, allowing regions of conformational flexibility to be explored [29]. The typical range of distance lies in the range of 1.8–6.0 nm, even though longer distances have been measured on perdeuterated samples [30].

  Starting from the AlphaFold prediction of tp‐CP12, we combined SDSL‐EPR and SAXS data together with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to bring a reliable view of the structural and dynamic properties of the dimeric state of this atypical CP12. We demonstrated the antiparallel organisation of the coiled‐coil region as initially proposed by the AlphaFold model. On the other hand, we highlighted the high flexibility of the C‐terminal regions contrary to the AlphaFold prediction. Altogether, our results provide the first structural insights on the function of this CP12 in the regulation of the CBB cycle in diatoms whereas its structural organisation drastically differs from its homologous counterparts from Plantae and cyanobacteria.


## Results

### AlphaFold2 model
  AlphaFold2 (AF2) was used to predict the homodimeric structure of tp‐CP12. While this manuscript was being written, AlphaFold3 was released, and we also ran it on tp‐CP12. It provided the same overall predictions as AlphaFold2. This gave us confidence to keep using the AF2 models in all our subsequent analyses. The top five dimer models are shown in Fig. S1B, including mostly elongated forms (models 1, 3–5) and a triangular one where the lateral domains are folded onto the central domain (model 2). As SAXS data revealed that the tp‐CP12 dimer adopts an elongated cylindrical form with kinks [18], we selected a representative model that best fitted into the SAXS shape, which we henceforth call AF2‐CP12 (Fig. 1A). Overall, the helical regions were modelled with high confidence, except for the C‐terminal helices and some parts of the second helices. On the other hand, the random coils, potentially disordered regions, were modelled with low confidence. For this model, the estimated percentages of the secondary structure elements using YASARA View [31] were 79.1% helix, 2.5% turn, 18.4% random coil and 0% β sheet. The α‐helical content is higher compared to what was reported from deconvolution of circular dichroism spectra (32–50%) [18] suggesting that in solution the protein contains more disordered or flexible segments than the model. The coiled‐coil domain spans residues 46–82 (among them 46–75 predicted with high prediction confidence), consistent with the 46–82 predicted coiled‐coil sequence [18]. For the sake of clarity, the AF2‐CP12 model and its schematic representations are shown in Fig. 1A,B using a rainbow colour code for each monomer (from the N‐terminus in blue to the C‐terminus in magenta). It is noticeable that the central coiled‐coil (green helices) is antiparallel and that the two C‐terminal helices (magenta helices) are densely packed onto the central coiled‐coil domain, forming a bundle of four helices. This antiparallel organisation of the coiled‐coil region given by AF2 is in contradiction with the Swiss‐Model model, which was parallel [18]. Figure 1C shows the theoretical scattering curve calculated from the AF2‐CP12 model and its comparison with the experimental SAXS data previously published [18]. The calculated curve exhibits a poor fit, in particular in the low and medium q region (χ 2 = 29.9).

  In conclusion, even though the elongated AF2‐CP12 models appear to be in rather good accordance with former experimental data, the model undoubtedly needs to be refined by considering the suspected flexibility of the protein. Furthermore, considering the discrepancy between the AF2 and the Swiss‐Model coiled‐coil models, experimental evidence is also needed to assess the parallel or antiparallel orientation of the coiled‐coil. To address these questions, we have therefore undertaken a study based on site‐directed spin labelling (SDSL) EPR spectroscopy, which we have then combined with molecular dynamics analysis.


### Local dynamics probed by rt cw‐EPR
  To investigate the structural and dynamic properties of the homodimer, in particular in the coiled‐coil region, we used nitroxide spin labels grafted at different positions. We designed five tp‐CP12 variants so that each of them contains a single cysteine residue per monomer at a chosen position. In four of these variants, the single Cys was within or at the putative borders of the predicted coiled‐coil domain: at positions 39, 46, 56 and 83. The choice of the labelling sites was driven by the Ser to Cys substitution to minimise the structural impact of the mutation. As the WT tp‐CP12 contains two natural cysteines at positions 142 and 150 (Fig. S1A), triple mutations were thus introduced: S39C‐C142S‐C150S, S46C‐C142S‐C150S, S56C‐C142S‐C150S and S83C‐C142S‐C150S respectively. The simple mutant C142S, that contains a single cysteine residue at position 150, was used to specifically probe the dynamics of the C‐terminal region. Covalent attachment of the MTSL spin label (1‐oxyl‐2,2,5,5‐tetramethylpyrroline‐3‐methyl) methanethiosulfonate at each single sulfhydryl group per monomer (R1 side chain) allowed us to analyse the local dynamics of the label at each site. Each corresponding variant is named hereafter S39R1, S46R1, S56R1, S83R1 and C150R1 (Fig. 2A).

  X‐band cw‐EPR spectra of all tp‐CP12 MTSL‐labelled variants were recorded individually and their spectral shapes were simulated using SimLabel [32] (Fig. 2). Spectral simulation is commonly used to decompose each spectrum into potentially multiple components and to extract for each of them the rotational correlation time τ  c  and proportions of populations. Sharp EPR spectral shapes (corresponding to low τ  c ) indicate a high mobility of the grafted nitroxide label while broad spectral shapes (corresponding to high τ  c ) are related to a restricted mobility of the label. For each tp‐CP12 variant spectrum, two components were required to reach satisfactory simulation indicating different environments experienced by the label (Fig. 2B,C, Fig. S2). This result highlights the coexistence of different dynamic states of the nitroxide side chain. Even though one cannot directly attribute an EPR spectral shape to a specific secondary structure, it is worth noting that the coexistence of different dynamic states is frequently observed when nitroxides are grafted onto α‐helical main‐chain [33, 34, 35]. For tp‐CP12, this observation is furthermore consistent with CD data displaying a high content of α‐helical structures and NMR data showing the presence of dynamic structural elements [18]. The highest mobility of the spin label is encountered for S39R1 located downstream of the predicted coiled‐coil region. The spectral shape is close to the ones observed for highly flexible systems or disordered regions with τ  c  values ≤ 1.0 ns [36] and is consistent with the presence of a loop in the AF2 model. For S46R1 and S83R1 located on both sides of the coiled‐coil, the mobility of the spin labels is rather high with the broadest component having τ  c  values slightly above 1.0 ns, consistent with positions at the external limits of the coiled‐coil proposed by the AF2 model. On the other hand, the MTSL spin label has a restricted mobility (major component with a τ  c  value of 3.3 ns) at position S56R1, which is in the middle of the predicted coiled‐coil. Similarly, in the C‐terminal region, C150R1 has also a restricted mobility with its major components having a τ  c  value of 2.6 ns. Altogether, the experimental cw‐EPR analyses are globally consistent with the different structural elements proposed by the AF2 model going from high mobility within loops to more restricted mobilities within the predicted α‐helical segments.


### Dimer organisation probed by DEER measurements
  DEER experiments were performed to measure inter‐label distance distributions in each labelled tp‐CP12 variant. This method is indeed key to determine the orientation of a coiled‐coil: in antiparallel homodimers, labels along a coiled‐coil show distances between homologous spin pairs that come closer and then further apart [37, 38], whereas for parallel organisation the distances between labels remain similar and relatively short for any position [39].

  We first computed the inter‐label distance distributions for the AF2 model by using mmm 2019 software [40] to evaluate rotamers populated by the spin labels (Fig. 3A). As the AF2 model predicts an antiparallel coiled‐coil, the predicted inter‐label distance distributions are getting shorter from position 39, 43 to 56 and then become longer for position 83. Interestingly, as the two C‐termini of each monomer are very close to each other in the AF2 model, the predicted inter‐label distance distribution between the two C150 residues is very narrow and centred at the short distance of 1.6 nm.

  DEER inter‐label distance distributions measured on each labelled tp‐CP12 variant were then compared to the predicted ones (Fig. 3B,C, Fig. S3 for the raw data and Fig. S4 for relaxation data). In the region encompassing the predicted coiled‐coil (aa 46–82), inter‐label distance distributions reveal that the labels are getting closer from residues 39, 46 and 56 and then further apart for residue 83. More quantitatively, if we reduce the distance distribution to the maximum of the major peak, the distance evolution goes from 6.8 nm (position 39), 4.3 nm (position 46), 3.2 nm (position 56) to 6.4 nm (position 83). This experimental observation validates the antiparallel organisation of the homodimer predicted by AlphaFold2.

  However, when comparing with the MMM calculations, several discrepancies appear. For positions 39, 46 and 56, the experimental distances are significantly shorter than the predicted ones, whereas on the contrary, for position 83, the maximum of the experimental distances is longer. Moreover, the distance distribution for S56R1 is much broader compared to the computed one. These differences between experimental and computed distances reveal that, although the homodimer is indeed antiparallel, the structural organisation between each monomer differs significantly from the AF2 model. Interestingly, for position C150 at the C‐terminal end, the discrepancy between the experiment and the model is the most obvious. The experimental distance distribution is very broad, covering a large range between 2 to 4 nm. This range expands to long distances beyond the domain of confidence of the experiment and could potentially be larger to higher undetectable distances. This observation is in contradiction with the C‐terminal parts folded back on the coiled‐coil region proposed by the AF2 model (see Fig. 1 schematic magenta cylinders). As for SAXS, DEER data demonstrate here conformational discrepancies between the structure in solution and the AF2 model.


### Molecular dynamic simulations
  The discrepancies observed between the experimental SAXS and DEER results and the AF2 model prompted us to further refine the model using restrained MD simulation techniques combining both DEER and SAXS data as constraints.

  We started the structural refinement using classical MD simulations. However, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity and the high conformational flexibility that was revealed by DEER could not be efficiently sampled by our preliminary calculations with conventional MD simulations with constant temperature, constant pressure ensemble, even at a temperature higher than room temperature. In particular, the large experimental distances between residues C150 in the two C‐termini could not be reached by MD simulations in a reasonable computational time because the C‐terminal helices were too packed against the coiled‐coil domain. We therefore manually modified the AF2 model by moving away the two C‐termini from the coiled‐coil region to increase the distances between the C‐termini as indicated by the DEER results (Fig. S5). This modified model was then subjected to all‐atom harmonic restrained MD simulations without spin labels followed by restrained ensemble MD (reMD) simulations with spin labels, to obtain a realistic conformational ensemble that fit both the experimental SAXS curve and interlabel distance distributions derived from DEER experiments. This strategy has proven successful for MD simulations of DEER data [41, 42, 43, 44].

  From five independent reMD simulations, we calculated the distance distributions between each pair of residues at positions 39, 46, 56, 83 and 150 and compared them with experimental DEER data (Fig. 4A,B). For S39R1, S83R1 and C150R1 spin pairs, the simulated distributions overlapped well with the experimental distributions, whereas we observed only partial overlaps for S46R1 and S56R1. We notice that the experimental distance distribution for the S56R1 spin pair is broader than the simulated one. The simulated distance distributions of S46R1 and of S56R1 spin pairs are shifted towards shorter and larger distances respectively (~ 1 nm) compared to the experimental ones. Residue S56 is located in the middle of the predicted coiled‐coil, and S46 is at the border of the coiled‐coil. This therefore suggests that the coiled‐coil domain adopts a slightly different conformation than in the simulated model. Finally, the bimodal distance distribution measured for S46R1 is missing in the simulated distance distribution.

  For a comprehensive evaluation on the reMD sampling structures, we extracted a total of 20 000 structures from the five parallel trajectories of reMD to represent the ensemble of structures populated by the dynamical protein. We confronted the average scattering curve computed from this ensemble of structures to the experimental SAXS data. The average radius of gyration of this pool is 38.2 Å, equal to the experimental value (38.2 ± 0.4 Å) unlike the Rg of the initial AF2 model (35.7 Å). In addition, the average scattering curve of this structure pool much better simulates the experimental scattering curve (χ 2 = 14.9) than the individual initial AF2 model (χ 2 = 29.9) (Fig. 4C). The distribution of radius of gyration (Rg) of these 20 000 sampled structures displays three major peaks at 36.8 Å, 37.6 Å and 39.4 Å (Fig. 5A). A representative model of each peak is shown on Fig. 5B. In these three models, the respective orientations of the three domains are slightly different. The C‐terminal extremities just after residue C150 are disordered and only a short unstable α‐helical segment remains from the two well‐folded long α‐helices of the C‐terminal extremities of the initial AF2 model. The linker containing residue S39 (blue spheres) is also disordered on a longer sequence than in the AF2 model. In the three models, one of the helices forming the central coiled‐coil exhibits a bend around residue S56 (green spheres) as a hairpin that was not present in the initial AF2 model (Fig. S6). This hairpin is dubious, because simulated distances between these residues are shifted compared to the experimental DEER distances. Furthermore, the mobility of the S56R1 side chain is described as the most constrained by cw‐EPR experiments. This probably inaccurate hairpin in the models could also explain the final discrepancies between the SAXS experimental data and the calculated average scattering curve of the reMD ensemble. However, this translates the probable dynamics of the coiled‐coil itself.

  Altogether, even though not perfectly adjusted to the experimental data, the strategy developed by using MD simulations has brought a significant improvement in the refinement of the tp‐CP12 dimer model.


## Discussion
  In this report, we provide the first structure of a CP12 protein from a diatom, thanks to a unique combination of experimental techniques, EPR and SAXS, and in silico methods: AlphaFold prediction and MD simulations. This strategy also allowed us to describe the local and global dynamics of the protein along its sequence. While AlphaFold prediction provided us a template for the structure of tp‐CP12 with no experimental structural homologue on the full sequence, MD allowed us to refine this model by using our experimental data from SDSL‐EPR DEER distance distributions as constraints. The final model, or more precisely its ensemble of conformations, is consistent with all our other experimental data gathered so far. The fit of the SAXS data was significantly improved by considering this ensemble of conformations compared to the single AlphaFold model. NMR data [18] revealed that tp‐CP12 undergoes conformational exchanges at timescales ranging up to the ms, with unstable secondary structures. Indeed, the linkers between the folded domains exhibit very different conformations in the structure ensemble, and the long C‐terminal helix predicted by AlphaFold is actually mostly unfolded. The percentage of α‐helices in the representative models shown in Fig. 5B drops to ~ 50%, now in accordance with our previous CD data [18], unlike the initial AF2 model.

  Using DEER we could unambiguously determine the antiparallel orientation of the coiled‐coil. The exact respective position and conformation of the two helices in this coiled‐coil is, however, not very well modelled here, as shown by the fit of the DEER distances by reMD for residues 46 and 56. This might also explain the discrepancies in the fit to the SAXS data. Interestingly, the two helical branches of the coiled‐coil are not symmetric and exhibit slightly different conformations in the final models obtained by reMD simulations. This coiled‐coil domain is probably loose and unstable, and its multiple conformations could not be sufficiently sampled by our reMD process. It is, however, not critical to understand the structural organisation of this diatom CP12.

  Altogether, our results show that AlphaFold is a very good tool to provide a template for the structure of a protein with no structural homologue. However, when these proteins are oligomeric, flexible, with unstable regions, it needs experimental information to refine the proposed structure [45]. The putative flexibility of the protein is suggested by AlphaFold through the low confidence scores [46], but it provides no information on the conformational space explored by the protein. As reported here, a combination of experimental techniques such as EPR and SAXS, with MD adequately fills this gap.

  The characteristic feature of CP12 proteins is a conserved motif AWD_VEEL. The sequence of CP12 from T. pseudonana contains two of these motifs per monomer with slight variations: LWD_VEEM in the N‐terminus and AWE_VEEI in the C‐terminus. In Plantae and cyanobacteria, these motifs are embedded in an unstable helical bundle: the helices are disordered in reduced conditions and are stabilised by a disulphide bridge connecting the two helices in oxidised conditions [7, 9, 11]. In T. pseudonana, these helical bundles do not contain any cysteine residue and are probably rather stable even without a disulphide bridge to stabilise them: they indeed keep their structure upon MD processes, unlike other less stable parts of the protein, such as the coiled‐coil domain and the two C‐termini.

  In Plantae and cyanobacteria, this helical bundle binds to the active site cleft of PRK, thus inhibiting the enzyme. As for T. pseudonana, the helical bundle of CP12 from some cyanobacteria and red algae does not contain any cysteine residue. Interestingly, for the S. elongatus PCC7942 and Galderia sulphuraria, the interaction between CP12 and PRK has been shown and involves the conserved helical bundle of CP12 [6, 47]. This suggests that interaction with PRK is possible with a helix bundle that does not require a disulphide bridge to stabilise its folded active conformation, and it may be also the case for T. pseudonana CP12.

  PRK activity is differently regulated in diatoms than in other photosynthetic organisms, and in particular, PRK is not redox‐regulated. Furthermore, the ternary complex CP12‐GAPDH‐PRK has never been identified so far in diatoms [17, 48]. Other important functions for CP12 have been proposed, one of which is to stabilise PRK in conditions where the CBB is active [49, 50], possibly through weak and transient PRK‐CP12 interaction, irrespective of the redox conditions [49].

  We thus hypothesise that such interaction might exist in diatoms, but has not been detected because of its transient nature. Despite our effort, we could not produce and purify T. pseudonana PRK. We therefore modelled its structure with AlphaFold. As shown in Fig. 6, its active site cleft can accommodate the corresponding two helix bundles of its cognate tp‐CP12 with either the LWD_VEEM or the AWE_VEEI motifs, as in the CP12‐PRK complex of the other organisms. The dimeric CP12 from T. pseudonana therefore contains four domains likely to bind PRK. A possible advantage of this redundancy could be an increase in the local concentration of the PRK‐binding domain in the CP12 to compensate for a possible low‐affinity interaction. Further experimental evidence is required to analyse the stability of these helix bundles and to characterise the conditions and the properties of the interaction between CP12 and PRK, in order to decipher the mechanisms of regulation of this enzyme in diatoms. SDSL‐EPR experiments targeting these domains would be a method of choice for such investigation.

  All in all, our results strongly suggest that tp‐CP12 is able to interact with PRK, regardless of the redox conditions, and provide clues on the possible functions of CP12 in diatoms.

  In addition, CP12 is known to bind and inhibit GAPDH in oxidised conditions, thus avoiding excessive NADPH consumption during the night. This interaction notably involves a highly conserved CxxxPxxxC region that folds in a conserved U‐turn fold when the disulphide bridge is formed [51], as well as a C‐terminal motif YDx. The unique cysteine pair in T. pseudonana CP12 is in the disordered C‐terminal region but does not encompass the conserved proline residue, and the C‐terminal motif is absent. Considering the disordered properties of the C‐terminus, these two cysteine residues may form a disulphide bridge (Fig. S7). However, this region is likely to undergo an induced folding upon oxidation or upon binding to GAPDH. Therefore, one cannot infer at this stage any role of the disordered C‐terminus and its pair of cysteine residues in a putative interaction with GAPDH or another partner. A ternary complex made of GAPDH, ferredoxin‐NADP reductase (FNR) and a putative CP12 has been observed in the diatom A. formosa [16], but one cannot conclude from our data on the possible mechanisms of interaction between CP12 and GAPDH from diatoms.

  To try to decipher further the putative function(s) of this diatom CP12 from its structure, we used Foldseek [52] to look for structural homologues in the Protein Data Bank or in databases of structure predictions. When we provided as input one of the final models of reMD simulations, only two structural homologues were found in the PDB: the cyanobacterial CP12 in complex with GAPDH (6gfo.pdb), and the cyanobacterial fusion protein CBS‐CP12 (5npl.pdb). The probability of homology is very low because only a short sequence is concerned (~ 35 residues of the helix bundle around the CP12 motif). When we used AF2 as input, Foldseek then provides several structural homologues (Table S1). The ten best homologues with a very high probability (1.0) are ‘hypothetical protein’ or ‘uncharacterised protein’ all from microalgae including a majority of diatoms. They all possess the typical motif of CP12, and their structure is indeed very similar to tp‐CP12, with only different conformations of the flexible linkers between the domains. The following best homologues include bacterial membrane proteins with only the coiled‐coil domain in common with tp‐CP12, whereas tp‐CP12 is not predicted as a membrane protein, and we therefore do not consider them. All the best structural homologues arose from AlphaFold predictions, and not from experimental structural characterisations, which means that so far, CP12 from T. pseudonana is the first representative of a structure experimentally characterised. Considering the exhaustive AlphaFold predictions of known protein sequences [53], it also means that diatom CP12s possess a unique tertiary and quaternary fold not found elsewhere in the living world. The structural features of diatom CP12 versus plant, microalgal and cyanobacterial CP12 might explain at least in part their different regulation, and this needs to be further investigated.


## Materials and methods

### Chemicals
  Unless otherwise noted, all chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade and used without further purification.


### Protein expression and purification
  Synthetic genes of tp‐CP12 mutants were designed based on the tp‐CP12 WT cDNA used in [18] with mutations at the appropriate positions coding for cysteine or serine residues. Each synthetic gene was inserted into a pET‐28a vector between the NdeI and BamHI restriction sites by Genecust (GeneCust, Ellange, Luxembourg). The gene is thus preceded by a sequence coding for a N‐terminal His‐tag followed by a thrombin cleavage site. Escherichia coli strains BL21‐C41(DE3) were transformed with each resulting plasmid and then grown in 2YT medium with 30 μg·mL−1 kanamycin as described in [18]. Purification of the His‐tagged protein from the soluble fraction of the sonicated cell pellets followed a two‐step process: a nickel ion affinity chromatography was run on a Ni‐NTA agarose column (Dutscher His‐Trap HP 5 mL), where contaminants were washed out with 45 mm of imidazole, and His‐tagged tp‐CP12 eluted at 300 mm imidazole. After concentration, the protein was incubated overnight with thrombin (T4648; Sigma, Saint‐Louis, MI, USA) (1 U for 100 μg of tp‐CP12) at 4 °C to cleave the His‐tag and then loaded onto a size‐exclusion chromatography column (Superdex Increase S200 10 × 300 mm × mm) equilibrated with 50 mm sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.5, 50 mm NaCl, 1 mM DTT. The purified protein was concentrated on Centricon (spin X‐UF ultra centrifugal concentrator, Corning, 10 kDa cut‐off) and then flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Protein concentrations were calculated using OD280 measurements and the theoretical absorption coefficient ε280 = 12 490 M−1·cm−1 as obtained using the program ProtParam at the EXPASY server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).


### Small angle X‐ray scattering analysis
  SAXS data acquisition and reduction was described in [18]. Online CRYSOL3.2.1. [54] (https://www.embl‐hamburg.de/biosaxs/atsas‐online/crysol.php) was used to calculate the theoretical scattering curve of single 3D‐models devoid of any spin label, with respect to the experimental SAXS data of tp‐CP12 [18]. Averaging of the theoretical scattering curves and analysis of the reMD distribution of structures were performed using in‐house python scripts.


### Spin labelling procedure
  Each tp‐CP12 variant, ∼ 100 nmol in a 50 mM phosphate buffer at pH 6.5, was incubated with an excess of DTT (1 : 10) for 30 min at room temperature to allow reduction of the thiol groups of cysteine residues. The excess of DTT was removed using a gel filtration desalting PD‐10 column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, Illinois, USA). The fractions containing the protein (checked by OD280) were pooled and a 20‐fold excess of MTSL was immediately added to the protein solution. The mixture was incubated for 4 h at room temperature under stirring and the unreacted spin label was removed with a PD‐10 desalting column using a deuterated buffer (50 mm phosphate buffer pH 6.5). The labelled tp‐CP12 were concentrated with ultracentrifugal filters (Vivaspin 5 kDa; Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) and the final protein concentrations were measured by the UV/vis absorbance at 280 nm. Labelling efficiencies were derived from the double integral of cw‐EPR spectra compared to a standard sample of known concentration S39R1: 81(5)%; S46R1: 75(5)%; S56R1: 95(5)%; S83R1: 91(5)%; C150R1: 88(5)%. Labelled variants were stored at −80 °C. For DEER measurements, spin‐labelled variants were supplemented with d8‐glycerol (final concentration 30% v/v) used as a cryoprotectant. The solutions were transferred into a quartz capillary and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen.


### EPR spectroscopy
  Cw‐EPR experiments were carried out on an Elexsys 500 II Bruker spectrometer equipped with a Super High Q sensitivity resonator operating at X‐band (9.8 GHz) at room temperature (T = 295 K) loading 50 μL of spin‐labelled tp‐CP12 at 50 μm in a quartz capillary. The microwave power was 20 mW, the magnetic field modulation amplitude 0.1 mT and frequency 100 kHz, the field sweep 15 mT. Simulations of rt EPR spectra were performed using SimLabel [32], a MATLAB Graphical User Interface (GUI) that uses some functions of the EasySpin toolbox [55] and is dedicated to multicomponent simulations of EPR spectra from SDSL‐EPR experiments [32].

  DEER experiments were performed on a Bruker ELEXSYS E580 at Q‐band (34 GHz) using the EN 5107D2 resonator. The system was equipped with an Oxford helium cryostat temperature regulation unit, and the data were acquired at 60 K. Four‐pulse DEER experiments [27] were run with pulse durations of 20 ns (π/2) and 40 ns (π), interpulse delays τ 1 of 200 ns, and τ 2 adjusted according to T  m  (typically between 2.0 to 6.0 μs). The pump frequency was centred at the maximum resonance, while the observer frequency was 56 MHz lower. An 8‐step phase cycling was applied to remove unwanted echoes. The concentration of labelled protein samples was 80 μm, and total acquisition time was between 8 to 16 h corresponding to 40–60 averaged scans. DEER distance distributions were extracted from raw experimental data using DEERAnalysis  2019  software [56].


### Computational modelling

#### AlphaFold modelling
  Structure prediction for the tp‐CP12 homodimer was performed using AlphaFold v2.1.1‐Multimer (AF2) and the default databases [57, 58, 59]. The sequence of mature tp‐CP12 (see SI Figure S1) was used in the AF2 input file for multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) lookup and structural template matching. The predicted local distance difference test score (pLDDT), was used to estimate the per‐residue confidence of the models.

  The percentages of the secondary structure elements of the models were assessed using YASARA View [31].

  Modification of the selected AF2 model was performed using PyMOL [60] by a rotation around the N‐Ca bond of residue I134 and by respecting the Ramachandran plot.

  Structure prediction of T. pseudonana PRK was performed using AlphaFold3 webserver [61] based on the sequence provided by UNIPROT (entry B8BZ40) devoid of the first 15 residues, predicted from the chloroplast transit peptide [18].


### Refinement by molecular dynamics simulations
  Harmonic restrained MD simulations were run with the Amber 20 package based on the modified AF2 model. The Amber ff19SB force field [62] was used for the protein, which was immersed into a cubic periodic box filled with water solvent molecules modelled by the OPC model [63] with the LEaP program of AmberTools20. All charged residues were considered in their standard protonation state at pH = 7.0. We added 28 sodium ions to neutralise the simulation box, and the total number of atoms became 178 352. Nonbonded particle‐particle interactions [64] were considered with a distance cut‐off of 9 Å, and the long‐range electrostatic interactions were treated with the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method [65].

  After minimisation, heating and equilibration of the simulation system, several rounds of MD simulations were conducted imposing harmonic restraints on the CA‐CA distances across the dimer for respective spin‐labelled residues S39, S46, S56, S83 and C150 with a spring force of 30 kcal·(mol·Å2)−1. Each round ran for 100 ps with a time step of 2 fs at T = 300 K and P = 0.978 atm. The trajectories and snapshots were saved every 1 ps. The initial/target distances for each CA atom pair were 73 Å/68 Å for S39, 57 Å/43 Å for S46, 26 Å/32 Å for S56, 51 Å/64 Å for S83 and 12 Å/25 Å for C150, respectively.

  The following reMD simulations were run by using a modified version of NAMD2 [43, 44] with all‐atom CHARMM36m protein force field [66]. 25 copies of all‐atom MTSL spin labels (CYR1 in CHARMM‐GUI) [67] were attached to residues S39, S46, S56, S83 and C150, respectively, with reMD Prepper in vacuum for saving the computational resources. The force field of all‐atom CYR1 spin label [68] is provided by CHARMM‐GUI. Spatial overlap among the 25 copies of CYR1 spin labels was allowed by neglecting the interactions among them.

  Five independent all‐atom reMD simulations were conducted at 303.15 K with different random number seeds using Langevin dynamics with a damping coefficient of 5 ps−1. Before each reMD production run, we performed minimisation and equilibration, during which only side chain atoms were relaxed, keeping the backbone atom positions with harmonic restraints of 2 kcal·(mol·Å2)−1 imposed on them. For all spin pairs, the simulated distance distributions from the five independent simulations were strikingly similar, indicating a good convergence for the sampling of the distance distributions in the reMD simulations.

  For these structural refinements, we switched off the harmonic restraints imposed on the backbone atoms and conducted production runs of reMD simulations for 2 ns with a time step of 0.5 fs. The long‐range electrostatic interactions were treated with the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method [69] and the nonbonded interactions were truncated at a 10 Å distance cut‐off. The distance distributions of each spin label pair were restrained with a force constant of 100 kcal·(mol·Å2)−1 toward the experimental distance distribution histograms with a bin width of 0.025 nm. The atomic coordinates of each reMD production run were saved every 1 ps. Since each spin label has 25 copies, a total number of 625 distances for each spin label pair were obtained from a single snapshot of trajectories, and a total of 1 250 000 data points were yielded for every spin label pair from one single reMD production run. Trajectory analysis and protein visualisation were performed with VMD [70] and PyMOL [60], respectively. The saved atomic coordinates of reMD productions were then removed of CYR1 spin labels and then used as an ensemble of structures to simulate the SAXS data.
