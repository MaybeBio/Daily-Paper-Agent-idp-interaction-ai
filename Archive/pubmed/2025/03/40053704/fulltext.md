# Growth of clusters towards liquid-liquid phase separation of monoclonal antibodies as characterized by small angle X-ray scattering and molecular dynamics simulation


## Abstract
  In concentrated protein solutions, short-range attractions (SRA) contribute to liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) as a function of temperature and salinity, particularly when the charge and thus long range repulsions (LRR) are low near the isoelectric point pI. Herein we study how SRA and solution morphology vary with the approach to LLPS from increased SRA for two monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) as salt concentration is reduced near the pI. These properties are quantified using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) interpreted via coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations and compared with less descriptive properties from static and dynamic light scattering. Experimental structure factors are fit with a library of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for a coarse-grained (CG) 12-bead mAb model to determine the SRA strength (K) and cluster size distributions. Proximity to LLPS and clustering characteristics in mAb solutions are impacted by both net charge, which are modified by pH, and the strength of anisotropic electrostatic SRA (charge-charge, charge-dipole, hydrogen bonding etc) which are screened and weakened by added salts. The trends in LLPS are consistent with the reduced diffusion interaction parameter kD/B22ex for dilute solutions. However, greater insight is provided with SAXS along with CG-MD simulations, in particular, the growth of clusters is observed with the approach to LLPS with decreasing salinity over a wide range of concentrations.


## Introduction
  Subcutaneous injection of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) simplifies administration for treating cancer, autoimmune disorders, and infectious disease, for example Covid-191–8. Over a wide range of concentrations even up to 200–300 mg/mL, mAbs may become opalescent and undergo liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS)9–11. Near the LLPS boundary, large concentration gradients may produce opalescence and lower the long term colloidal stability12, 13. The probability of LLPS increases with an increase in short range attraction (SRA) upon decreasing the temperature T relative to upper critical solution temperature (UCST)9 or with a pH close to the isoelectric point (pI) where long range electrostatic repulsion (LRR) provides less stability10 14–16. The increasing SRA may lead to the formation of reversible mAb oligomers that increase the opalescence, or turbidity (τ), eventually leading to small suspended droplets of a new phase15–19. Additionally, at high concentrations SRA also may produce solution viscosities upwards of 20 cP, which negatively impact processing and syringe injectability. Based on the shape of mAbs and amino acid sequence, the SRA can be highly anisotropic, because of electrostatic (e.g. charge-charge20 ,charge-dipole, dipole-dipole21 and hydrogen bonding interactions) and hydrophobic interactions. The interactions may be probed at high concentration by small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) data modeled with coarse grained MD simulation22. Surprisingly, for a series of 59 mAbs at 150 mg/mL, the ones with the highest viscosities did not have high opalescence and those with high opalescence did not have high viscosities23. As such, an understanding of the mechanisms by which the highly directional SRA between mAbs under crowded conditions influence opalescence, LLPS or viscosity remains elusive.

  The onset of opalescence in mAb solutions is influenced by protein concentration cP, concentration and type of added salts, pH and charge and SRA of specific patches on the mAb surface9, 12, 17, 18, 24–26. Near the critical concentration (cP ≈ 90 mg/mL for mAbs14, 26) and temperature, long-range concentration fluctuations produce a growing correlation length (ξ) and opalescence, as predicted by Ornstein-Zernike theory9, 27–29. Far away from critical point, on the other hand, opalescence may be produced by reversible oligomers or clusters that are typically characterized via static light scattering20, 30 or SAXS31, 32. Added salt may raise or lower SRA and τ. For a particular IgG1 mAb, decreasing NaCl from 40 to 5 mM resulted in an increase in opalescence and a higher UCST, indicating that SRA with electrostatic character contributed to LLPS10. In contrast, for another IgG1 at pH 6, increasing salinity raised opalescence indicating stronger SRA and a decrease in LRR near the critical point, yet the viscosity decreased11. The presence of network-like assemblies of mAbs with a mesh size of ~100 nm far from the critical point was then confirmed via measurements with scanning electron-assisted dielectric microscopy (SE-ADM)33. Here the addition of NaCl suppressed the network structure, and an even larger reduction was obtained by using arginine hydrochloride (ArgHCl)18.

  Opalescence and LLPS at high concentration may be predicted from low concentration measurements of the osmotic second virial coefficient B22 and the diffusion interaction parameter kD9, 13 only in limited cases. Values of kD larger than a threshold of +20 mL/g correlated reasonably well with either low viscosity or low opalescence at 150 mg/mL for 59 different mAbs23, 34, 35. At the UCST for several globular proteins (i.e. lysozyme or γB-crystallin), the B22 at the critical point, B22,c, is between −1.8 and −2.9 (relative to that of hard spheres), consistent with spherical colloids with a weak isotropic short-range attraction, for which B22,c ~ −1.613, 36, 37. Interestingly, B22,c was close to −2 for three mAbs under either NaCl or ammonium sulfate, however “COE-19” mAb at low ionic strength had a much more attractive B22,c ~ - 538.

  In the case of globular proteins, the binodal curve plotted in terms of B22 vs volume fraction may be predicted with a hard sphere reference state with isotropic potentials, following the Noro-Frenkel extended law of corresponding states39–41. In a recent study13, the phase diagram of 3 mAbs plotted in terms of B22 – B22,c versus volume fraction normalized by the critical value deviated significantly from that of lysozyme. For mAb systems characterized by patchy attractions the binodal was narrower than that of less patchy more uniform spherical systems, in agreement with trends predicted for model colloids42. For other mAbs under salting out conditions the binodal curve was much broader, given the uniform distribution of SRA on the mAb surface.

  SAXS has been used to study the structure factor S(q) and the correlation length of concentration fluctuations ξ for relatively low molecular weight proteins near LLPS boundaries. In the case of globular γB-crystallin, S(q) near the binodal could be successfully fitted with the Ornstein-Zernike structure factor prediction27, 40. The ξ increases by approximately one order of magnitude as the critical point was approached via decreasing temperature. Both the S(q = 0.02Å−1) and ξ of lysozyme showed two maxima as the hydrostatic pressure was increased from 0 to 3 kPa43, 44. For bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 25° C, the SAXS-measured S(q) at q ~ 0.008 Å−1 and 100 mg/mL increased by a factor 3 as Y3+ concentration increased from 3 to 6 mM, indicating the onset of LLPS upon screening LRR with trivalent ions45, 46 47. Following a different approach, the negative B22 of lysozyme solutions, as calculated from a SAXS-determined coarse-grained pair attraction decreased approximately linearly as the temperature was decreased to the LLPS boundary at high concentration, both at 1 bar and at high pressure.43, 48 However, to the best of our knowledge there are no available studies where SAXS was used to quantify how the protein-protein interactions (PPIs) and the microstructure of mAb solutions change as the LLPS boundary is approached.

  SAXS is gaining attention for characterizing PPI at high concentration over a range of length scales where the patchy mAb-mAb interactions could be characterized13, 22, 31, 32, 49–54,. Recently, experimentally measured S(q) of IgG1, IgG2 and IgG4 subclasses of vedolizumab were fit based on results from MD simulations of a 12-bead model to determine the strength of SRA (K) between the CDR and CH3 domains. The variation in K for the subclasses was correlated to the interaction energy calculated from the 3D molecular structure of the mAb51. A similar approach was used to characterize pH dependent effects of a hydrophobic patch in the CDR loop of a mAb on the SRA and viscosity22. Another study fit the S(q) of an IgG1 mAb with a 9-bead coarse-grained model to obtain both the charge and the strength of attraction. The extracted parameters were then used to predict both the hydrodynamic function and the shear viscosity55. None of these studies examined S(q) upon the approach to LLPS.

  Even when an increase in the SRA forms reversible oligomers that have a large influence on the viscosity20, 22, 31, 32, 38, 50, 51, they may or may not have a similar influence on opalescence and LLPS. In the case of a mAb that underwent phase separation with an increase in NaCl concentration from 0 to 150 mM, the viscosity decreased 10 fold at 120 mg/mL11. For another mAb, decreasing NaCl concentration towards LLPS from 140 to 20 mM resulted into an order of magnitude increase in viscosity, suggesting that both LLPS and viscosity were strongly governed by electrostatic components of SRA10. A key unmet goal remains to be able to predict the role of LRR and various types of SRA on opalescence, LLPS and viscosity with guidance from novel methods such as SAXS/simulation at high concentration.

  The objective of this paper is to elucidate the mechanisms by which the highly anisotropic SRA of mAb solutions at high concentration influence both opalescence and LLPS. We compare mAb 3 which undergoes LLPS with decreasing salinity to mAb 2 which does not down to 5° C and 16 mM NaCl, both near the pI. To complement previous studies of LLPS of mAbs with SLS, we utilize SAXS in conjunction with MD simulation to probe a wide range of length scales from 3 to 90 nm. The effective structure factors Seff(q) are compared in the one phase region as the salt concentration is reduced to approach the binodal. The experimental Seff(q) is fit with a library of coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulations based on a 12-bead model, which describes the highly directional PPIs by means of bead-specific SRA and LRR. The strength of anisotropic SRA and cluster size distributions are quantified with the approach to LLPS and compared with the experimentally measured sample τ or structure factor at q = 0.0018Å−1 from SLS. The Seff(q = 0.007Å−1) and average cluster size near LLPS are observed to be much larger than for mAbs in earlier studies which were further away from the LLPS boundary22, 31, 32, 50, 51. The analysis of the SAXS results provide novel insight into how highly directional PPIs under crowded conditions affect phase separation at a mAb concentration near the critical concentration and also at a much higher concentration of 150 mg/ml.


## Materials and Methods

### Sample preparation
  mAb 2 and mAb 3 (subclass IgG4, pI=6.2 and 8.4 respectively) were provided by Eli Lilly at ~50 mg/mL in a proprietary buffer. The mAbs were dialyzed at either pH 8 in 10 mM tricine and 16–25 mM NaCl against the corresponding stock buffers for two steps of 12 hours each22, 30 (Section S1) or at pH 6 in 10 mM trivalent citrate (ionic strength IS ≈ 50 mM, Section S1.1). Next, the solutions were concentrated via ultrafiltration at 4500 rcf to ~60 mg/mL using Amicon Ultra-15 10k MWCO centrifugal filters. The cP and NaCl content of the mAb 2 and mAb 3 solutions were then adjusted to the target formulation values using a “two-step dilution method” with two dilution buffers, both containing 10 mM citrate at pH 6 or 10 mM tricine at pH 8. The first dilution buffer had the same NaCl concentration as the concentrated mAb stock and the second one had a lower NaCl concentration (further detail is given in Section S2.1, Fig. S1–S4, Section S2.2 and Eq. S2.2.1–S2.2.17). This method avoided the need of pre-mixing a buffer with a unique NaCl concentration for each formulated protein sample as previously reported22, 51.


### Temperature-cP phase diagram determination
  Two temperature (T) T-cP diagrams (Fig. 1a, b) were obtained by formulating mAb 3 at 60 and 80 mg/mL at 10 mM NaCl (Fig. 1a, closed black squares and red circles) and 15 mM (Fig. 1b, closed black squares and red circles) with a temperature-controlled water bath (Section S3.1 and Fig. S5). To allow sufficient time for phase separation to occur, T was held constant at ambient, 15°C, 10°C and 7°C for at least two hours. If a sample underwent phase separation, the cP of each phase was measured via UV-vis spectrophotometry from 2 μL aliquots following a previously published procedure22, 31, 56 (see also Sections S2 and S3.2) and the sample was then resubmerged for 24 hours to further decrease T (Fig. 1a and 1b, open black squares and red circles). To approximate the T-cp phase boundary, the measured concentrations of the two-phase systems at each T were fit with an empirical equation57 (Table S1),  (1) c-cccc=BTc-TTc12,#  where cc and Tc are the concentration and temperature at the critical point and B is a fit parameter. Unless indicated explicitly, all the visual observations of LLPS reported in this work were done for samples that were not centrifuged.


### Turbidity and static light scattering structure factor measurements
  Samples with τ up to ~100 NTU, were measured using a Wyatt DynaPro NanoStar II (Wyatt Technologies, Santa Barbara, CA) light scattering detector with a wavelength λ = 659 nm laser and scattering angle θ = 90°. The instrument was calibrated with AMCO Clear formazin 0–100 NTU turbidity standards (GFS Chemicals, Powell, OH). For each standard, a Wyatt quartz microcuvette (Wyatt Technologies, Santa Barbara, CA) was charged with 70 μL and the scattering detector voltage was recorded and averaged over a two minute period. Measurements were made where the voltage was stable and did not fluctuate because of particles such as dust. The voltage was normalized by the laser power to obtain a calibration curve of the voltage at full laser power vs τ in NTU (Fig. S6). Samples were loaded into the same quartz microcuvette and the scattering detector voltage was collected and normalized in the same manner as the NTU standards. As settling droplets of the heavy phase is not desired for τ measurement, mAb samples were not centrifuged prior to measurement. For samples with τ higher than 100 NTU, it was measured with a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Section S4 and Fig. S7). From the measured Rayleigh ratios, the structure factor at a wave vector q = 0.0018 Å−1, S0SLS, was evaluated30 according to the following relationship:  (2) S0SLS=1MwRθK0cP#  where Mw is the mAb molecular weight, Rθ is the Rayleigh ratio measured at θ = 90°, K0 is the optical constant K0=4πn2dn/dc2/(NAλ4) and cP is the protein concentration. For calculating K0, n is the solution refractive index, dn/dc is the refractive index increment of mAbs (given as 0.185 mL/g), NA is Avogadro’s number, and λ is the laser wavelength30


### Dynamic light scattering measurements
  DLS experiments were conducted using the same procedure described previously31, 58. Briefly, the collective diffusion coefficient (Dc) was measured via a Wyatt DynaPro NanoStar II light scattering instrument (Wyatt Technologies, Santa Barbara, CA) at a wavelength λ = 660 nm and an angle θ = 90°, corresponding to a wave vector q=(4π/λ)sinθ/2=0.02nm-1. The Dc was calculated using the quadratic cumulants algorithm. From diffusion measurements between 2 and 15 mg/mL, the diffusion interaction parameter (kD) was then fit assuming Dc=D0(1+kDcp), where D0 is the diffusion coefficient in the limit of infinite dilution.


### SAXS measurements
  SAXS intensities I(q) at cP ≥ 80mg/mL were collected on a Xenocs Ganesha benchtop X-ray scattering instrument (Xenocs, Holyoke, MA) as previously reported22, 51. To collect data at the lowest possible q, samples were collected in the “SAXS mode” (sample-to-detector distance setting: (0.007Å−1 ≤ q ≤ 0.2 Å−1) instead of the “MAXS mode” used in our earlier studies22, 31, 51. Therefore, samples were run for approximately 16000 s as opposed to the previous value of 3000 s to achieve a similar signal-to-noise for I(q). For each mAb sample, a 50 μL aliquot was loaded in a 1.5 mm OD quartz capillary (Charles Supper, Natick, MA) and the sample was centrifuged at 100 rcf for 60 seconds to settle the liquid at the bottom of the capillary and to remove bubbles. Complementary measurements at cP = 0.5 and 20 mg/mL were measured at beamline 7a of the Cornell High Energy Synchrotron Source (CHESS, Ithaca, NY) BioSAXS facility as described previously31, 59. The form factors P(q) were obtained by merging I(q) at 0.5 mg/mL and q < 0.06Å−1 with I(q) at 20 mg/mL and q > 0.06Å−1 with the BioXTas RAW software suite. The effective static structure factor Seff(q) was calculated as described previously22. In brief, I(q) and P(q) were scaled using a Porod-law fit at approximately 0.15 ≤ q ≤ 0.20 Å−1 to normalize the intensities by cp. Seff(q) was calculated as Seffq=IPorod(q)/PPorod(q), where IPorodq and PPorodq are the intensity and form factor after Porod law-scaling, respectively.

  Form factors used for mAb 2 were the same as the P(q) collected by Chowdhury et al22. For 25mM NaCl, the P(q) used for calculating Seff(q) was at 50mM NaCl as Chowdhury et al22 measured P(q) at a minimum NaCl concentration of 50mM. At 250mM NaCl the P(q) was measured at the correct NaCl concentration. The minimum q value of the P(q) measurements is q = 0.012 Å−1, larger than the minimum q in this work. A Guinier fit was performed by fitting the region 0.012 ≤ q ≤ 0.05 Å−1 to the Guinier Equation Pq=I0exp(-1/3)Rg2q2, where Rg is the radius of gyration and I0 is the intensity as q → 0 Å−1, both of which are fit parameters. The parameters were then substituted back into the Guinier equation to extrapolate P(q) down to q = 0.007 Å−1.

  To examine the effect of temperature, a capillary was placed adjacent to a thermocouple in a sample holder connected to a chiller bath. The instrument was set to the “MAXS” mode (0.012 ≤ q ≤ 0.5Å−1) and the temperature was equilibrated at 25°C for one hour. The scattering intensity was collected for 2,500 seconds for a total of 3,000,000 counts. This process was repeated by decreasing the setpoint by 5°C of the previous setting down to a final temperature of 5°C with one hour equilibration at each temperature.


### 12-bead model
  CG-MD simulations were initialized using a modified method from Chowdhury et al22, 31, 49, 51. mAbs were represented as a 12-bead model defined previously22, 31, 51. Each bead has a modified Lennard-Jones potential (Eq. 3) representing excluded volume and weak van der Waals attraction22, 51, 60, and a screened Coulombic repulsion (Eq. 4) for electrostatic repulsions49. Q is the charge per bead, which was set to 0.26 for mAb 3 at pH 8, corresponding to a scaling parameter22, 31, 51 ψ=12Q/Qtheor≈0.6 which accounts for solvent accessibility as well as ion pairing. The theoretical charge of the full mAb at pH 8, was 5.1 as calculated by summing the charges of each residue predicted by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. For mAb 3 at pH 6, Q was chosen as 1.11. Targeted beads (abbreviated as “SRA beads”) also interact by short-range anisotropic interactions22 :  (5) βuattrijdB=βKe-ZrijdB-1rijdB#  where K is the strength of attraction and Z is the inverse length scale of attraction which is set to 10, equivalent to 10% of the bead diameter. We chose SRA beads in locations corresponding to the CDR of Fab arms and to the CH3 domain of the Fc. Similar to our previous work22, 31 KFab-Fc was chosen to be equal to KFab-Fab and the Fc-Fc interaction was set to zero. For brevity, both KFab-Fab and KFab-Fc will be abbreviated as K. The MD simulations were run using the LAMMPS package, and the Seff(q) and the center of mass radial distribution function gCOM(r) was determined22, 31. The cluster size distribution was calculated using the same method published previously22. Numerous combinations of ϕ, K, and ionic strengths at the selected Q values were run to compile a library of simulated Seff(q). The bead model used in this work is the same as the one used by Chowdhury et al22, but the two simulation libraries used in each work are separate as they have a different scope of the ϕ, K, Q and ionic strength combinations.

  For experimental Seff(q) at cP = 80 mg/mL IS ≤ 25mM, where Seff(q) was most sensitive to changes in K in this study, a set of simulations with K ranging from 2.70 to 3.00 kT in increments of 0.05 kT was performed with a larger cubic box to increase the resolution in q and the precision of K. Here the concentration was nearest to the critical value and fluctuations were the largest. The larger box size has a length of 380 nm, with 16,000 mAbs or 192,000 beads in total compared to the default box size of 280 nm with 6,000 mAbs or 72,000 total beads.

  The experimental Seff(q) were referenced to this library given Q, ϕ and ionic strength to obtain the lowest average relative deviation (ARD, Eq. 6)22:  (6) ARD%=100N∑iSeffqi,exp-Seffqi,simSeffqi,exp#  ARDs were calculated for the q range 0.007 ≥ q ≥ 0.03 Å−1 to prioritize better fits at the lowest q range where Seff(q) is most sensitive to changes in salinity. For selected conditions, images of the simulation box were rendered using the OVITO software (OVITO GmbH, Germany).


## Results and Discussion

### Coexistence curve diagrams and turbidity measurements

#### Coexistence curve diagrams
  The T-cP phase diagrams of mAb 3 at pH 8 near the pI (8.4) with 10 and 15 mM NaCl were prepared by the above two-step dilution method (Section S2) for feed concentrations of 60 and 80 mg/mL. The feed concentration (Fig. 1a,b, filled black and red symbols) had a negligible effect on cP in both the light and the heavy phases (Fig. 1a,b, empty black and red symbols). For each feed concentration, images of two-phase samples at T = 15°C, IS=10 and 15 mM NaCl are shown in Fig. 1c. At 7, 10 and 15°C, the width of the two-phase region is larger for 10 mM than 15 mM NaCl (Fig. 1d),. For comparison, coexistence curves are shown for three mAbs from the literature10, 26, 34. At 10 mM NaCl, the mAb 3 solutions remained two phases up to the maximum studied temperature of 23°C (Fig. 1a). At 23°C and 15 mM NaCl (Fig. 1b), the turbidities were higher in the range 70 ≤ cP ≤ 100 mg/mL (orange triangles), well above 100 NTU, whereas they were < 100 NTU at lower and higher concentrations (blue triangles), with detailed data given in Fig. S3. At these intermediate concentrations, the temperature was closer to the temperature of LLPS. At cP = 80 mg/mL, 16 mM NaCl and 23 °C, mAb 3 was confirmed to be two phases (Fig. S23); a salinity versus protein phase envelope based on visual observation is given in Fig. S3. The T-cP phase diagram fits for mAb 3 at 10 and 15 mM NaCl (Fig. 1a and 1b green lines and Table S1 for the fit parameters and estimation of uncertainties) were obtained assuming a critical concentration cc ~ 90 mg/mL, consistent with previous observations for IgG1 mAbs14, 34.

  At pH 6 well below the pI (8.4) and without added salt (the ionic strength from the multivalent citrate buffer is ~50 mM, see Section S1.1), the mAb 3 solutions were single-phase at T = 19°C. The samples with 35.3 mg/mL ≤ cP ≤ 133.5 mg/mL were visibly more turbid than those at lower and higher concentrations (Fig. S7.1). They remained a single phase as T was lowered to 5°C for 24 hours (Fig. S7.2). Quantitative τ measurements were not performed for the mAb 3 samples at pH 6. Consistent with the findings of Nishi et al (Fig. 5 of10), moving the pH away from pI stabilized the solution due to an increase in electrostatic repulsion.

  The salinity coexistence curve was determined at 23°C and pH 8, assuming that the salinity was the same in each phase, although this is only an approximation13 (Fig. 2a). Photographs of mAb 3 solutions at cP ≈ 40 mg/mL (Fig. S8) indicate a single phase at 20 and 15 mM IS; however, a meniscus is clearly seen as indicated by the red arrow at 10 mM IS. For samples prepared in the range 61 mg/mL ≤ cP ≤ 125 mg/mL and 12.2 mM ≤ IS ≤ 25 mM, only the sample at cP = 61 mg/mL and IS = 12.2 mM phase separated upon centrifugation at 5000 rcf for 5 minutes (Fig. S9). At 23°C, 60 mg/mL ≤ cP ≤ 200 mg/mL and 25mM ≤ IS ≤ 12 mM, mAb 3 samples remained a single phase after being centrifuged at 4,500 rcf for 10 minutes (Fig. 2a, filled blue circles). At IS = 16 mM and cP ≈ 90 mg/mL, phase separation occurs upon one hour of centrifugation at 18,000 rcf (Fig. 2a, empty blue squares and inset picture). Dialyzing mAb 3 samples versus 10 mM tricine stock buffer with either 10 or 5 mM NaCl and at a feed concentration cP ≈ 50 mg/mL resulted in phase separation at room temperature without centrifugation (Fig. 2a, empty red circles). Moreover, mAb 2 at pH 6 and low IS=16 mM did not show phase separation at 80 mg/mL and 23°C after 2 hours of centrifugation at 18,000 rcf (Fig. S9.1). The NaCl concentration versus cP coexistence diagram of mAb 3 is asymmetric with a steeper slope in the low cP relative to the high cP branch (Fig. 2a) as is commonly observed for mAbs10, 13, 34. The width of the two phase region is similar to those reported by Sibanda et al for the “COE-01”, “COE-07” and “COE-19” mAbs13 (Fig. 2b).

  Decreasing IS from 150 to 16 mM NaCl at cP ≈ 90mg/mL and at 23°C led to phase separation of mAb 3 with an apparent critical salinity of 16 mM (Fig. 2a), obtained via setting cP = 90mg/mL in the cubic fit of the IS-cP diagram (Tables S2 and S3). kD became considerably more attractive as NaCl decreased reaching −26.8 mL/g (Table 1 and Fig. S10 for the collective diffusion Dc versus concentration curves). This kD is more attractive than the value of −13 mL/g for mAb 2 at pH 6 at the same salinity (Table 1), consistent with the lack of phase separation for mAb 2. Similar salt effects on kD have been observed for certain IgG1 mAbs10, 34 at 25°C34. An approximately 3-fold decrease in kD was observed for the “COE-01” IgG1 mAb as LLPS was approached by decreasing the NaCl concentration13 (Table S3). Additional context is gained when normalizing kD by the hard-sphere second virial coefficient from excluded volume, B22ex=16πRH3/3. RH is the hydrodynamic radius of each mAb from the intercept of the kD plot (section S8 and Table S12). In the study by Sibanda et al, LLPS was observed when kD/B22ex became more attractive than a threshold of ~ −2 for their same three mAbs in Fig. 2b13. In the current study, only mAb 3 at 16mM NaCl had a kD/B22ex value more attractive than this threshold, consistent with the result it is the only system in Table 1 which phase separates.


#### S0SLS measurements at 90° at T = 23°C
  In the one-phase region, conditions were selected for SLS (Fig. 2a, empty green triangles), SAXS (Fig. 2a, empty green diamonds), and SLS+SAXS measurements (Fig. 2a, filled green diamonds). At cP = 80 mg/mL and IS = 25 mM, the mAb 3 sample was one phase even after a 2 hour centrifugation at 18,000 rcf (Fig. 2a, inset picture on the top). At cP = 80mg/mL, S0SLS of mAb 3 increased from 2.0 to 5.8 as NaCl decreased from 150mM to 25mM NaCl. The S0SLS values were four to five fold smaller for mAb 2 at the same cP as it was much less turbid. Similar trends were observed at cP = 130mg/mL, but with the increased packing fraction and steric repulsion, the magnitude and changes in S0SLS were smaller. In all of these cases, SRA, which also may cause LLPS10, increased as local charges were less screened as IS decreased. These results follow similar trends as seen with UV-vis measurements of τ at 0° in NTU units (Fig. S3).


### Predictions of Seff(q) and 〈n〉 in the simulation library versus K and IS for two values of Q
  The Seff(q) based on the 12-bead model was quantified for a library of a large set of ϕ, K, Q and IS in Fig. S11–S17 as listed in Table S4 over a range of interest for the experimental data. The sensitivity of Seff(q) to K and Q is given in Fig. 3. Although average cluster sizes are listed in the legend in parentheses, the trends are best understood in Fig. 4. The growth in clusters with an increase in K from 2.9 to 3.0 is visualized in Fig. 5. At ϕ = 0.075, IS=25 mM, and Q=0.26 increasing K from 2.8 to 3.0 kT results in a large increase in Seff(q) and a 40-fold increase of the average cluster size 〈n〉 (Fig. 3 and 4). At this low Q, SRA play a dominant role on Seff(q) with a minimal effect of LRR. At a Q of 1.11, and an IS of 50 mM, the added LRR causes a less significant upturn in Seff(q) for each K (Fig. 3) and thus 〈n〉 values are much smaller (Fig. 4). The LRR keeps the attractive beads further apart to inhibit cluster formation. At a low charge of Q = 0.26, the effect of IS on 〈n〉 is very small for K < 3.0 kT (Fig. 4a). These results demonstrate that increasing SRA produces larger average cluster sizes near the pI, and the cluster size decreases with an increase in charge away from the pI.


### Structure of mAb solutions near the LLPS and fits with 12-bead model MD simulations

#### mAb 3 form factors and reproducibility of SAXS results
  Unlike kD (Section 1) which is measured in dilute solutions, the Seff(q) determined by SAXS and simulation quantifies the structure and interactions of mAb solutions at multiple length scales and at high cP22, 31, 32, 49, 51. The PPorod(q) form factor of mAb 3 at pH 8 did not vary significantly within the range of explored NaCl concentrations (Fig. S18). Therefore, the PPorod(q) values in Fig. S18 were interpolated with respect to NaCl concentration, and the outcome was used to compute the effective structure factor Seffq=IPorod(q)/PPorod(q) for all mAb 3 conditions. IPorod(q) results are given for all conditions in Fig. S19. For duplicate experiments of two SAXS samples at pH 8, cP ≈ 80 mg/mL and IS = 25 mM the relative standard deviation RSD=σ/x- of Seff(q = 0.007) was 1.5% as expected from the calculated propagated error (see Fig. S20 and Table S5 and Table S5.1). These samples remained a single phase after one hour of centrifugation at 18,000 rcf (Fig. S21). The PPorod(q) of did not vary significantly with added NaCl at pH 6 and were interpolated from PPorod(q) reported previously22.


#### SAXS of mAb 3 at pH 6 with Q = 1.11 (pI = 8.4)
  The effect of temperature and mAb concentration was studied at pH 6 well below pI = 8.4 of mAb 3 where the charge was 1.11 per bead. At cP = 184.7 mg/mL with 10 mM citrate buffer and no added salt, the Seff(q = 0.012Å−1) grew from 0.46 at 25°C to 0.63 at 5°C (Fig. 6a). Such modest growth suggests a modest increase in SRA in the presence of the steric22 and long-range repulsion. An increase in SRA with a decrease in temperature commonly leads to UCST type LLPS. The small change in SRA is further supported by the observation at T = 5°C that the solution remains as a single phase and does not appear turbid visually (Fig. S7.2). As shown in Fig. 6b, increasing cP from 40 mg/mL to 185 mg/mL at pH 6 results in a 4-fold reduction of the Seff(q = 0.012Å−1) due to greater steric repulsion (see Section S6.1 for the determination of uncertainties in cP for such samples at pH 6). A similar decrease in Seff(q = 0.012Å−1) was reported previously for the same mAb 2 at IS = 50 mM NaCl and at pH 4.5 or pH 8, both far from pI as cP was increased from 75 to 200 mg/mL (Fig. 2a and 2c of22). Whereas these data show typical trends for the concentration effect, in the studies below, we only considered two concentrations.


#### Effect of IS on mAb 2 pH 6 and mAb 3 pH 8 at 80mg/mL
  The Seff(q) for each mAb at the pI were fit with the simulation library by minimizing the ARD between the experimental and simulated Seff(q) (See Fig. S24–S27 and Table S7, S7.1, S7.2 for all K values and corresponding ARD used to determine the best fit). The optimized value of K for mAb 3 was found to change markedly with ionic strength (Fig. 7a, 8c), indicating the salt screened SRA, namely, charge-charge, charge-dipole and hydrogen bonding interactions22, 31, 49, 51. For this low Q, the effect of screening of LRR was small. The Seff(q = 0.007 Å−1) of mAb 3 at pH 8 16 mM was twice as large as that of mAb 2 at pH 6 at the same IS (Fig. 6d, 8b,e). The values of K and Seff(q = 0.007Å−1) for mAb 3 were larger than previously observed for more weakly interacting mAbs further away from LLPS.22, 31, 32, 51 (Table S8). The seemingly modest change in K from 3.00 to 2.90 kT was significant enough to cause the 〈n〉 = 200 of mAb 3 to be about two orders of magnitude larger than that of mAb 2, 〈n〉 = 7 (Table 1). As was shown in Fig. 4, 〈n〉 is very sensitive to K in this regime. A cluster size of 200 may suggest the onset of nucleation of a new phase (see Fig. 5c). These simulation results for K and 〈n〉 are consistent with the key distinction between the two mAbs, being that mAb 3 phase separated upon decreasing NaCl slightly below 16mM whereas mAb 2 did not.


#### Effect of IS on mAb 2 pH 6 and mAb 3 pH 8 at 150mg/mL
  At a higher cP = 150 mg/mL, decreasing NaCl from 150 mM to 16 mM caused the K of mAb 3 pH 8 to increase from 2.6 to 2.9 kT (Fig. 7b, 8c) which produced a two order of magnitude increase in 〈n〉 (Fig. 8d). At 150 mg/mL, the K of mAb 2 pH 6 was 0.1 kT lower than that of mAb 3 pH 8 at low IS (Fig. 8c,f). Consequently, 〈n〉 was ~ 5-fold larger for mAb 3 (Table 1). At 150 mg/mL, mAb 3 phase separated after dropping NaCl below 16mM while mAb 2 does not. Consistent with this difference, visualizations of the simulation box show that the best fit for mAb 3 contained a single system spanning cluster (Fig. S29a) and the largest cluster found in the trajectory was size n = 3,913. This cluster had a radius of gyration Rg = 108nm, or half the simulation box length (Table S11). However, the magnitude of the Rg is so large compared to the box length that the cluster volume is constrained by the periodic boundary conditions. The reported Rg is therefore a lower bound as the cluster would likely be larger for a larger simulation box. The clusters for mAb 2 were clearly smaller from visual inspection (Fig. S29b), and the average cluster Rg for mAb 2 was 60nm, ~30nm smaller than for mAb 3 (Table S11). The effective short-range attractions between mAbs, as quantified by K in the simplified 12-bead model, are weaker at cp = 150 mg/mL than at 80 mg/mL. At 80 mg/mL, mAbs are close enough to interact, and there is sufficient free volume to allow their attractive patches to align22. At the higher cP, the weaker short-range attractions reflect crowding-induced misalignment of attractive patches between neighboring mAbs22.


#### Effect of pH on mAb 3
  The change in Q and LRR with pH was shown to have a modest effect on K and Seff(q) as the IS of 50 mM from the multivalent citrate buffer increased the screening of LRR. At 80 mg/mL decreasing the pH from 8 to 6 had only a slight effect on the Seff(q = 0.012Å−1) (Fig. 6c). Even though K was slightly larger at pH 6 than at pH 8.0, 〈n〉 was 40 times larger at pH 8 due to weaker LRR (Fig. 8d, Table 1). Increasing the pH from 6 (130 mg/ml) to 8 (150 mg/ml) caused a 20-fold increase in 〈n〉 despite a K of 2.9 kT for each (Fig. 6c, 8c,d, Table 1). 〈n〉 became larger as the LRR decreased when Q became small, and thus the molecules are closer together and influenced more strongly by SRA.


#### Fits of Seff(q) to the Ornstein-Zernike equation
  The Seff(q) may be expressed in terms of a component related to the critical point and a non-critical component27. The Seff(q) of mAb 3 at pH 8 at selected conditions were fit with the Ornstein-Zernike (OZ) structure factor equation for near-critical conditions at low q27, 44  (7) Seffq=S01+q2ξ2#  where ξ is the correlation length and S0 is the structure factor at q = 0 (Fig. 7c and 7d and Table 1). For each fit it was assumed that S0=S0SLS. The OZ equation accurately described the behavior at low q for cP =80 mg/mL and 25 mM NaCl. The low value of ξ of ~10nm, the length scale of a single monomer, signifies that the distance from the critical point was relatively large. The quality of the fits decreased significantly with increased salinity or cP as the system moved further from the critical point, thus Eq. 7 became less applicable. All ξ and Seff(q) values were much smaller than those of for globular proteins which were measured much closer to the critical point27, 43, 44 (Table S9).

  At 80 mg/mL 25mM NaCl, K = 2.90 kT and most clusters were roughly the same magnitude as ξ (Fig. 5b) while at cp = 150mg/mL and 16mM NaCl, the size of clusters are much larger than ξ(Fig. S29). The relatively small values of ξ versus large increase in cluster size with increasing cP and the poor fit from the OZ equation far from cc suggest that the clusters in this study were driven primarily by SRA and not by critical density fluctuations.

  At IS = 10 mM, below the binodal in the two-phase region, the Seff(q) of the heavy phase of mAb 3 at pH 8 obtained from a feed concentration of 127 mg/mL was very similar to that measured at 158 mg/mL in the single-phase region (Fig. S28). Thus, large fluctuations were not seen in the one phase region near LLPS at these conditions, in agreement with the ξ at cp = 150mg/mL.


#### Cumulative cluster size distribution C(n), cluster radius of gyration Rg(n), and center of mass radial distribution function gCOM(r)
  Additional understanding of morphology and interactions can be gained from the cumulative cluster size distribution C(n) and average cluster radius of gyration Rg(n) (Fig. 10a, Fig. S33). At K = 2.5 kT, far from LLPS (see Table 1), C(n=1) = 0.88, thus 12% of mAbs were in clusters with size larger than 1. The largest cluster size was n ~ 10 (Rg(10) ~ 15 nm), given by the smallest value of n for which C(n)=1 (Fig. 10a, blue line, Fig. S33). The center of mass radial distribution function gCOM(r) at this K has a broad peak from r ~ 3.6 – 4.0 dB. Increasing K to 2.90 kT gave the best fit to mAb 2 pH 6 at cP = 80mg/mL and 16mM NaCl. Here, C(n=1) dropped to 0.6 and C(n=10) decreased to 0.8 as clusters of n > 10 began to appear, while the maximum cluster size increased to ~100 (Rg(100) ~ 30nm) (Fig. 10a, Fig. S33). The size of the largest clusters was still much smaller than the box length and was consistent with mAb 2 not phase separating. The higher K introduced a gCOM(r) peak at r ~ 1dB from planar stacking of mAb molecules 31, 32 (Fig. 10b). For mAb 3, K = 3.00 kT was the best fit at cP = 80mg/mL and 16mM NaCl. Here, C(n=10) decreased by ~40% to 0.5, and the largest cluster size increased by an order of magnitude from ~100 to over 2,000 (Fig. 10a, Fig. S33). The Rg of the largest cluster reached 100 nm, which is comparable to the simulation box and consistent with mAb 3 phase separating at 16mM NaCl (Fig. 10, Fig.S33). This length scale was also consistent with the size of aggregates (~100nm) in a turbid solution of bovine gamma globulin measured vis scanning electron-assisted dielectric microscopy (SE-ADM)33. The height of all peaks in gCOM(r) increased compared to 2.90 kT. The shoulder at r ~ 2.3 dB developed into a peak, which could signify domain-level overlaps32 (Fig. 10b). The bulk value gCOM(r) = 1 was not reached even at r ~ 10 dB indicating long range structuring from large clusters. The maximum gCOM(r) peak height was dB 1.75 at r ~ 4 dB, larger than the maximum value ~1.2 at r ~ 3.6 dB reported in our previous work22, 31, 32, 51 for less attractive mAbs with lower K.

  To compare with ξ fits to experimental SAXS and assess proximity to a critical point, the rescaled total correlation functions Γr=lnr[gCOMr-1] from simulation (Section S7 and Fig. S32) were evaluated. Far from a critical point, Γ(r) shows oscillating behavior for r > 1. As a critical point is approached, long-ranged correlation lengths cause the total correlation function hr=gCOMr-1 to scale with r such that h(r)∝r-1exp(-r/ξ) 61, 62. Therefore, long range oscillations in Γ(r) will be damped as Γ(r) assumes the form of an exponential decay 61, 62. Γ(r) for K = 3.00 kT, the best fit for mAb 3 at cp = 80mg/mL and 16mM NaCl (Fig. S33, red curve), shows damped oscillations at long r indicating proximity to a critical point. All other Γ(r) show oscillations of higher amplitude and are consequently much further away from a critical point (Fig. S33). The oscillations present at K = 2.90 kT, the best fit for mAb 3 at cp = 80mg/mL and 25mM NaCl, coincides with the low ξ of from the OZ fit. The NaCl and temperature were too high to cause significant critical density fluctuations.


## Conclusions
  Whereas the approach to the LLPS boundary has been studied with SAXS for globular proteins43, 45, 48, 63–65 this study extends the domain to mAbs with highly anisotropic short-range attraction. Near LLPS, SAXS interpreted with MD simulation provided changes in the strength of SRA (K) and cluster morphology and size distribution over a range of length scales; these properties were not accessible from earlier studies of turbidity and SLS13, 22, 23, 31, 51, 66. For mAb 3 near the isoelectric point at pH 8, where LRR is negligible, a decrease in salinity increased SRA which led to the formation of clusters that were pre-cursors to LLPS. Thus, the local anisotropic charge-charge, charge dipole and hydrogen bonding SRA became stronger with less screening by NaCl. Upon the approach to LLPS at 80 mg/mL, S0SLS, Seff(q = 0.007 Å−1) from SAXS and K increased and the average cluster Rg grew to 47 nm. At 150 mg/mL, the behavior was similar whereby clusters grew to 100 nm forming system spanning clusters for a simulation box length of 220 nm, precursors to the onset of LLPS. The larger clusters at 150 versus 80 mg/mL were a consequence of the higher packing fraction and greater effects of SRA from closer mAb spacings. Given the low correlation length at 150 mg/ml from the Ornstein-Zernicke equation, the SLS reflected SRA driven cluster formation, but not large density fluctuations or critical opalescence, which are suppressed at higher mAb concentration. At pH 6, where mAb 3 was charged, the LRR prevented LLPS even without added salt.

  For mAb 2 relative to mAb 3, the same decrease in salinity at 80mg/mL produced a smaller increase for S0SLS, Seff(q = 0.007 Å−1), K and 〈n〉. Here system spanning clusters were not formed, consistent with the lack of LLPS in experiment. At 16 mM IS, a change in K from 2.9 to 3 kT, the best fit values for mAb 2 and mAb 3, respectively, produced a 30-fold increase in 〈n〉 indicating high sensitivity to K. At 16 mM IS, the low concentration kD/B22ex values were −0.8 and −2.5 for mAb 2 and mAb 3, respectively. Thus, the threshold for LLPS from this low concentration measurement was consistent with the value of −2 for kD/B22ex for a set of mAbs by Sibanda et al13. Although this low concentration property is useful, it does not provide information about cluster morphology and underestimates effects of anisotropic SRA when mAbs are closely spaced at high concentration. The quantification of K from SAXS will be relevant in future work for calculating phase boundaries with models for chemical potential such as the Wertheim’s thermodynamic perturbation theory67.
