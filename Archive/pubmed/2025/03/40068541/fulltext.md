# Atomistic Molecular Dynamics Simulations of Intrinsically Disordered Proteins


## Abstract
  Recent years have seen remarkable gains in the accuracy of atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and expansion in the types of calculated properties that can be directly compared with experimental measurements. These advances occurred due to the use of IDP-tested force fields and the porting of MD simulations to GPUs and other computational technologies. All-atom MD simulations are now explaining the sequence-dependent dynamics of IDPs; elucidating the mechanisms of their binding to other proteins, nucleic acids, and membranes; revealing the modes of drug action on them; and characterizing their phase separation. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are further expanding the reach of atomistic MD simulations.


## Introduction
  Ever since the solution of the first protein structure by X-ray crystallography [1], the central tenet of molecular biophysics and structural biology has been that the amino-acid sequence of a protein determines its structure, which in turn determines its function. A culmination of this line of pursuit is the AI-based method Alphafold for predicting the structures of proteins from their sequences [2], which just won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. However, this central tenet has been challenged by the prevalence of intrinsically disordered proteins or regions thereof (IDPs or IDRs). Without well-defined structures, a crucial link between sequence and function is missing for IDPs. Substitutes for structure have been proposed for IDPs, including conformation [3–5] and dynamics [6,7]. Such intermediaries between sequence and function are indispensable for explaining the functional mechanisms of IDPs.

  Residue-specific chemical shifts from NMR spectroscopy have shown that IDPs typically have little secondary structures. IDPs are thus similar to the unfolded state of structured proteins [7] and can sample vast conformational space. The typical conformations of IDPs are much more open than the folded state of structured proteins with the same sequence lengths, as demonstrated by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) [8]. Complementing local conformations by chemical shifts and overall size and shape by SAXS, Föster resonance energy transfer (FRET) can generate distance restraints between labeled sites in the 10s of Å range.

  NMR spectroscopy is uniquely able to characterize residue-specific protein dynamics. NMR relaxation experiments probe the time correlation function of bond vectors, such as the backbone NH bonds. Instead of a clean separation in timescales into internal and overall motions for structured proteins [9], the relaxation of NH bond vectors in IDPs involves a continuum of timescales from ps to ~5 ns [6,10,11]. Transverse relaxation rates (R2) measured by NMR relaxation are sensitive to motions on the 5-ns timescale and sequence-dependent [6,12]. IDP chain configuration occurs on a longer timescale, from 10s to 100s of ns, according to ns fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (nsFCS) [13]. Rare contact formation on the μs timescale has been captured by photo-induced electron transfer [14].

  Proteins function by binding with partners, and this statement holds even truer for IDPs. The malleability of IDPs enables them to readily adapt to their binding partners, which, as with structured proteins, include other proteins, small molecules, nucleic acids, and membranes. The disordered nature changes the characteristics of binding modes. For example, an IDP undergoing folding upon binding to a structured protein often adopts an extended conformation, with structural stability derived mainly from inter- instead of intramolecular interactions [15–17]. The extended binding pose preserves specificity and yet enables fast dissociation, making IDPs ideal for signaling and regulation [18]. Likewise, the disordered nature confers distinct advantages to IDPs in mediating liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), including the potential for all residues, not just surface residues in structured proteins, to form intermolecular interactions [19]. A small molecule can lock an IDP into a specific conformation [20], but as presented below, other modes of action are prevalent. IDPs tend to have a high level of charged residues, and some IDPs use opposite net charges to drive heterodimer formation and heterotypic LLPS [21]. Similarly, cationic residues of IDPs mediate binding to anionic partners such as nucleic acids [22] and cell membranes. The different modes of IDPs binding to membranes and the various functions mediated by IDP-membrane binding have been reviewed recently [23]. IDP dynamics, binding, and sampling techniques have also been covered in other recent reviews [12,24–26].

  All-atom MD simulations have been an equal partner to NMR and other experimental techniques. Thanks to the development and testing of force fields on IDPs [6,27–33] and the porting of MD simulations to GPUs [34], there have been remarkable gains in the accuracy of calculated properties and expansion in the types of properties amenable to direct comparison with experiments. This review focuses on physical insights derived from explicit-solvent all-atom simulations that are unattainable from experiments or coarse-grained simulations.


### IDP simulations require IDP-tested force fields
  For much of their history, all-atom force fields for protein-water systems have been parameterized for folded proteins. It is still too often to see these “legacy” force fields directly used to simulate IDPs, leading to the over-stabilization of secondary structures and over-compaction of the entire protein. For accurate representation of IDPs, the use of force fields tested on IDPs is essential. The TIP4P-D water model was parameterized to increase the Lennard-Jones well depth and thereby strengthen the water-protein (and water-water) dispersion interactions, reducing or eliminating the over-compaction of IDPs when combined with various protein force fields [28]. A 2018 benchmarking concluded that none of the then-existing force fields modeled both structured proteins and IDPs well, and consequently the Amber99SB-disp protein force field and the accompanying a99SB-disp water model were developed for IDPs, which reproduced well radius of gyration and NMR measurements for α-synuclein and other IDPs [31]. This force field combination was on par with Amber19SB [35] / TIP4P-D when benchmarked on α-synuclein [36].

  Besides conformational properties, dynamic properties are also crucial to the functions of IDPs. Until recently, it was common to reweigh or rescale simulation results to match NMR relaxation data. Recent IDP simulations have shown that some force fields can reproduce both conformational and dynamic properties of IDPs. Virtanen et al. [37] compared simulations with three force fields against NMR relaxation experiments on the periplasmic domain of TonB, comprising a ~150-residue disordered region and a ~100-residue folded domain. In simulations with an IDP-tested combination (Amberff03ws [27] and TIP4P/2005), the disordered region remained expanded and the calculated NMR relaxation properties for both the disordered region and the folded domain agreed with the experimental data; by contrast, in simulations with a legacy force field (Amber99SB-ILDN / TIP3P) and another IDP-tested force field (Charmm36m [30]), the disordered region collapsed around the folded domain. Hicks et al. [38] benchmarked five force-field combinations on the 64-residue IDP ChiZ: Amber14SB [39] / TIP4P-D, Amberff03ws / TIP4P/2005, Amber99SB-ILDN / TIP4P-D, Amber15IPQ / SPCEb [29], and Charmm36m, and found Amber14SB / TIP4P-D was the best in matching experimental Cα and Cβ chemical shifts and SAXS profile. Moreover, this force-field combination reproduced well NMR relaxation properties. This combination was further validated on eight IDPs ranging in sequence length from 40 (Aβb40) to 140 residues (α-synuclein) [6] and on structured proteins [40]. Another force field, Amber99SBnmr2 [33], also modeled well the NMR relaxation properties of two IDPs [41]. As MD simulations are typically run at a constant temperature, thermostats introduced to regulate temperature may perturb protein dynamics and this effect can and should be removed [40].


### All-atom simulations provide explanations for the sequence dependence of IDP dynamics
  All-atom simulations unveil how dynamics is coded by the sequence of an IDP. ChiZ sampled broad conformational space but also exhibited asymmetry in transverse relaxation rate between the N- and C-halves of the sequence [38]. NH time correlation functions were fit to a sum of three exponentials, with time constants around 0.2, 1.5, and 5 ns, respectively. The slower dynamics in the N-half, as indicated by larger amplitudes of the 5-ns component, arose from a higher propensity of salt-bridge and cation-π formation between local residues, due partly to the exclusive presence of all three acidic residues in the N-half. The C-half, with faster dynamics, was predicted to more readily adapt to binding partners. In a follow-up study investigating ChiZ binding to acidic membranes [42], the C-half was indeed the primary membrane-binding region, through the formation of multiple transient contacts between its basic residues and acidic lipid headgroups. Acidic residues in the N-half competed with lipids for interactions with basic residues.

  By calculating NMR relaxation properties of eight diverse IDPs, Dey et al. [6] derived general rules for the sequence dependence of backbone dynamics. Local contacts and transient secondary structures, especially α-helices, increase the amplitudes of the slower component. By contrast, glycine generally promotes fast dynamics but could also facilitate local as well as long-range contact formation. Inspired by this understanding, Qin and Zhou [7] developed a sequence-based method, SeqDYN, to predict R2 of IDPs using a mathematical model (https://zhougroup-uic.github.io/SeqDYNidp/). A q parameter modeled the tendency of the 20 types of amino acids to have high or low R2 and their influence on the R2 value of a neighboring residue. Training on the R2 data of 45 IDPs yielded q parameters that were high for aromatic, arginine, and long-branched aliphatic amino acids and low for glycine and short polar amino acids; the influence on neighboring residues had a half-length of 5.6 residues.

  The q parameter captures the propensity of an amino acid to form intramolecular interactions. In the dense phase after LLPS, each IDP chain is surrounded by other IDP chains, and intramolecular interactions give way to intermolecular interactions. Correspondingly, q captures the propensity of an amino acid to form such intermolecular interactions and hence the drive for LLPS. Indeed, the q parameter correlates with the threshold concentration for LLPS [43]; aromatic amino acids and arginine have high q values and are also known to mediate LLPS via π-π and cation-π interactions. In a similar fashion, two oppositely charged IDPs, histone H1.0 (193 residues; net charge +53) and its nuclear chaperone prothymosin-α (113 residues; net charge −44) form a heterodimer in dilute conditions and undergo heterotypic LLPS at higher concentrations [21]. The average contact numbers of individual residues of prothymosin-α in the dense phase were similar to those in the heterodimer, except for a modest elevation in the first 20 residues, which are low in charged residues. However, the contacts in the dense phase were much more transient and promiscuous. The chain reconfiguration time of prothymosin-α in MD simulations increased 10-fold, from 10 ns to 110 ns, upon heterodimer formation, matching the values measured by nsFCS.

  All-atom simulations can also probe the rare formation of long-range contacts that may be implicated in functional processes. In the HOX transcription factor DFD, a cation-π interaction between W346 in the disordered N-terminal region and R394 in a three-helix bundle formed ~3% of the time [6]. Presumably, after the three-helix bundle binds to DNA, the N-terminal region continues to switch between the major open form and the minor intramolecularly bound form. However, upon DNA binding of the co-transcription factor, W346 docks to the latter to stabilize the ternary complex [44]; R394 thus serves as a competing site for W346 in the ternary complex. Importantly, the time constant for forming rare long-range contacts can be measured by photo-induced electron transfer, as demonstrated on the 125-residue C-terminal disordered region of the measles virus nucleoprotein [14]. This IDR contains an 18-residue molecular recognition element (residues 485–502; α-MoRE), which forms an α-helix upon binding to the folded X domain of the phosphoprotein (Figure 1a). The relaxation times, τCW, due to photo-induced electron transfer from tryptophan at residue 518 to cysteine at residue 482 or 488 were 7.6 μs and 2.8 μs, respectively, exhibiting a much greater disparity than predicted for a homopolymer model. Simulations reproduced the greater disparity in τCW and revealed four shallow wells (S1 to S4) that accounted for 24%, 14%, 8%, and 2%, respectively, of sampled conformations and were stabilized by partial helices (S2 and S3) and long-range salt bridges (S1 and S4). Only S3 and S4 were competent for the C488-W518 and C482-W518 electron transfer, respectively.


### All-atom simulations elucidate mechanisms of IDP binding to folded proteins
  Robustelli et al. [45] simulated the binding of α-MoRE to the X domain. The simulations were run at an elevated temperature of 400 K to accelerate the transition between the free and bound states, yielding 72 binding and unbinding events in 200 μs. Moreover, the unbinding events were converted into binding events by reversing the direction of time. In the transition-state ensemble, obtained using the fraction of native contacts as the reaction coordinate and calculating PFold by running additional simulations, α-MoRE formed an intermediate level of helical content (similar to that in the free state). The flexibility in these partially helical conformations allowed α-MoRE to form multiple native contacts with the X domain (Figure 1a). From the transition state, both the helical content and the number of native contacts grew to reach the native complex.

  The initial binding of a disordered transactivation subdomain (residues 17–27) of tumor suppressor protein p53 to the oncoprotein MDM2 (residues 25–109) was simulated by parallel cascade selection MD [46]. In this approach, multiple short simulations are conducted and snapshots with the shortest (or longest) inter-protein distances are selected to run the next cycle of binding (or unbinding) simulations. Then, two near-native poses were selected to run additional simulations of 60 ns each (Figure 1b). Again, the p53 fragment grew in helical content as more native contacts were formed, accompanied by dehydration of the interface. Finally, all the poses were analyzed by Markov-state modeling (MSM) to yield an association rate constant of 0.25 × 107 M−1s−1 and dissociation rate constant of 1.1 s−1, which agreed well with experimental measurements.

  The binding of the disordered transactivation domain of c-Myb and the KIX domain of the CREB-binding protein was simulated using the adaptive bandit method [47]. This method is similar to parallel cascade selection MD, except here the selection of snapshots for the next cycle is based on a free energy estimated through MSM. In total, 1809 simulations of 250 ns each were run. Two bound poses were obtained, with c-Myb forming a single long helix in each case, which are similar to those reported by NMR as “bound” and “secondary” (Figure 1c). The rate constant for binding to the “bound” site, calculated using MSM, was ~3 × 107 M−1s−1, close to the experimental value.


### All-atom simulations reveal different modes of drug action on IDPs
  Unlike structured proteins that present a well-defined site for drug binding, IDPs lack a specific binding pocket. All-atom simulations and NMR chemical shift perturbation can identify IDP residues that frequently interact with drug molecules. Drug binding may physically block an IDP’s interaction with a partner protein much like classical inhibitors of protein-protein interactions, or it may shift the IDP to a conformational state that is incapable of binding to a partner protein, though usually not locking the IDP to a specific conformation. Similar modes of action can also prevent IDP aggregation.

  The interaction between p53 and MDM2 is a high-value target for cancer research; both proteins bind small molecules. In simulations using the REST2 enhanced sampling method [48], binding of the small molecule EGCG restricted the p53 N-terminal domain (NTD; residues 1–61) to conformations too compact for binding to MDM2 (Figure 2a) [49]. The compaction was achieved by mediating the interactions between W23-K24 and P47-T55. p53 binding is regulated by a disordered “lid” consisting of the N-terminal 24 residues of MDM2 [50]: the lid equilibrates between a dominant closed conformation and a minor open conformation on a 10-ms timescale; it competes with p53 for the same binding site. In contrast, the lid increased the binding affinity of the p53-binding site for the small molecule AM-7290 by ~250-fold [51]. Umbrella sampling yielded a free energy difference of 1.4 kcal/mol between the closed and open conformations of apo MDM2 (Figure 2b left part). Further simulations showed that the lid became ordered by AM-7290, through interactions with V14 and T16 and inducing helix formation in residues 21–25 (Figure 2b right part) [52].

  The disordered transactivation unit-5 region (residues 350–448) of the androgen receptor is a crucial molecular recognition motif in the transactivation implicated in castration-resistant prostate cancer. Chemical shift perturbation indicated three nascent helical regions (R1, R2, and R3) as the primary binding sites for the drug molecule EPI-002 [53]. Binding was not detected with individual R1, R2, and R3 fragments, suggesting that the drug binds to interhelical interfaces. In simulations of the R2-R3 fragment (residues L391-G446) using REST2, EPI-002 primarily interacted with two aromatic residues, W397 and W433 [54]. In parallel simulations, a second-generation compound EPI-7170 showed a 2.5-fold increase in binding affinity. The latter interacted primarily with W397 and F437. The higher affinity of EPI-7170 derived from the greater propensity of its dichlorinated phenyl ring in forming π-stacking with the aromatic residues.

  Some IDPs are amyloidogenic, for which inhibition of aggregation by small molecules is a promising therapeutic approach. Simulations have revealed different modes of drug action on Aβ42 and α-synuclein. For Aβ42, simulations using an adaptive sampling technique showed that, through π-π interactions with residues F4, Y10, F19, and F20, the aggregation-inhibition molecule 10074-G5 kept the IDP from sampling very compact conformations, which could potentially lead to aggregation [55]. (By contrast, an earlier simulation study proposed that small molecule-induced collapse prevented Aβ42 aggregation [56].) For α-synuclein, chemical shift perturbation indicated that the drug molecule Fasudil localized in the aggregation-prone C-terminal residues 121–140 while MD simulations showed elevated contact probabilities at the C-terminal residues (Figure 2c left) [57]. Electrostatic and π-π interactions were frequently formed with D135/E137 and Y133/Y136, respectively (Figure 2c right).

  Interestingly, α-synuclein residues showing high contact probabilities with the drug also have elevated R2 values. This correlation can be explained by the argument above regarding the relation between R2 and the drive for LLPS: the propensity for a residue to form intramolecular interactions is similar to that for forming intermolecular interactions with a drug.


### All-atom simulations of phase-separated systems enable direct comparison with experimental measurements
  Starting with a homogeneous solution of 64 copies of a tetrapeptide FFssFF, Mazarakos et al. [58] observed spontaneous LLPS in simulations. This was achieved by starting at an initial concentration inside the spinodal, leading to LLPS by spinodal decomposition; the approach was hence dubbed SpiDec. The SpiDec approach was used to simulate the LLPS of eight nonpolar tetrapeptides, reproducing the different condensate morphologies of these peptides observed on a microscope [43]. A dispersivity parameter, defined as the fraction of surrounding molecules that moved away after 1 μs of simulation, captured the order of tetrapeptides in the speed of condensate fusion, which involves breaking and reforming intermolecular contacts. In further simulations of two-phase coexistence in an elongated box (Figure 3a), both the dilute-phase concentrations (Figure 3b, c) and the dense-phase viscosities of FFssFF and LLssLL matched well with experimental data. The latter, derived from mean-square-displacement (Figure 3d), showed a 7-fold difference and was attributed to stronger interactions and a higher concentration in FFssFF condensates.

  Galvanetto et al. [21] simulated the dense phase of a prothymosin-α / histone H1.0 mixture (at 96 and 80 copies, respectively). The resulting chain reconfiguration time of prothymosin-α matched the experimental value of 400 ns, which was only three times longer than the counterpart in the heterodimer (see above) despite a 300-fold increase in macroscopic viscosity. This discrepancy was rationalized by experimental data showing that the effective viscosity increased with the size of the moving species. Indeed, the calculated diffusion constants of prothymosin-α in water and the dense phase differed by ~30-fold, implicating a 30-fold difference in effective viscosity. Due to the high net charges of the two IDPs, the heterotypic LLPS was strongly attenuated by salt.

  In contrast, the homotypic LLPS of the hnRNPA1 low-complexity domain (A1 for short) was promoted by salt [59]. MacAinsh et al. [60] performed all-atom simulations of 8 copies of A1 over 50 to 1000 mM NaCl to explain this observation. The A1 chains did not condense at 50 mM NaCl and showed moderate and high levels of condensation at 150–300 and 500–1000 mM NaCl, respectively. A1 has a large (+9) net charge; the first effect of NaCl was to neutralize the net charge by binding an excess amount of Cl– over Na+. Notably, the ions bind to not only charged sidechains but also backbone amide and carbonyl. Moreover, Na+ and Cl– frequently bridged between A1 chains to fortify intermolecular interaction networks. At high concentrations, NaCl also indirectly strengthened π-π, cation-π, and amino-π interactions, by drawing water away from the interaction partners. Based on this understanding of salt effects, MacAinsh et al. defined four distinct classes of salt dependence for the homotypic LLPS of IDPs and predicted these classes from amino-acid composition, specifically the fractions of charged residues, net charges, and aromatic residues. A1 represents the class with high net charges and high aromatic residues, where LLPS starts at intermediate salt and is further promoted by high salt. Compared to all-atom simulations, coarse-grained simulations capture the net charge neutralization effect [61] but miss ion binding to the backbone and indirect strengthening of π-type interactions. ATP drives the LLPS of IDPs with a large positive net charge; all-atom simulations revealed that this occurs because ATP can bridge between IDP chains by forming a variety of interactions including salt bridge, cation-π, π-π, and hydrogen bonds [62].


### AI and ML expand the reach of all-atom simulations
  Recently, AI and ML have been employed to expand the conformational space of IDPs. Generative autoencoders, trained on 10%−30% of a few μs of MD simulations, rapidly produced conformations of IDPs, which mimicked those sampled from the rest of the simulations [3]. IDP conformations, represented by Cartesian coordinates, were mapped to the latent space by the encoder part of an autoencoder; the conformational distribution in the latent space was fit to a multivariate Gaussian. Random latent vectors generated from this Gaussian were mapped back to Cartesian space by the decoder part of the autoencoder. A similar study used variational autoencoders, which included a variational layer between the encoder and decoder that constrained latent vectors to a specified Gaussian distribution [63]. In another study, a diffusion model-based approach called IDPFold was developed to generate conformational ensembles of IDPs from their sequences [64]. Initial training was done on 25,495 NMR and crystal structures and 77,600 conformations from coarse-grained simulations of 3,880 IDPs [4], after extracting sequence features using a protein language model. Additional training was done on 1 μs all-atom simulations of 27 IDPs.

  The foregoing methods do not have information about the transitions between the generated conformations over time. Löhr et al. [65] employed the variational approach for Markov processes network (VAMPNet) [66] to determine the interconversion rates of Aβ42 between conformational states. From 315 μs of all-atom simulations with the adaptive sampling technique, inter-residue distances at two snapshots separated by a lag time of 5 ns were fed to the VAMPNet to produce state assignments for the two snapshots. This information was used to calculate the transition probabilities between four states, the mean lifetimes of these states (1 to 2.5 μs), and the transition times between the states (1–12 μs). A follow-up study of Aβ42 bound with 10074-G5 (see above) [55] found a significant slowdown, from 234 ± 77 μs−1 to 11 ± 11 μs−1, in the transition rate from an extended conformational state to a compact state by the drug. The same approach captured intermediates along the binding pathway of α-MoRE and the X domain [67]. Among 12 conformational states, four were native-like bound states and interconverted on timescales of 80 to 500 ns.
