# Prediction of phase-separation propensities of disordered proteins from sequence


## Significance
  The formation of biomolecular condensates through the process of phase separation is thought to be a central organizing principle underlying cellular matter and has been shown to play a key role in many human diseases. We here describe a quantitative, sequence-based prediction model for the propensity for any disordered sequence to undergo phase separation. Our work combines molecular simulations and active learning to parameterize a machine learning model for phase separation. We show by comparison with both computational and wet lab experiments that our model accurately predicts simulated free energies of phase separation and predicts experimental saturation concentrations on par with simulations and may thus be used both to interpret and design experiments that link protein sequence and phase separation.


## Results and Discussion

### An Active Learning Protocol to Predict Transfer Free Energies from Sequence.
  We have previously shown that CALVADOS simulations give rise to dilute phase concentrations that are in good agreement with experiments for a range of proteins and variants 32). We therefore aimed to develop a PS predictor based on results from phase coexistence simulations using CALVADOS 2 (19, 32) (Fig. 1A), an improved version of CALVADOS fine-tuned to match experimental saturation concentrations. We used an active learning protocol to generate a diverse set of training data covering a large feature space in the human IDRome, so as to allow the model to correlate a broad range of sequence features to PS propensities (Fig. 1A). For the purpose of the active learning protocol, we initially built a support vector regression (SVR) model to predict the propensity of IDR systems to undergo homotypic PS, expressed as transfer free energies ΔG,[1]ΔG=kBTlncdilutecdense, from sequence features (Materials and Methods). We fixed simulation conditions to T = 293K and ionic strength I = 150 mM to be compatible with many in vitro experiments. The dynamic range of the simulations is roughly ΔG=−10 kBT to ΔG≈0 kBT; sequences that give rise to ΔG<−10 kBT have so little protein in the dilute phase during the simulation time that their propensity to PS cannot be distinguished. Similarly, sequences that are not predicted to undergo spontaneous PS (ΔG>0 kBT) will be assigned to ΔG=0 kBT because we cannot detect any stable condensate (Materials and Methods).

  At each learning iteration, we retrained the SVR model on the current set of ΔG values obtained from coexistence simulations. The input features of the SVR model are listed in Table 1 and defined in SI Appendix, Table S1. These features encode the physics of the CALVADOS 2 force field and properties previously found to be important for PS.

  Through an automated protocol, we selected new sequences to simulate out of a pool of 90% of the human IDRome (IDRome90). The remaining 10% (IDRome10) were held out as a test set that we only examined after having finalized model development and training. Briefly described, our active learning protocol selected new sequences for simulation based on three conditions: 1) Large range of ΔG values (roughly ΔG≈−10 kBT to ΔG≈0 kBT), 2) highest intermodel uncertainty in cross-validation, so as to explore sequences that the model was unsure about, and 3) large coverage of input sequence feature space.

  We monitored the convergence of the active learning protocol by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) and rms deviations (RMSD) between the SVR predictions and simulations of ΔG via cross-validation (80% training, 20% validation) as a function of the number of simulation sequence data points used for training (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The values of RMSD and r reached a plateau beyond ≈250 simulations (with a total of 362 simulations), and scatter plots of predicted vs. simulated ΔG show that the model can distinguish different PS propensities (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). We validated the convergence of the model for a set of 27 independent sequences from IDRome90 (Fig. 1B). We observed a strong improvement of the prediction accuracy up to ≈250 included simulated sequences, with only small improvements beyond. We therefore concluded that the training had converged.


### Dense Neural Network Improves Prediction Accuracy and Is Transferable.
  Having established convergence of the SVR prediction model, we pooled all simulation data from training and convergence test within the IDRome90 set, resulting in 362+27=389 sequences. We used these data to train two slightly different dense neural networks (NN): The first model predicts the transfer free energy ΔG (Eq. 1), whereas the second model predicts the natural logarithm of the saturation mass concentration of the IDR, i.e. of the dilute phase concentration in the coexistence simulations. For both dense NN models, we only selected input features with high predictive power alone or in pairs of input features (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We further removed Rg as input feature and replaced the apparent Flory scaling exponent ν by the surrogate model νSVR (55) to avoid requiring simulation work to calculate input features (Table 1). We optimized the network architecture via a grid search in parameter space (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Different architectures with two hidden layers gave very similar prediction performance (as measured by RMSD). We selected the model with hyperparameters α = 5 and 2×10 hidden layers for its combination of high performance and speed.

  The resulting ΔG and lncsat models showed excellent prediction accuracy, as measured by cross-validation (Fig. 2A and B). To test whether the NN models can be generalized to previously unseen data (i.e., data outside the sequence pool that could be selected during active learning of the SVR model), we predicted ΔG values for a test set of 26 held-out IDRome10 sequences. We find that the models predict simulated ΔG and lncsat for these independent sequences as accurately as for the IDRome90 sequences, thus concluding that the models predict simulated ΔG and lncsat with r > 0.9 and errors RMSD≈1kBT and RMSD≈0.8, respectively (Fig. 2 C and D).


### Benchmarking the Prediction Model with Experimental Data.
  The accurate prediction of CALVADOS 2 ΔG values by the NN model is encouraging, as the CALVADOS 2 model in turn has been fine-tuned to match experimental saturation concentrations (19). We therefore aimed to directly compare the NN predictions with experimental data.

  Previous simulation work has mostly focused on examining phase separation of variants of a small number of distinct sequences. Predicting absolute saturation concentrations across distinct and highly divergent IDR sequences is more difficult than assessing the effect of more local sequence perturbations, similar to the difficulty of predicting absolute protein stability compared to relative effects (57). To supplement the data in the literature and to test CALVADOS and our NN model more broadly and prospectively, we measured experimental saturation concentrations of six distinct (wild type) IDRs: EWSR1/ZNF384, Intracellular hyaluronan-binding protein 4, Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome chromosomal region 1 candidate gene B protein (SLC22A18AS), A-kinase anchor protein 8, THRAP3_THSD7A, and E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase Topors (SI Appendix, Experimental Methods; sequences, experimental conditions, and measured saturation concentrations in SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4). With this expanded dataset of measurements of phase separation of distinct sequences under comparable conditions, we separately compared the predicted saturation concentrations using the NN model with experimental PS data of divergent WT IDRs (Fig. 3A) and variants of hnRNPA1 LCD and LAF1 LCD (Fig. 3B) (SI Appendix, Table S2). None of these sequences were used during training of the predictor, but hnRNPA1 LCD and LAF1 LCD data were used in the original CALVADOS 2 parameterization work (19).

  We predicted saturation concentrations of the WT IDRs well, albeit with larger errors than for predictions of the simulation data (RMSD=1.12). This is likely due to inaccuracies in the CALVADOS 2 force field rather than the NN prediction model, because the NN prediction model and simulations estimate experimental saturation concentrations of five of the IDRs measured here equally well (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B) and agree better with each other than with the experimental data (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C).

  Sequence variant effects of A1 LCD variants were predicted with even larger error (RMSD=1.37) (Fig. 3B), whereas variant effects of LAF1 LCD variants were predicted excellently (RMSD=0.56). The decreased accuracy for A1 LCD variants is mostly due to four variants (A1Wminus, A1YtoW, A1FtoW, and A1allW), for which the predicted saturation concentrations were much higher than the experimental values (RMSD=3.12). All four outliers stem from variants where several residues were replaced with Trp residues, resulting in very large fractions of Trp residues compared to natural sequences in the IDRome (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This low abundance of Trp residues in natural proteins also suggests that the stickiness λ value for Trp in the CALVADOS 2 simulation model has greater uncertainty. Excluding the four Trp-rich outliers results in excellent A1 LCD variant prediction with RMSD=0.55 comparable to the LAF1 variants. Thus, we conclude that the NN model captures the propensity to phase separate as well as the CALVADOS model, and that the model is in overall relatively good agreement with experiments, with larger inaccuracies across highly divergent sequences or for proteins with unusually large fractions of Trp residues.


### Improving the CALVADOS Model Using the NN As a Surrogate Model.
  The data above suggest that λ(Trp) in the CALVADOS 2 FF is underestimated. While CALVADOS was originally parameterized using experimental data reporting on the conformational properties of IDRs in the dilute regime, it has been shown that one can also parameterize energy functions using measurements of phase separation (39, 58). Based on these ideas we explored whether the neural network model we have developed could also be used as a surrogate model for force field optimization (59, 60). Specifically, because the NN uses several features that explicitly depend on the force field parameters (λ) we hypothesized that the model could be used to predict changes in PS depending on changes in λ, and therefore to refine λ(Trp).

  To test this idea, we first analyzed predictions for a set of previously reported IDR coexistence simulations (35) performed using the CALVADOS 2 and CALVADOS 3 force fields (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The λ parameters in CALVADOS 3 were optimized using both IDRs and multidomain proteins and perform on par with CALVADOS 2 for prediction of IDR properties (35). We compared the simulation results with predictions using the λ values from CALVADOS 2 and 3 as input to the NN. As expected, if the sequence features entering the predictor were calculated with CALVADOS 2 λ parameters, the predictor performs better on CALVADOS 2 simulation data than a predictor using sequence features calculated with CALVADOS 3 parameters (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Vice versa, encoding sequence features using CALVADOS 3 parameters leads to better prediction accuracy of CALVADOS 3 simulation results than using CALVADOS 2 parameters. In fact, prediction of CALVADOS 3 simulation results using CALVADOS 3 parameters has an accuracy comparable to prediction of CALVADOS 2 simulation results using CALVADOS 2 parameters, with only slight increase in prediction error (RMSD=0.59 vs. RMSD=0.47) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B), which is lower than the deviation between CALVADOS 2 and 3 simulation results (RMSD=0.84) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Thus, we conclude that we can use the NN model as a surrogate model to explore the effects of changing the λ values on PS without needing to perform extensive coexistence simulations.

  Based on these observations, we predicted saturation concentrations of all WT and variant sequences discussed above (including the four Trp outliers) scanning λ(Trp) values between 0.9 and 1.8 (Fig. 3C). The predictions of all the pooled experimental data with the original CALVADOS 2 parameters (λ(Trp)=0.99) have RMSD=1.28. RMSD decreases with increasing λ(Trp) down to a minimum of RMSD=0.71 for λ(Trp)≈1.48, above which it increases again. Thus, predictions of dilute phase concentrations improve substantially, in particular for the A1-LCD variants with varying number of Trp residues, using λ(Trp)=1.48 compared to those from the original model (Fig. 3D).

  The original λ-pagination parameters were derived using experiments reporting on single-chain properties, and we thus asked how the substantially increased value of λ(Trp) would affect the radius of gyration and the agreement to experiments. We therefore performed single-chain simulations of all Trp-containing sequences that were used to parameterize or benchmark the CALVADOS 2 and 3 models (19, 35) to quantify the effect of the updated Trp parameter on the accuracy of the estimated radii of gyration (SI Appendix, Table S5 and Fig. S7). Increasing λ(Trp) from 0.99 to 1.48 had only minimal effect on the single-chain radius of gyration, with similar relative errors (Rgpred−Rgexp)/Rgexp and slightly lower χ2=Nseq−1∑i=1Nseq[(Rg,ipred−Rg,iexp)/σiexp]2 is the reported experimental error and Nseq the number of IDR sequences considered. Thus, because of the low abundance of Trp residues in most naturally occurring proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), simulations of single-chain compaction are relatively insensitive to the λ value for Trp.

  We note that while these results are encouraging and demonstrate that we can use the NN model as a surrogate to optimize the force field, the results are preliminary. Specifically, the increased value of λ(Trp) is mostly dependent on a few measurements titrating Trp residues in a single protein while keeping other λ values constant, and future work is needed to refine the analyses. In all analyses below we thus use the original CALVADOS 2 parameter set.


### PS Predictions Are Interpretable with Sequence Features.
  Having developed and benchmarked our NN model, we proceeded to use it to shed light on the role of various sequence features on PS and how they relate to one another. We thus used the NN model to predict ΔG for all sequences in the human IDRome, again noting that the dynamic range of our simulations and analyses corresponds to −10≲ΔG/kBT≲0. The distribution of ΔG is strongly skewed toward sequences with weak or non-PS values (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Only 571 (2%), 892 (3%), or 1,420 (5%) out of the 27,663 sequences in the IDRome (≤800 residues) are predicted to undergo PS when using PS thresholds of ΔG<−4kBT, −3kBT, or −2kBT, respectively. Therefore, only a small fraction of IDRs in the IDRome are predicted to undergo PS without partners at the given conditions (T = 293 K, I = 150 mM).

  We correlated the predicted ΔG values with each of the eight sequence features (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S1) that we use as input to the NN model (Fig. 4). ΔG correlates as expected with features describing the strengths of hydrophobic interactions and hydrophobic patterning: The mean sequence stickiness λ¯, fraction of aromatic residues, and sequence hydropathy decoration SHD (which also contains contributions from sequence composition and length) show negative correlation with ΔG. The AHpairs feature is always negative and decreases for sequence compositions with large λ¯ and bulky residues [i.e. large interaction volume of the Ashbaugh-Hatch (AH) potential; see SI Appendix, Table S1]. AHpairs shows positive correlation with ΔG, indicating higher PS propensity for sequences with sticky residues and large interaction volumes. Lower absolute NCPR correlates with lower ΔG, penalizing accumulation of net charge in the condensate, whereas the fraction of charged residues (FCR) appears to have only minimal effect on ΔG. The charge patterning parameter SCD is defined to be positive if charges of equal sign are spaced further apart in sequence than charges of opposite sign, and vice versa, with high weight on charges far apart (sequence nonlocal effects) (37, 61). ΔG correlates with SCD, but only in the left (negative) tail of the SCD distribution, suggesting that charge patterning becomes important for PS in the case of “charge-patchy” sequences with highly segregated opposite charges. The predicted single-chain scaling exponent νSVR shows positive correlation with increased predicted PS propensity (low ΔG), in accordance with previous work (10, 11, 36, 62).

  Together, these results show that the NN learned overall effects of physical properties that have previously been shown to affect PS and which are captured in the CALVADOS model. The high SDs across individual bins in Fig. 4 indicate that none of the individual features that we analyzed can quantitatively predict the PS propensities. In addition to highlighting the complex interplay between features, we also note that some of the features, including SHD and SCD, have been derived to capture properties of the sequence at fixed composition and length, and were therefore not designed to be used alone across the diverse set of IDRome sequences (63).

  We also investigated the dependence of ΔG on sets of two combined features, e.g., λ¯ and all other features, or SCD and all other features (SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S10). The corresponding 2D histograms show which combinations of features allow a clear distinction between low and high ΔG values. The combinations (λ¯,νSVR), (λ¯, SHD), and (λ¯, SCD) show clear ΔG separation, as do the combinations (SCD, SHD), (SCD, AHpairs), and (SCD, νSVR). Like our previously described model for single-chain compaction (55), our PS predictor therefore likely uses several related features to disentangle effects of sequence composition, patterning, and length.

  We also trained NN models on a reduced set of input features, using only one feature or combinations of two features as input (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Combinations of descriptors of sequence hydrophobicity and the predicted single-chain scaling exponent (which itself uses several features as input) performed best. All models using one or two input features were much less accurate compared to the full model and we found that including all features was needed for quantitative predictions of ΔG.

  We investigated, how confidently the model predicts ΔG for different regions in feature space. Using the data from 389 simulation results in the IDRome90 set, we trained a model to predict the unsigned prediction error of ΔG (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) based on sequence features and predicted ΔG values. The prediction-error model underestimated the compounded simulation and prediction error (RMSD=0.7 kBT vs. 1.0 kBT) and is only modestly correlated with the true unsigned prediction error of ΔG (r = 0.545; SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). The error for the ΔG model and the predicted error for the ΔG model only depend weakly on the simulated ΔG (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and C) or predicted ΔG (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). In light of these results, we instead report the RMSD of the IDRome10 test set [(RMSD(ΔG)≈1kBT and RMSD(lncsat)≈0.8] as global estimates of the prediction errors.


### Correlation Between Single-Chain Features and PS Propensity.
  We then proceeded to analyze the relationship between sequence, the conformational properties of single chains and PS. In line with expectations for homopolymers, we and others have found that IDRs are more expanded in homotypic condensates than in dilute solution of a poor solvent (water) (22, 32, 42). To examine these effects more broadly, we calculated the apparent internal-distance scaling exponent, ν, in the dilute and dense phases of 110 of the 389 training data sequences that we simulated during the active learning protocol and which had −10<ΔG/kBT<−4. While the chain compaction in the dilute phase varies substantially across sequences, in agreement with the compaction estimated from single-chain simulations, the IDRs all have ν ≈ 0.5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12) in the dense phase, in line with the condensates acting as a Θ solvent for the IDRs.

  It has been shown that measures of single-chain compaction such as ν are correlated with the PS propensity for related variants of given sequences (10, 11, 36, 62). We leveraged our fast model to screen thousands of sequence variants in order to learn which features might affect ν in the dilute phase and ΔG differently, noting that for heteropolymers the apparent internal-distance scaling exponent is not a unique measure of chain compaction. To this aim, we performed Monte Carlo (MC) sampling in sequence space to explore how our ΔG model reacts to sequence perturbations, starting from a range of weakly-to-intermediately PS-prone sequences (−4<ΔG/kBT<−1) and driving the sequences toward stronger predicted PS. We performed 200 MC runs each for different MC setups, with each setup corresponding to a set of sequence features that were restrained (“locked”) during the MC run. For illustration, we show in Fig. 5 rows (A–D) the evolution of ΔG against selected sequence features for 40 example MC runs under different restraints. The net distances traveled in ΔG and feature space are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S13 for all 200 runs for an extended set of features and conditions.

  We first determined the effect of a MC run in sequence space toward low ΔG (strong PS) via swap moves only, i.e., reshuffling the residues at a given IDR composition. The fixed composition is represented by a “locked” Jensen–Shannon divergence (JS Div.) in amino acid distribution with respect to the starting sequence (Fig. 5A). We observed clear positive correlations between changes in νSVR and ΔG as well as charge patterning (SCD) and ΔG, in agreement with earlier findings (10, 11, 27, 36, 62). In contrast, we do not see a strong effect of hydrophobic patchiness (SHD) for a given composition (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). To explore whether sequence changes can decouple PS and single-chain expansion, we performed MC runs locking single-chain expansion νSVR in addition to the sequence composition. In contrast to the situation above, we find that these two restraints effectively stall the MC runs (Fig. 5B), impeding progress toward low ΔG. Together, these results highlight that for a fixed sequence composition, single-chain expansion and PS propensity are often tightly linked by charge effects and cannot easily be decoupled. In this context, we note recent work that shows differential effects of changes in sequence-local and nonlocal charge patterning on chain compaction and phase separation (61).

  We then asked which changes in sequence composition (in addition to patterning) may possibly break the observed coupling between single-chain scaling exponent νSVR and PS propensity, i.e., which changes in the sequence affect ΔG while maintaining fixed single-chain expansion. We therefore performed additional MC runs in sequence space toward low predicted ΔG, this time allowing swap moves and substitutions to any of the 19 other residue types (i.e. changing sequence composition).

  When locking only νSVR, the MC algorithm drives the sequence toward stronger PS by increasing the average stickiness λ¯ of the chain while moderately decreasing segregation of opposite charges and overall charge content (i.e., increasing SCD and decreasing FCR) (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Fig. S13C). This suggests that the algorithm maintains the same (locked) single-chain expansion by compensating an increased chain stickiness with decreased charge content and separation, indicating that single-chain expansion reacts more sensitively than PS propensity to changes in charge separation along the sequence. In addition, the sequence is driven away from negative NCPR, but it is unclear if this is a side-effect of the higher stickiness of Arginine (λ = 0.73) compared to Aspartate (λ = 0.04) or Glutamate (λ = 0.00) in the CALVADOS 2 force field, or indicates a connection between slightly positive net charge and phase separation, possibly due to ion effects learned by CALVADOS 2 and, in turn, the NN predictor (see also ref. 22).

  When locking NCPR and νSVR, λ¯ increases slightly during the MC runs and FCR decreases on average while SCD is largely unaffected (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). Locking further sequence properties generally stalls the MC runs, as no sequences are found that satisfy all restraints and still progress toward low ΔG. Interestingly, locking λ¯, in addition to νSVR and NCPR, still allows some limited progress toward low ΔG. This is achieved by replacing residue types of small diameter with larger residues of similar λ values, which effectively increases the interaction range, as captured by the AHpairs parameter (SI Appendix, Fig. S13F).

  The key findings from our sequence exploration runs are as follows: 1) For a given sequence composition, changes in SCD and νSVR are strongly correlated with changes in PS propensities. 2) For a given composition, ΔG and νSVR are so tightly coupled that we could not substantially change one without affecting the other. 3) Globally, hydrophobic sequences with low FCR and low charge patterning as well as less hydrophobic sequences with high FCR and high charge patterning can have the same νSVR but substantially different PS propensities, with the more hydrophobic sequences with low FCR and low charge patterning showing stronger PS.


### Limitations.
  CALVADOS was trained to reproduce biophysical measurements of single-chain conformational properties and has been shown to reproduce both single-chain and PS properties that it was not trained on. We therefore rationalized that we could build an accurate prediction method for PS by targeting CALVADOS simulations. Nevertheless, these in silico predictions of homotypic PS may not capture all relevant properties of the densely crowded, heterogeneous environment in the cell (64–67). For example, while CALVADOS has been shown to capture some effects of varying the ionic strength on PS (19), it will not capture specific effects due to ion-specific asymmetrical partitioning in condensates (14). Similarly, sequences that do not undergo homotypic PS (for example highly charged sequences) may undergo PS with oppositely charged molecules in the cell. Likewise, other discrepancies between the in vitro and in vivo conditions will limit the model. While we have validated our prediction methods for natural sequences from the human IDRome, it is possible that they will be less accurate for nonnatural sequences. We note, however, that sequence design based on CALVADOS has shown transferability outside the realm of natural sequences (27).

  Furthermore, our predictors inherit the strengths and limitations of the CALVADOS 2 model. In particular, the ΔG and csat estimations from CALVADOS 2 direct coexistence simulations have a relative error, ⟨|csat, sim−csat, exp|/csat, exp⟩, of 90% (35), corresponding to an RMSD of ln(csat[g/L]) of 0.73 (not including the Trp-rich outliers highlighted in Fig. 3B as no simulation data are available for these sequences). Moreover, we deliberately trained our NN model to reproduce PS at a fixed temperature and ionic strength. Although training at different conditions is possible, the CALVADOS model does not fully capture variation of PS with temperature, as only the electrostatic term of the force field is temperature dependent via Eq. 5, whereas the effect of temperature on residue stickiness is not captured in the model. Furthermore, the description of electrostatic interactions based on a Debye-Hückel screening term with fixed cutoff of 4 nm has limitations at both very high and low ionic strengths and also neglects some ion- and pH-specific effects (12–15, 68, 69).


## Conclusion
  We have developed ML models to quantitatively predict homotypic PS of IDRs at physiologically relevant conditions. We devised and implemented an active learning approach to select the most relevant simulation data to train a model that estimates PS globally across diverse sequences. While previous models have been developed to classify sequences into those that phase separate and those that do not, we are not aware of other models to predict the saturation concentration and transfer free energies for a wide set of disordered protein sequences.

  Our results are thus complementary to recent work by Ginell et al. (53), where, leveraging the pairwise interaction parameters of CALVADOS 2 (19) and a modified form of Mpipi (33, 70) in a mean-field approach, the authors developed a model to rapidly compute interaction maps and semiquantitative phase diagrams between any pair of disordered proteins, validating their method with a range of biologically interesting systems.

  We envisage that our prediction methods may become valuable tools for experimentalists and theoreticians to obtain rapid and accurate estimates of in vitro PS propensities of IDRs before performing costly experiments or simulations and to design and interpret experimental and computational studies. Our ML models may also be used to explore more widely the relationship between sequence and PS properties and to link biological properties, disease, and PS at the proteome level. For example, two of the IDRs with experimental saturation concentrations determined here are part of fusion oncoproteins (EWSR1/ZNF384 and THRAP3_THSD7), and our model could help quantify more generally the propensity of disordered regions in fusion oncoproteins for forming condensates (71). In a similar spirit as a recently described approach (58), we have provided proof of principle of how our model can help refine force fields against measurements of phase separation. The code for our model is freely available, and we also provide easy access via an online implementation. Finally, by providing access to a unique and large set of direct-coexistence simulations for a wide range of sequences, we enable detailed analysis and insights into the relationship between sequence and PS properties including analyses of the structure and thermodynamics of PS.


## Materials and Methods

### CALVADOS 2 Force Field.
  We performed molecular dynamics simulations using the coarse-grained CALVADOS 2 model (19). As with similar HPS models (28, 72), each protein residue is represented by one bead with size σ and interaction strength λ.

  The full model is a linear combination of contributions to the potential energy,[2]UHPS=ubond+uDH+uAH,

  with ubond the bonded potential, uDH a Debye-Hückel electrostatic potential, and uAH the Ashbaugh-Hatch modification of a Lennard-Jones potential (72).

  Beads of neighboring residues in the sequence are connected by bonds described by a harmonic potential,[3]ubond(r)=12k(r−r0)2,

  using k = 8033 kJ mol−1 nm−2 as force constant and r0=0.38 nm as equilibrium distance.

  A Debye-Hückel potential describes the solvent-screened electrostatic interactions,[4]uDH(r)=qiqj4πϵ0ϵrexp(−r/D)r,

  with qi the charge of bead i, ϵ0 the vacuum permittivity, D=1/(8πBI) the Debye length of an electrolyte solution of ionic strength I, and B(ϵr) the Bjerrum length of temperature-dependent dielectric constant ϵr (73),[5]ϵr(T)=5321T+233.76−0.9297T+1.417×10−3T2−8.292×10−7T3.

  Electrostatic interactions were truncated and shifted at the cutoff distance rc=4 nm.

  Nonelectrostatic nonbonded interactions were represented by a truncated and shifted Ashbaugh-Hatch (AH) potential (72). It is a scaled Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential of the following functional form,[6]uAH(r)=uLJ(r)−λuLJ(rc)+ϵ(1−λ),r≤21/6σλ[uLJ(r)−uLJ(rc)],21/6σ<r≤rc0,r>rc,

  with σ=(σi+σj)/2, λ=(λi+λj)/2 for residues i and j, and the LJ potential[7]uLJ(r)=4ϵσr12−σr6,

  where ϵ=0.8368 kJ mol−1 and rc=2 nm.


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations and Estimation of PS Propensities.

#### Direct coexistence simulations.
  We used the openMM v8.0 simulation package (74) to perform molecular dynamics simulations. Proteins were inserted into an elongated simulation box with dimensions 25 nm × 25 nm × 300 nm for sequences with more than 350 residues and 20 nm × 20 nm × 200nm otherwise. Initial configurations were fully elongated proteins (along the z direction) packed in parallel in the box center in the z direction.

  We performed simulations in the NVT ensemble with a Langevin integrator (γ = 0.01 ps−1) with timestep of 0.01 ps. Protein configurations were saved at either 1 ns or 10 ns intervals. The first 600 ns of each simulation were discarded to account for equilibration (19). All simulations were run at temperature T=293K and ionic strength I = 150 mM.

  Time-averaged concentrations in the dense and dilute phases (particle density maps) were calculated with custom scripts (75). The scripts center the slab in the z direction based on a heuristic estimate of the center of density. This analysis assumes that there is at most one condensed phase in the simulation. Visual inspection of all density time series revealed that 12 simulations from the IDRome90 training set, two simulations from the IDRome10 validation set, and one simulation from the simulation set corresponding to the experimental data in this work showed the presence of two or more condensed phases; these represent simulations that did not converge to a single stable phase during the predefined simulation time. Since the analysis framework would erroneously interpret a smaller condensate as belonging to the dilute phase, and thus overestimate csat, these were removed from model training and validation. We note that the two simulations that were removed from the IDRome10 set were identified before assessing model accuracy. We show the time series for the 15 simulations in SI Appendix, Fig. S14. The 15 proteins that show this behavior are predicted to phase separate but have a high absolute NCPR and FCR when compared to those IDRs of the IDRome90 dataset (n = 389) that undergo PS (ΔG<−2kBT) (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

  The boundaries between dense and dilute phase were determined by fitting a hyperbolic tangent to the concentration profile, as described previously (19):[8]ρ(z)=(ρa+ρb)/2+(ρb−ρa)/2×tanh[(|z|−zDS)/t],

  with ρa and ρb the densities of the dense and dilute phases, respectively.

  The dense and dilute phases are estimated to be in regions |z|<zDS−βdenset and |z|>zDS+βdilutet, with βdense from 0.5 to 1.5 and βdilute from 2.5 to 6.0. The values for each protein are listed in the dataframes on the GitHub repository associated with this work. zDS and t are the position of the dividing surface and thickness of the interface, respectively.


#### Single-chain simulations.
  In order to compare chain expansion from slab and single-chain simulations, we performed single-chain simulations for a subset (n = 110) of sequences simulated in the course of the active learning protocol that show PS with −10<ΔG/kBT<−4. We performed each single-chain simulation in a simulation box of 25 nm × 25 nm × 25 nm at the same ensemble and conditions as the direct coexistence simulations. Simulations were carried out for 200 ns simulation time. The first 20 ns of each simulation run were discarded as equilibration.

  We performed additional single-chain simulations of a set of 51 IDR sequences containing Tryptophan (Trp) residues and with experimentally determined radii of gyration to assess the effect of changes in the λ(Trp) parameter on single-chain expansion. For each sequence, we ran one simulation with CALVADOS 2 parameters and one simulation with CALVADOS 2 parameters but modified λ(Trp)=1.48. Simulations were carried out for 1 μs at experimental temperatures, ionic strengths, and pH values, with other simulation conditions as above. 20 ns of each simulation run were discarded as equilibration. The sequences, radii of gyration, and references to the experimental data are listed in SI Appendix, Table S5.


### IDR Sequence Selection by Active Learning.
  We selected IDR sequences for the direct coexistence simulations in a multistep process that we devised to maximize model performance at minimal computational cost. Before initiating the model we selected 10% of the IDRome (IDRome10) to be used for final assessment of the model and did not analyze these sequences until the final analysis (76). The remaining 90% of the IDRome are denoted as IDRome90.

  We first collected initial seed simulations performed at the same temperature and ionic strength in previous work. The seed consisted of 38 YTH domain protein IDRs (75, 77) and 28 additional simulations from unpublished projects.

  We then devised an active learning protocol to explore additional IDR sequences for simulation. During each step in the active learning procedure, we trained an SVR model with parameters C = 10 and ϵ = 0.01 to predict transfer free energies ΔG (Eq. 1) to partition into the dense phase. We used the sklearn python package (78) for all ML models in this work. Out of a pool of sequence features, the algorithm selects the combination of three features that gives the best prediction (measured by the Pearson correlation coefficient, r). The pool of features is listed in Table 1. The features are defined in SI Appendix, Table S1.

  In these analyses, we used ν obtained from analyses of single-chain simulations (55). We determined the prediction accuracy as the average Pearson correlation coefficient, r, on the validation set from cross-validation for each feature combination, each with 80% and 20% of simulations randomly chosen as training and validation set, respectively. The set of 50 models from different train-validation splits with average highest-performing input feature combination was then used to predict ΔG for all sequences in the IDRome90 set, resulting in 50 predicted ΔG per IDRome90 sequence. Under the assumption that a large cross-model uncertainty indicates lack of accuracy for specific types of sequences in the IDRome90 (76), we restricted the pool of new sequences to simulate to the top 100 sequences with highest ΔG variance. Out of these 100 sequences, we picked 5 to 10 sequences maximizing the distance in feature space, as calculated by the Mahalanobis distance (dM). We first selected the highest variance sequence for simulations; then we selected a second sequence (out of the 100 sequences) with the highest dM to the first sequence, then a third sequence by maximizing the sum of dM to the first two sequences, etc., resulting in 5 to 10 new sequences to simulate based on available computational resources at each iteration. Based on this protocol, we iteratively selected and simulated a total of 137 sequences. If a selected sequence coincided with a sequence from the initial seed simulations, only the latest simulation was retained.

  Following this first phase of sequence exploration, we modified the active learning algorithm to focus the learning on a more uniform range of predicted ΔG values. We therefore added another criterion to the procedure in the above-described protocol: In the modified protocol, we selected the top 5 to 10 sequences with highest cross-model variances (top 50%) and dM separately for bins of ΔG (in units of kBT): [−∞,−6], [−6,−5], [−5,−4], [−4,−3], [−3,−2], [−2,−1], and [−1,1]. In this way, we selected sequences with different values of predicted ΔG for further simulations. We iteratively selected and simulated 179 additional sequences based on this modified protocol.

  Once the model appeared to have converged, we selected additional sequences for a final convergence test from within the IDRome90 set, drawing four new sequences randomly from each predicted ΔG bin, with the same brackets as above.


### Dense Neural Models to Predict Transfer Free Energies and Saturation Concentrations from the Final Set of Simulations.
  We built and trained two small dense neural networks (NN) to predict ΔG and lncsat from sequence features. These models were trained on the final set of 389 phase coexistence simulations gathered from the three-step procedure described above.

  We chose the input features listed in SI Appendix, Table S1 except N, Mw, and κ, as those showed limited prediction accuracy alone or using pairs of features (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We also removed Rg to restrict the input to features that can rapidly be generated from sequence without requiring simulation work. In the SVR models described above we used values for the apparent scaling exponent (ν) based on single-chain simulations (55); for the NN we instead used an accurate sequence-based SVR model νSVR (55). The prediction of νSVR in turn uses SCD, SHD, κ, FCR, and λ¯ as input features (55). The final input features for the NN were thus λ¯, faro, SHD, NCPR, FCR, SCD, AHpairs, and νSVR (Table 1). We note that several of these features were designed to be used individually for fixed sequence composition and length and that combining them as input to the NN helps overcome this limitation.

  We performed a hyperparameter grid optimization for the regularization parameter α and architecture of hidden layers, converging on a final set of parameters, α = 5 and two hidden layers of 10 nodes each (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). As for the SVR model, the accuracy of the model was determined by 50 train-validation splits (80% training, 20% validation), using Pearson’s r and RMSD as metrics.

  We selected 26 sequences from the IDRome10 for final assessment of model accuracy. As for the IDRome90 convergence test above, the sequences were selected randomly from bins of predicted ΔG values, now using the NN predictor instead of the SVR predictor to sort sequences into ΔG bins. We used the same ΔG bin definitions as above.


### Monte-Carlo Simulations in Sequence Space.
  We performed Monte-Carlo (MC) sampling in sequence space to explore how sequence variations by swaps or substitutions relate to changes in PS propensities. The sequence length N was fixed to the initial sequence length.

  At each iteration, the algorithm chose randomly between swap moves or substitutions with equal probability (unless only swap moves were allowed). If swap moves were chosen by the algorithm, the residue types of 5 pairs of positions in the sequence were swapped (attempted swaps of identical residue types or positions led to repeated tries). If substitution moves were chosen by the algorithm, 5 random residues along the sequence were substituted with any of the other 19 residue types with equal probability.

  The algorithm computed the features and predicted ΔG of the resulting trial sequences. The set of 5 moves (swap or substitutions) were collectively accepted or rejected by the algorithm. To be accepted, the features needed to be within tolerance of the restraints, where applicable, with tolerances ΔνSVR=±0.001, Δλ¯=±0.002, ΔAHpairs=±0.002, ΔNCPR=±0.002, ΔSCD=±0.04, and ΔSHD=±0.02. In addition, the predicted ΔG of the trial sequence needed to satisfy a Metropolis criterion,[9]pacc(uold,unew)=1,unew≤uoldea(uold−unew),unew>uold,

  with a = 100 and u=k(x−xt)2, where x and xt are current and target value, respectively, and k = 0.3.


### Internal-Distance Scaling Exponent in Dense and Dilute Phase.
  We calculated the internal-distance scaling exponent ν separately for the dense and dilute phase. We rms averaged all intraprotein residue distances dij=⟨dij2⟩ (for pairs of residues i, j separated with sequence distance |j−i|) from proteins with center-of-mass in the designated region (e.g. dilute phase). ν was then obtained from a fit of dij=R0|j−i|ν to the data, with R0 as a flexible fit parameter and |j−i|>5.


### Experimental Measurement of Saturation Concentrations.
  The full Materials and Methods for experiments in this work are described in SI Appendix, Text. In brief, 6xHis-tagged IDR constructs were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) Gold cells, followed by sonication, centrifugation, and purification on a Ni-NTA column. The 6xHis-tag was cleaved with TEV protease and the proteins were purified again on a Ni-NTA and size exclusion column. The lengths and purities of the proteins were confirmed by SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). After desalting, phase separation was induced by adding NaCl to a final concentration of 150 mM, followed by centrifugation to separate the dense and dilute phase. The protein concentration of the dilute phase was measured via UV-Vis spectroscopy. The phase-separated solutions were imaged with differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).
