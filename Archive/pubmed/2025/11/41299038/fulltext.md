# A multimodal framework for comprehensive driver variant prediction in cancer


## Background
  Cancer genomes contain many mutations, but only a subset drive tumor development. Accurately pinpointing these driver variants remains challenging. We aim to build an accurate and interpretable model by combining DNA sequence, protein 3D structure, and cancer omics data.


## Methods
  We present ModVAR, a multimodal model that integrates DNA sequences, predicted protein tertiary structures, and cancer omics data to classify driver variants. The approach uses pre-trained models (DNAbert2 and ESMFold) and a self-supervised strategy for cancer omics profiles. We evaluate performance on clinically and experimentally validated driver variants with standard classification metrics, examine therapeutic relevance through molecular docking, assess modeling of variants in intrinsically disordered protein regions, and analyze modality contributions.


## Results
  Here we show that ModVAR achieves strong accuracy across benchmarks for identifying validated driver variants. It prioritizes variants with potential therapeutic actionability supported by docking analyses, and the inclusion of structural predictions enables effective modeling of variants in intrinsically disordered regions. Interpretation indicates that the protein structure modality contributes most to predictions. At scale, the method produces 3,971,946 publicly available variant annotations.


## Conclusions
  ModVAR integrates sequence, structure, and cancer omics signals to aid driver-variant discovery. It provides robust performance across tasks, supports hypothesis generation and target discovery, and supplies a large-scale resource that advances cancer research and personalized therapy.


## Introduction
  Cancer is a multifaceted disease profoundly influenced by genetic variants1. Among these, a small subset known as driver variants undergoes positive selection and directly contributes to oncogenesis2, while the majority are neutral passengers with no impact on tumorigenesis3. Unraveling the identity of driver variants is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms behind cancer4. Currently, a wealth of cancer genomics data is available through collaborative initiatives such as the Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC)5, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)6, and the International Cancer Genome Consortium7. These comprehensive resources provide an invaluable repository of mutations observed across a wide spectrum of cancer types. However, the abundance of passenger variants poses a great challenge in accurately identifying key cancer driver mutations within these vast datasets8. The lack of confirmed driver variants significantly hampers progress in precision medicine, hindering the creation and application of targeted therapies aimed at tackling the genetic causes of cancer.

  To bridge the gap in the understanding of driver variants, computational methodologies have been developed, drawing on the synergy of statistical and machine learning techniques. These approaches harness the vast potential of sequence data and biological patterns, advancing our ability to predict the pathogenicity of previously unannotated variants. By integrating multi-omics annotations, tools such as CADD9 and the unsupervised Eigen10 method stand out, offering comprehensive assessments by consolidating diverse functional annotations into unified metrics. Furthermore, sophisticated algorithms like DeepSEA11 enhance predictive accuracy by merging DNA sequence information with data on chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding. Additionally, approaches like M-CAP12 build upon existing prediction frameworks, combining nine established pathogenicity likelihood scores with a suite of other relevant annotations. Similarly, REVEL13, employing a random-forest-based model, synthesizes 18 pathogenicity prediction scores, delivering nuanced and precise evaluations. Collectively, these innovations significantly bolster our capacity to identify and understand critical driver variants.

  Protein stability and functionality are closely linked to variant pathogenesis14,15, prompting the development of variant prediction methods based on protein sequence features. Therefore, prediction tools utilizing protein features have emerged in more recent years. EVE16 incorporates evolutionary information from protein multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) to enhance prediction accuracy. The advent of AlphaFold217 revolutionized protein structure prediction by analyzing the amino acid sequence and MSA information of proteins, and the prediction accuracy approached the experimental level, breaking the long-standing bottleneck in this field. This breakthrough was further expanded upon by AlphaFold318, which introduced a new model architecture and training data, integrated multiple related prediction tasks into one model, and constructed a broader model for predicting the structure of biomolecules, covering not only proteins, but also protein-ligand complexes and protein-DNA complexes. Around the time AlphaFold2 was released, several advanced protein structure prediction models were developed as well. RoseTTAFold19 uses a three-track network architecture to enhance prediction accuracy by integrating information from 1D sequences, 2D distance maps, and 3D coordinates. ESMFold20 employs a Transformer-based approach to bypass the need for searching the MSA. Recently, AlphaMissense21, built upon AlphaFold2, has been proposed for predicting the functional impact of missense variants. This model assesses the pathogenicity of a variant by analyzing structural alterations caused by the variant and their impact on protein function. The impressive performance of AlphaMissense highlights the potential of leveraging protein structure for predicting pathogenic variants, spurring the development of other prediction methods that incorporate protein tertiary structure features22. However, these methods primarily focus on modeling changes in protein structure, limiting their applicability to variants beyond missense variations. Besides, these methods do not comprehensively integrate multi-aspect features like DNA sequences and genomic annotations, which have proven valuable in previous analysis9–11. Furthermore, the majority of existing methods are not tailored to model the unique complexities of cancer, which is driven by a multitude of cellular abnormalities distinct from those in general genetic disorders23. Integrating cancer-specific omics data into predictive models holds significant promise for enhancing the identification of driver variants, thereby advancing our understanding and treatment of cancer24–27.

  To comprehensively model the complex interplay of genomic, structural, and cellular factors that drive cancer development, we introduce ModVAR, a novel multimodality driver variant prediction model. ModVAR leverages a sophisticated pre-training and fine-tuning architecture to extract and integrate features from three distinct modalities: DNA sequence, protein tertiary structure, and cancer omics data. This innovative fusion process yields highly informative pathogenicity scores for cancer variants. For model pre-training, we harnessed the power of DNAbert228 and ESMFold29 for the DNA sequence and protein modalities, respectively. For the cancer omics modality, we developed a novel pre-training task using self-supervised learning on omics features (tabular data). Subsequently, a meticulous fine-tuning phase was implemented using a robust training set comprised of high-precision driver variants30 and clinically relevant cancer variants from ClinVar31. Rigorous benchmarking against 14 state-of-the-art functional variant prediction methods on diverse test sets, including ClinVar and experimental assay-related cancer variants32, revealed ModVAR’s capability to accurately predict clinically relevant and experimentally validated driver variants. Additionally, ModVAR’s multimodal dynamic variant modeling approach enables its application to a broader context of variant tasks. ModVAR excels in the identification of cancer-targetable variants, and we verified the targets through molecular docking. Notably, ModVAR also effectively models driver variants in intrinsically disordered protein regions, which further broadens the scope. Leveraging the advanced understanding of disordered protein regions provided by protein structure prediction modules, ModVAR offers valuable insights into the functional roles of these regions in disease mechanisms. Furthermore, the highly flexible architecture makes ModVAR support predictions for indel variants, enhancing its potential for diverse applications. To delve deeper into ModVAR’s capabilities, we conducted a comprehensive multimodal interpretation analysis, revealing its ability to effectively integrate diverse information across modalities, enabling more accurate and nuanced predictions in a variety of scenarios. Finally, we applied ModVAR to score all variants (3,971,946 variants) within the COSMIC CMC project, making these valuable predictions publicly available as a valuable resource for the scientific community.


## Methods

### Data preprocessing
  In this study, the dataset serves three primary purposes: pre-training, fine-tuning for driver variant prediction, and benchmarking (Fig. S1). For pre-training the cancer omics modality, we utilize large-scale unlabeled cancer variant data from the COSMIC Cancer Mutation Census (CMC)5 project. We experimented with different training set sizes (100,000/50,000/10,000/5000), and ultimately chose a training set of 50,000 samples for pre-training.

  To address the oncogenic driver variant identification problem, we constructed a fine-tuned training set using high-precision validated and clinical cancer variant data labeled as pathogenic/benign. The high-precision validated variant data were sourced from the OncoVar database30, which provides a generalized list of pan-cancer driver variants. We filtered this data based on the “COSMIC70” annotation term, retaining variants with a recurrence number or recurrence site number greater than 2. This resulted in 2477 driver variants, which were used as positive samples (Driver). An equal number of negative samples (Passengers) were randomly selected from the same database, resulting in a total of 4954 high-precision driver prediction samples. For the clinical dataset, we selected cancer-related variants from the ClinVar database31 for the period up to and including 2022. To ensure data quality, we removed samples lacking assertion criteria or exhibiting conflicting interpretations based on the “ReviewStatus” annotation term. Additionally, samples with more than three submissions (Tier3) were filtered out based on the “NumberSubmitters” annotation item. Finally, we classified samples with a “ClinicalSignificance” value of Pathogenic or Benign as positive and negative samples, respectively, resulting in 1631 positive and 2472 negative samples, totaling 4103 clinical data points. The final model was fine-tuned using a combined dataset of 9057 samples from the high-precision validation and clinical datasets. This approach enabled the model to learn highly validated and clinically relevant information about driver variants.

  To assess model performance, we constructed multiple benchmark datasets, encompassing both clinical and experimental assay data. For the clinical datasets, we selected cancer variants added to the ClinVar database in 2023, ensuring independence from and no overlap with the training data. To minimize statistical bias, we conducted three random samplings. We applied the same cleaning procedure as for the training set, classifying variants with “ClinicalSignificance” values of Pathogenic and Likely Pathogenic as positive, while Benign and Likely Benign as negative. Finally, we divided the benchmark set into tiers (Tier1/2/3) based on the “NumberSubmitters” annotation (greater than 1, 2, and 3, respectively). For experimental assay benchmarks, we included a tumor-related experimental calibration dataset32 containing: Protein 3D structure clustering dataset32, which comprises 807 positive samples (Driver) and 1416 negative samples (Passengers), totaling 2223 entries; In-vitro cell viability assay dataset33, which consists of 244 positive samples and 544 negative samples, totaling 788 entries; In-vivo tumor generation dataset34, which contains 42 positive samples and 25 negative samples, totaling 67 entries. These benchmark datasets were established to validate the reliability of the model on high-precision, tumor-related calibration datasets.

  Ethical approval was not sought or required for this study, as all datasets employed were publicly available.


### Multimodal feature preprocessing
  ModVAR integrates features from three primary modalities: DNA sequence, protein 3D structure, and cancer omics data. In addition to these core data sources, four conservation scores are also included to enhance model prediction stability and robustness. These scores, obtained from the dbNSFP4.5a database35,36, are SIFT4G37, Polyphen238, LRT39 and VEST440.

  The DNA sequence modality encompasses a 250 bp window of contextual information flanking the variant site. This approach captures both the variant itself and the surrounding sequence context. The resulting sequences—a pre-variant reference (Ref) and a post-variant altered (Alt) sequence—are used as input for the model. This strategy effectively handles various variant types, including indels.

  The protein 3D structure modality focuses on the amino acid change caused by the variant. A window of 50 amino acids upstream and downstream of the affected amino acid is extracted, resulting in two sequences: a wild-type sequence (before variant) and an altered sequence (after variant). In the case of silent variants, where the DNA change doesn’t alter the amino acid sequence, the full 100 amino acid sequence surrounding the variant is directly used as input.

  The cancer-omics modality integrates data from three sources: gene annotation, epigenetic data, and mutation data from the TCGA cohort (Fig. S12). For gene annotation, we extracted 46 columns of relevant features, including DNA methylation, copy number variation data, and somatic mutation data across 33 types of cancer from the TCGA Pan-cancer Atlas (Table S6). Epigenetic features are gathered from three variant-related databases: ENCODE, Roadmap, and FANTOM5 (Table S7). After principal component analysis, 96 dimensions are extracted as features representing the principal components of the epigenetic features. Cancer-specific statistical annotations, extracted from the variant database in the TCGA PanCanAtlas6 project, include 83 columns of features related to the number and frequency of cancer-specific variants within a 100 bp window surrounding the variant. This information is aggregated at the variant level (Supplementary Data 1). These three data sources are combined, resulting in a total of 225 dimensions of cancer omics features.


### ModVAR
  ModVAR employs a multimodal pre-training and fine-tuning framework. During the pre-training phase, three main modalities are used to encode data: DNA sequence, protein 3D structure, and cancer-omics tabular data.

  The DNA sequence modality leverages DNABERT-228 as a pre-training model to extract reference (Ref) and alternate (Alt) sequences, generating a 768-dimensional encoded vector. The protein 3D structure modality utilizes the ESMFold model29 for pre-training, taking the mean value of the protein embeddings to produce a 1280-dimensional encoding vector. For the cancer-omics modality, we developed an unsupervised pre-training module specifically for cancer omics features, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. This module, named STPM, employs a contrastive learning approach to extract information from unlabeled data. It uses a non-linear structure combining fully-connected layers and ReLU activation to project continuous data into a d-dimensional hidden layer, enabling embedding. The cancer-omics features are then fed into a self-attention and intersample attention transformer model. This model facilitates attentional interactions between rows and columns of the omics data through tensor dimensional transformation. ### Benchmark methods setting
  To evaluate the performance of ModVAR and other variant pathogenicity prediction tools in identifying cancer driver variants, this study conducted comparative experiments against 14 classical or state-of-the-art prediction methods. These included four cancer-specific methods: CScape47, CScape Somatic27, CanDrA25 and FATHMM-XF26,ten comprehensive variant identification methods: CADD48, PrimateAI49, MutationAssessor50, gMVP51, VARITY52, REVEL13, M-CAP12, ESM-1b22, EVE16, and AlphaMissense21.

  Scores for CScape and CScape Somatic were obtained from their official websites (http://www.cscape.biocompute.org.uk/, http://cscape-somatic.biocompute.org.uk/), encompassing all coding regions. For FATHMM-XF, the scores were obtained from Open CRAVAT. For CanDrA, the scores were downloaded from CanDrA (mdanderson.org), named “Cancer-in-General”. Scores for the remaining non-cancer specific tools were all retrieved from the dbnsfp4.7a35 database, and the normalized “rankscore” was used for each algorithm in the experiment. Specifically, for the VARITY method, the VARITY-R scores were considered in this comparative assessment.


### Performance evaluation
  This study utilized several high-precision clinical and experimental assay benchmarks to evaluate model performance across various tasks. Key metrics include the receiver operating characteristic (ROC), precision-recall curve (PRC), and F1 score53–55. The area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) is a standard metric for binary classifiers, with the ROC curve plotting the False Positive Rate (FPR) on the x-axis against the true positive rate (TPR) on the y-axis. The PRC metric, which plots Precision on the x-axis against Recall on the y-axis, emphasizes performance on positive samples, particularly in datasets with imbalanced label distributions. The F1 score is a standard metric that provides a balance between precision and recall for binary classifiers. It is calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and represents the balance between the two metrics. In this study, all tools were evaluated using their raw scores. Therefore, when calculating the F1 score and discussing classification performance, the optimal classification threshold was determined using Youden’s Index. For ModVAR, we observed that the optimal thresholds across multiple datasets fall between 0.4 and 0.6. Therefore, we recommend classifying mutations with ModVAR scores <0.4 as passenger mutations, those with scores >0.6 as driver mutations, and mutations with scores between 0.4 and 0.6 as likely pathogenic mutations. Additionally, the Mann–Whitney U test, a non-parametric statistical hypothesis test, was employed to determine whether two independent samples originate from the same distribution. In this study, the first group comprised prediction scores for variants labeled as 1 (driver), while the second group included scores for variants labeled as 0 (passenger). The p-value from the Mann–Whitney U test indicates the statistical significance of classification performance across various tools in driver identification tasks.


### ModVAR predictions in a broader variants context
  To explore ModVAR’s predictive capability in a broader context, we constructed two test sets: a targetable variant dataset and a driver variant dataset in intrinsically disordered protein regions. For the targetable variant dataset, drug target mutations (positive samples) were obtained from the CIViC56 database. To ensure fairness, we selected cancer targetable variants added in the 2024 update of CIViC, resulting in 49 positive samples. Non-drug target mutations (negative samples) were randomly selected at a 1:1 ratio from the COSMIC CMC project, with 10 rounds of sampling to account for statistical variation. For the intrinsically disordered protein regions test set, driver/passenger variants (positive/negative samples) were sourced from the DisProt57 database. We selected disordered protein fragments with lengths >200 from DisProt and retrieved corresponding SNP mutations using the UniProt58 database based on their ACC numbers. Each SNP mutation was then cross-referenced with the ClinVar database to assign a pathogenicity label. Mutations were classified as positive if at least one “Pathogenic” or “Likely Pathogenic” annotation was present; if multiple conflicting annotations were found, the mutation was excluded. The final dataset consisted of 293 positive and negative samples.

  For both datasets, we performed comparative experiments using the same VEP tool and evaluation metrics (see “Benchmark Methods Setting and Performance Evaluation”).


### Indels prediction with ModVAR
  ModVAR is capable of predicting the pathogenicity of Indel variants. For this study, we screened cancer-related non-SNP variants from the ClinVar database, up to and including year 2023. These variants were cleaned using the same process outlined in the “Dataset Preprocessing” subsection, ultimately yielding a test set of 16,311 samples for Indel prediction.

  Since most existing VEP tools lack support for Indel variant pathogenicity prediction, the four selected conservation scores could not be directly obtained from ModVAR’s input features. Therefore, we queried the conservation scores of the nearest cancer-associated SNPs in the COSMIC CMC database as a substitute and incorporated them into the model. CADD was chosen as the comparison algorithm.


### Statistics and reproducibility
  We deployed the ModVAR model on a Linux x86 server with an AMD EPYC 7R13 Processor, 15GB RAM, and an NVIDIA L4 GPU with 24GB VRAM. On a dataset of 326 samples, we measured an average inference time of 1.17 s per mutation. Among the processing steps, feature extraction for DNA and protein structural features was the most time-consuming, with average speeds of 0.92 s per mutation and 0.24 s per mutation, respectively.


## Result

### Overview of the ModVAR framework
  This study introduces ModVAR, a driver variant prediction model employing a pre-training and multimodal fine-tuning architecture. The model leverages three primary modalities: DNA, protein tertiary structure, and cancer omics. These are distinctly colored in Fig. 1a for clear representation. For DNA input, ModVAR queries the UCSC genome database59 using the hg19 standard to retrieve the DNA sequences of the variant site and its context. For protein input, the annovar60 tool is then used to convert variant annotations to corresponding amino acid sequences. For cancer omics feature input, gene-level annotations are calculated statistically using the TCGA database, cancer-specific features are derived from PanCanAtlas6 project, and epigenomic features are extracted from ENCODE61, Roadmap62, and FANTOM563 annotation databases (detailed in “Multimodal Feature Preprocessing” section). All annotated features are then combined to form the cancer omics Modality. During the pre-training stage, unsupervised upstream tasks are defined for each modality. DNA-BERT228 is employed as the pre-training model for the DNA sequence modality, while ESMFold29 serves as the pre-training model for the protein tertiary structure modality. For the cancer omics modality, this study leverages the tabular data pre-training algorithm, which utilizes cancer variant data from the COSMIC CMC project to pre-train models corresponding to the cancer-omics modality (detailed in Methods section).

  Next, ModVAR integrates the representations from different pre-trained modalities using a multimodal fine-tuning architecture. The parameters of all unsupervised pre-trained models are frozen, and their intermediate hidden layer outputs serve as extracted features from upstream modalities. These features are then fed into the multimodal fine-tuning model to further extract features relevant to the downstream driver prediction task from each modality. Additionally, ModVAR incorporates four conservation scores (SIFT4G37, Polyphen238, LRT39, and VEST440) to enhance model robustness. To enhance the explanatory power of the model, ModVAR used the contrast attention mechanism to reinforce the variability between the reference (Ref) and altered (Alt) values of the DNA sequence as well as the protein structure. Finally, the model extracts a feature vector for each modality and utilizes the multimodal low-rank tensor fusion module (LMF) to perform efficient feature fusion. The resulting fused composite feature vector is then input into the MLP for projection into the categorical feature space, ultimately outputting a predicted pathogenicity score ranging from 0 to 1.


### ModVAR is effective in predicting clinical cancer variants
  To rigorously assess the predictive power of ModVAR in clinical cancer variant identification, we constructed a benchmark test set comprising recent cancer-related variants sourced from the ClinVar database, specifically those added in 2023. This selection ensured the independence of the test data, and we randomly sampled the data three times, thereby minimizing potential biases and enhancing the robustness of our evaluation. Furthermore, the test set was stratified into three tiers (Tier1, Tier2, Tier3) based on the strength of clinical evidence, enabling a nuanced evaluation of model performance across varying levels of accuracy. We compared ModVAR against fourteen leading variant prediction tools, including four cancer-specific methods CScape47, FATHMM-XF26, CScape Somatic27, CanDrA25, as well as more general variant predictors. This comprehensive comparison allowed us to thoroughly evaluate ModVAR’s performance relative to the current state-of-the-art approaches.

  Our analysis focused on the Tier1 dataset. ModVAR demonstrated leading performance, achieving an AUROC of 0.985 ± 0.003 and a F1 of 0.941 ± 0.004(Fig. 2a). Notably, while other top-performing algorithms such as CADD showed strong results (AUROC: 0.982 ± 0.009, F1: 0.929 ± 0.009), ModVAR still showed competitive performance. Moreover, in the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test (Fig. 2b), which measures the statistical significance of the prediction differences, ModVAR achieved a p-value of 3.08E-231, outperforming CADD (1.09E-219). In clinical applications, low error rates (False Positives/Negatives Rates) are crucial for evaluating tools. Therefore, we conducted a comparison of error rates on ClinVar. Figure 2d shows the FPR/FNR curves of ModVAR and CADD at different thresholds. It is evident that ModVAR generally exhibits lower FPR/FNR, with FPR lower than CADD at all thresholds. We further calculated the optimal classification thresholds for both tools using Youden’s Index, which were 0.48 for ModVAR and 0.7 for CADD. At these optimal thresholds, ModVAR’s FPR (5.36%) and FNR (6.15%) are both lower than CADD’s FPR (7.17%) and FNR (6.82%). Further validation was conducted through head-to-head comparisons of ModVAR against each benchmark tool on a subset of their actual predicted samples (Fig. 2c). Remarkably, ModVAR maintained its superior performance across all comparisons, delivering higher AUROC scores and more significant p-values, underscoring its robustness in clinical variant prediction. Extending this analysis to the Tier2 and Tier3 datasets, ModVAR continued to outperform its peers, achieving AUROC scores of 0.988 ± 0.001 and 0.977 ± 0.002, F1 scores of 0.954 ± 0.002 and 0.937 ± 0.005, and p-values of 1.17E-94 and 1.76E-50, respectively (Figs. S3–4; Table S1). To understand the contributions of different data modalities, we performed ablation analysis (Fig. 3e). ### Comprehensive variant interpretation through multimodal data integration
  At the heart of ModVAR’s architecture lies a sophisticated integration of three distinct modalities: DNA sequences, protein structural features, and cancer omics data, which includes a wealth of multi-omics and annotated features. Our in-depth interpretation of variant analyses reveals that each modality contributes a unique and complementary perspective, drawing from its specific entry point to enrich the overall representation. This harmonious interplay of multimodal data fosters a deeper and more nuanced understanding of variant pathogenicity, empowering ModVAR to deliver predictions with both high accuracy and valuable insight.

  We conducted the multimodal interpretability analysis by using the SHAP method45 (as shown in Fig. 1a). By computing SHAP values for different modality features, we were able to quantify the contribution of each modality to the model’s predictions. The first row in Fig. 7a shows the average contribution of each modality across the full dataset. This analysis reveals that protein structural features play a dominant role (~76%), followed by cancer omics features (~23%). DNA sequence features contribute minimally (~1%), indicating that protein structural data and cancer omics data are crucial drivers in variant prediction. To further explore modality contributions, we analyzed specific variant cases (subsequent bars in Fig. 7a). Two distinct point variants (C-A and C-T) occurring at the same locus (chr1:17350510) resulted in two significantly different amino acid sequence changes: stopgain and nonsynonymous. For the stopgain variant (C-T), ModVAR identified a substantial amino acid change, leading to a higher protein contribution (86%) compared to the average. Conversely, the DNA and cancer annotation features contributed relatively less (14%). In contrast, for the nonsynonymous variant (C-A), the model detected differences in the DNA and annotation features, resulting in a significant reduction in protein structural information contribution (55%) and a substantial increase in the contribution of cancer omics (40%). The silent variant rs587776551 (chr11:108151895G-A) stands out as one of the few instances in the dataset where the protein structure remains unchanged. In this specific case, the model significantly increased the contribution from the DNA sequence modality (58%) and decreased the contribution from both protein structure and cancer annotation features (42%). This suggests that for the cancer driver variants prediction task, the DNA sequence might provide less additional information compared to protein structural features and cancer omics features. This finding is consistent with the observation that AlphaMissense, which relies solely on structural features, achieves excellent performance on most datasets. However, in special cases (e.g., silent variants), where protein structural features don’t provide effective information, integrating multimodal data, considering DNA changes, plays a crucial role in the prediction. The protein feature for the stopgain variant was less pronounced (dull in color), and this difference was more evident in the score differences between the two features. The protein structural modality, on the other hand, provided high-dimensional features reflecting the significant differences in protein 3D structure caused by the two variants. Ultimately, through modality fusion, ModVAR organically combined information from each modality to generate a more comprehensive final prediction score. The right panel of Fig. 7b depicts the 3D structure changes predicted by AlphaFold3 for the protein. This prediction also shows that the stopgain variant introduces significant structural differences, consistent with the features extracted by ModVAR.

The final model assigned a pathogenicity score of 0.841 to this variant, consistent with its pathogenic label. This demonstrates ModVAR’s ability to accurately predict the pathogenicity of variants, even when protein structural information is limited, by leveraging the information provided by other modalities.

## Discussion
  In this study, we introduce ModVAR, a pioneering model for predicting somatic cancer driver variants through a sophisticated multimodal pre-train and fine-tuning architecture. ModVAR stands as the first framework to synergistically integrate protein structural features, DNA sequence features, and cancer omics features, marking a significant advancement in the field of cancer genomics. By leveraging pre-trained models, such as DNA-BERT2 and ESMFold, ModVAR effectively extracts modality-specific features from DNA and protein sequences, while a self-supervised pre-training model based on comparative learning is employed for cancer omics data. These extracted features are subsequently processed through ModVAR’s multimodal fine-tuning module, utilizing contrast attention and feature fusion techniques to generate final pathogenicity scores. Our model has demonstrated superior performance across various benchmark datasets and exhibits the flexibility to predict driver indels by accommodating extended DNA and protein sequences. Our interpretability analysis underscores the critical role of protein structural modalities in variant pathogenicity prediction, and specific sample analyses illustrate the model’s benefit from complementary representations across modalities. Furthermore, by applying ModVAR to score variants in the COSMIC CMC project, we observe a higher proportion of high pathogenicity scores in variants located on driver gene segments, reinforcing ModVAR’s efficacy and potential for broader applications in complex disease prediction.

  In the broader context of variant prediction tasks, we found that ModVAR has extensive potential applications. In the cancer targetable variants prediction task, molecular docking analysis revealed that ModVAR successfully identified highly targetable variants which were missed by most other methods. This underscores the advantage of ModVAR’s multimodal architecture—combining DNA, protein structure, and cancer omics data—providing more comprehensive and complementary features, which leads to superior performance compared to single-modal tools. Additionally, in the protein disordered region driver prediction task, ModVAR demonstrated high capability in identifying driver variants, owing to its precise modeling of the protein structures module, effectively tackling this challenging task.

  The emergence of AlphaFold has sparked great interest among researchers regarding the insights provided by protein structural features. Our SHAP interpretable analysis reveals that the protein modality dominates the prediction task for cancer drivers, accounting for 76% of the three modalities. This finding aligns with the successful application of unimodal variant prediction models such as AlphaMissense and EVE. ModVAR specifically employs ESMFold as the protein-level pre-training model and utilizes the contrastive attention module during fine-tuning to focus on the difference in protein structure before and after the variant. By leveraging modern structure prediction methods for atom-level protein structure prediction, ModVAR has the potential to unravel the mechanisms of cancer development at a deeper level of individual point mutations, rather than gene level as previously possible.

  In our analysis, we conducted an ablation study to analyze the contribution of each modality within ModVAR. We found that while using only protein structural features yielded good results on most datasets, the performance was generally slightly lower than that of AlphaMissense, especially on specific datasets such as the protein 3D structure clustering dataset. However, when we incorporated multimodal data, ModVAR consistently outperformed AlphaMissense across most datasets. Furthermore, we conducted an ablation study on the gene features within the cancer-omics modality (Table S5) and found that gene features consistently improved the performance of ModVAR. The most significant improvements were observed in applications such as cancer druggable variants, which corresponds to the reduced contribution of protein modality in the SHAP analysis of these subsets. These analyses demonstrate the advantage of integrating complementary features from different modalities, which enhances the model’s ability to predict variant drivers more effectively. Additionally, for variant types that do not lead to amino acid changes, such as silent variants, a unimodal prediction tool like AlphaMissense—which focuses solely on protein features—would struggle to predict their potential pathogenicity. In contrast, ModVAR’s multimodal nature allows it to provide more comprehensive prediction results by leveraging supplementary information from other modalities. This makes ModVAR capable of delivering accurate predictions even when a single modality lacks sufficient information.

  The multimodal architecture of ModVAR empowers it to synthesize comprehensive contextual information about variants, enhancing the depth and accuracy of its predictions. Through detailed visualizations and analyses of ModVAR’s predictions for specific variant samples, we found that the cancer omics annotation modality often supplies a stable contextual background regarding variant locations, thereby offering crucial complementary information. However, when different variants occur at the same locus (e.g., rs397516836 C-T/A), reliance solely on cancer omics proves insufficient for distinguishing between these variants. For driver silent variants that do not alter amino acid sequences (e.g., rs587776551 G-A), our studies revealed a significant reduction in the contribution from the protein modality. In these cases, the DNA sequence modality partially compensates for the required variant characterization information. By integrating this with cancer omics annotations, ModVAR can ultimately deliver accurate prediction results from a multimodal perspective, highlighting the necessity and effectiveness of a holistic approach in variant pathogenicity prediction.

  Despite the advantages of incorporating complementary multimodal information, ModVAR has its limitations. One aspect worth considering is a more refined processing of the features for each modality. For example, in the cancer-omics modality, the current strategy for cancer-specific statistical annotations is to use a 100 bp window around the mutation site to measure nearby mutations. However, this approach does not take into account the varying influence of mutations based on their distance from the central site. We have already tried designing features that incorporate distance weighting, but the results only showed a slight performance difference. Therefore, in the future, ModVAR’s feature extraction module can be optimized to achieve more precise modeling results. Another major constraint stems from our choice to use pre-trained models—DNA-BERT2 for the representation of DNA sequences and ESMFold for the representation of protein structures—where the computational costs and deployment considerations led us to freeze their parameters during fine-tuning. This decision, while practical, may limit the model’s accuracy. In recent years, large language models (LLMs) that process text, like the transition from GPT-4 to GPT-4V73, have shown significant advancements towards multimodal capabilities. Incorporating novel pre-training and fine-tuning algorithms from these LLMs could potentially enhance ModVAR’s performance. Additionaly, there is limited exploration of non-coding region drivers, which do not cause structural changes in proteins but may influence gene expression through regulatory mechanisms74. Accurately modeling the associated protein structures of these non-coding variants could further aid in predicting their functional impacts. Given the importance of protein structural features verified in our study, future study could include structural features of proteins regulated by non-coding variants to see if these features also play a significant role in variant prediction tasks for non-coding regions. ModVAR’s multimodal pre-training and fine-tuning architecture offers good scalability. Future work could consider adding new modalities to the existing model. For instance, using an LLM to provide additional text modality information and integrating specific variant-related knowledge into the model. Integrating LLMs or other models with stronger interpretability can help researchers delve deeper into how changes in sequences, protein structures, and cancer omics annotation elements specifically affect the functionality of mutations, thus providing crucial insights into cancer mechanisms and development in the future.
