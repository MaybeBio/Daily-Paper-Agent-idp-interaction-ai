# Using Multiple Isotope-Labeled Infrared Spectra for the Structural Characterization of an Intrinsically Disordered Peptide


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) rapidly interconvert between conformers, requiring an ensemble description. This complicates their experimental characterization, and force field limitations pose challenges for their simulation. Here, we use isotope-labeled and unlabeled infrared (IR) spectra to reweight simulated ensembles of the elastin-like peptide GVGVPGVG, a paradigmatic disordered peptide. By comparing the results obtained with different spectra, we explicitly show that the weights are underdetermined by the ensemble averaged data. We identify which labels and frequency regions maximize structural information while minimizing sensitivity to simulation error and show that these regions report on whether the peptide makes specific interactions. Our work shows the importance of incorporating simulations and simulated spectra at the planning stages of isotope-labeled IR experiments and more generally provides a framework for interpreting IR data for IDPs.


## Introduction
  Many proteins that play critical roles in signaling, regulation, and molecular recognition—with important roles in both health and disease—function without adopting well-defined three-dimensional structures1–6. Such intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) challenge traditional structure-function paradigms7,8. Experimental and computational studies show that IDPs interconvert between conformers on picosecond to millisecond timescales9. Understanding their function therefore requires an ensemble description, which can take the form of either a set of conformational states and their populations or distributions over collective variables such as the end-to-end distance or the radius of gyration6,8,10.

  Several experimental methods have been used to characterize IDPs structurally6,9–13. The most widely used technique is nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), which provides site-specific structural information through chemical shifts, residual dipolar couplings (RDCs), J-couplings, and nuclear Overhauser effects (NOEs)14,15. Other popular techniques include small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS), which probes the overall size and shape of the protein16, and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, which can be used to estimate disorder and secondary structure content10. Single-molecule Förster resonant energy transfer (smFRET) has been used as well and avoids ensemble averaging to yield a distribution of distances between the donor and acceptor probe17.

  Here, we focus on amide I infrared (IR) spectroscopy. The amide I band at 1600–1700 cm−1, which is primarily composed of the C=O stretch, is highly sensitive to secondary structure and local hydrogen bonding18. Its key advantage is its fast inherent measurement timescale—on the order of a few picoseconds-allowing it to probe conformers before they interconvert19. Amide I spectroscopy has been widely used to study peptide structure and dynamics, including folding and conformational changes20, aggregation21, and amyloid formation22. A recent surge of interest in this method has been driven by spectroscopic mapping techniques23–26, which enable structure-based modeling. These models enable qualitative connections between molecular structures and vibrational spectra, readily extending their application to IDPs.

  Apart from single-molecule studies, an experimental signal represents a population-weighted average of the observable from all conformers sampled during the measurement5,10,11,27. This makes it difficult to resolve the underlying structural heterogeneity of an IDP from experimental data alone. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations offer a potential solution by providing full atomistic detail but come with their own limitations. In addition to the inherent challenge of sampling, results for IDPs can be sensitive to the choice of protein force field and water model28–30. Force fields are usually optimized for stably folded proteins and tend to overestimate the amount of secondary structure and compaction of unfolded states31–33. Efforts have been made to improve existing force fields or create IDP-specific force fields, but their accuracies appear to vary with sequence and observable32,34–37.

  An approach that integrates experiments with simulations can in principle yield high accuracy and microscopic detail. Such an approach involves performing experimental measurements, obtaining a basis of conformers through simulations, calculating the experimental observables from the basis through a forward model, and reweighting the conformers to match experimental observables27,33. Previous work in our group and others demonstrated the power of this approach for interpreting IR spectra of IDPs. Sethi et al.38 used a Bayesian approach to estimate the populations of conformers of α-synuclein. Baiz et al.39 used Markov state models (MSMs) to derive a basis of conformers to interpret spectroscopic data of the ribosomal protein NTL9. Reppert et al.19 used a maximum entropy (ME) approach to refine the distribution of a β-turn distance of four elastin-like peptides, a model system for the study of IDPs40,41. By constraining the mean and variance of a single isotope-labeled peak, they corrected for force field biases in the relative populations of extended versus collapsed states. Feng et al.42 advanced the methodology by adapting a more flexible Bayesian approach43 which allowed them to incorporate multiple constraints from FTIR and 2D IR spectra. They demonstrated their approach by correcting for the biases in the populations of the three conformers of the trialanine (AAA) peptide. Thomassen et al.44 used a similar approach and calculated 2D IR spectra of structural clusters of R5 silaffin peptide and fit them to experimental spectra to characterize the secondary structure in neat and silica-bound forms.

  Combining experiment and simulation as described above faces two major challenges. The first is that the conformer populations are underdetermined by the experimental observables11,45,46. The second challenge is overfitting, which can occur due to errors in the forward model and/or noise in the experimental data47,48. To address these issues, it is recommended to combine data from several experiments13,27,49. In the context of IR spectroscopy, data from different isotope labels is a natural choice. Since the introduction of a label redshifts its frequency by increasing the reduced mass of its oscillator, it becomes isolated from the main band, providing site-specific information50,51. This site specificity allows multiple labels to provide complementary views of the structural ensemble, enabling the study of discrete conformational changes52, folding kinetics53, and conformational heterogeneity39,54.

  Using amide I IR spectroscopy on multiple site-specifically labeled samples provides an opportunity to refine a global conformational ensemble with the use of numerous constraints on local conformational preferences. In this study, we exploit a dataset of eight isotope-labeled and one unlabeled FTIR spectra of the elastin-like peptide GVGVPGVG to investigate how multiple site-specific constraints enhance the structural characterization of an IDP. We analyze data from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with dimensionality reduction techniques to identify four kinetically resolvable states that are characterized by the presence or absence of a backbone bend near the N-terminus and a β-turn on the C-terminus. We further resolve substructure within the four states to identify twelve conformers that the peptide populates. We find marked discrepancies in the populations of the states and conformers in simulations with the CHARMM2755,56 (CHARMM22/CMAP) and CHARMM36m35 force fields: CHARMM27 predicts a higher proportion of compact states than CHARMM36m.

  By simulating the spectra of the four states for each of the experimental labels, we identify the most informative isotope labels and frequency regions which maximize structural resolution while minimizing sensitivity to errors in spectral simulation. We demonstrate that single labels at strategic positions offer more robust constraints than multiple labels, owing to coupling errors. We also show that the prior distributions from MD, specifically the distribution of configurations within each of the four states, has a significant effect on the results. Performing the analysis with the twelve-state model yields greater insight into conformational preferences but does not qualitatively change the interpretation of the results.

  The data reveal that the ELP is primarily in an extended conformation, validating the improvements made to CHARMM36m parameters32,35 for IDP sampling. Beyond the specific insights into this IDP system, this approach establishes a general framework for the analysis of isotope-labeled data and the design of isotope-labeled IR experiments so as to maximize structural information while minimizing computational bias and experimental cost. This work presents a step toward the global characterization of the structural ensemble of an IDP with isotope-labeled IR spectroscopy.


## Methods

### FTIR Spectra.
  We analyze eight isotope-labeled and one unlabeled FTIR spectra of GVGVPGVG that were previously collected57. The labels were incorporated at the carbonyl group of the amide units of the peptide backbone. We name the residues in sequential order from the N- to the C-terminus (G1V2G3V4P5G6V7G8) and denote the labels by the preceding amino acid (Figure 1b). Using these conventions, there were one unlabeled peptide (UL), five singly labeled peptides (G1, G3, V4, G6, and V7), two dually labeled peptides (G3G6 and V4V7), and one triply labeled peptide (V4G6V7). We remark that this naming convention differs from that in ref. 57. All labels were 13C18O except for G3 in the triply labeled peptide, which was 13CO to distinguish it spectrally from the other labels in that construct. The FTIR spectra were collected at 10°C at pH = 1.0 (DCl in D2O; 150 mmol of 1:1 KD2PO4:K2DPO4). Thus, all amide groups and both termini are deuterated (ND3+, COOD).


### Molecular Dynamics Simulations.
  Molecular dynamics simulations were prepared with GROMACS tools58 and performed with the OpenMM 8.059 simulation package. The peptide in the fully extended conformation was put in a cubic box of water molecules and overlaps were deleted; the side length of the box was chosen to be 2 nm longer than the length of the fully extended conformation. Both termini were protonated, and one Cl− ion was added to neutralize the charge. To compare the performance of various force fields, we used the CHARMM2755,56 with the original TIP3P model60 and CHARMM36m35 force fields with CHARMM-modified TIP3P, resulting in a total of 3951 atoms for the CHARMM27 and 3972 atoms for the CHARMM36m simulations, respectively. Exploratory simulations were performed with four other force fields (AMBER99SB-ILDN61/CHARMM36IDPSFF62/OPLS-AA63/CHARMM3664) but are not discussed further as their results were quite similar to those obtained for CHARMM27 (first three) or CHARMM36m (last).

  The equilibration procedure was the same for both force fields. First, we performed an energy minimization with a tolerance of 10 kJ/mol. Then, we applied a force constant of 100.0 kJ mol−1 nm to all backbone atoms to allow the solvent and ion to equilibrate. We then performed a 100 ps NVT simulation to equilibrate the system at 283.15 K followed by a 1 ps NPT simulation. The position restraints were removed, and the NPT simulation was continued for 1 ns. The final box size was taken to be the box volume at the end of the NPT simulation, with box lengths of 4.92 and 4.87 nm corresponding to densities of 1.031 and 0.999 g/mL, for the CHARMM27 and CHARMM36m simulations, respectively. The MonteCarloBarostat method in OpenMM was used for the NPT simulations. The system was then allowed to equilibrate further in the NVT ensemble for 5 ns. After equilibration, we performed a 500 ns unbiased production run, with coordinates saved every 20 fs for input to the spectral simulations.

  All simulations were performed with Langevin dynamics65 with a friction coefficient of 1 ps−1, a timestep of 2 fs, and a temperature of 283.15 K to match experimental conditions. Long-range electrostatics were treated with the Particle-Mesh-Ewald (PME) method66 with a nonbonded cutoff of 1 nm. All bonds involving hydrogen were constrained using the SHAKE67 algorithm.


### Spectral Simulation.
  FTIR spectra were simulated using a mixed quantum-classical coupled oscillator model in which a vibrational spectroscopic map was used to translate atomic coordinates and charges from an MD simulation into a time-dependent amide I Hamiltonian and transition dipole moment trajectory5168. This model treats the amide I vibration quantum mechanically while treating all other (bath) coordinates classically. These were used to calculate a dipole-dipole time correlation function, which was then Fourier transformed to obtain the IR absorption spectrum. The time-dependent Hamiltonian was constructed in the basis of local mode amide vibrations of the peptide backbone, resulting in a 7 × 7 Hamiltonian corresponding to the seven amide bonds. The diagonal elements of this Hamiltonian are the “site energies” (local mode vibrational frequency) of each amide I oscillator and the off-diagonal elements are the vibrational couplings between them.

  In this model, the frequency of each oscillator depends on its electrostatic interactions with the surroundings. We use a one-site field map which relates the frequency shift to the electric field on the amide O69: the isolated 12C16O amide I oscillator frequency70 is given by ω=ω0+2629.9cm-1⋅a0e/Eh, where the gas phase frequency ω0=1709.1cm-1 for all amino acids except proline (ω0=1677.6cm-1); see the Supplementary Information of ref. 70. Constant redshifts of 43 and 65 cm−1 are applied for 13C and 13C18O, respectively51,68. Electric fields were computed using CHARMM27/TIP3P charges for the trajectories from both force fields to enable direct comparison. Glycine is the only amino acid in this force field with a net charged side chain (+0.06e), and we transferred this charge to the Cα atom, so that all side chains are neutral; this change also produces more accurate frequencies. This map has a frequency prediction error42 of 2 cm−1. Transition dipole moments were calculated per ref. 25. For the couplings between oscillators, we used additional established maps derived from density functional theory calculations to calculate the through-bond coupling between bonded nearest neighbor amides and transition charge coupling for through-space interaction between non-bonded amides71.

  We used the program g_amide72 to generate the amide I Hamiltonian and dipole moment trajectories and the program g_spec73 to obtain the spectrum. Spectra were obtained through numerical wavefunction propagation by calculating a response function from the dipole correlation functions74. The spectrum of each 100-ps segment was obtained by calculating the correlation function propagated for 10 ps starting from each frame and averaging those realizations, equivalent to a frequency resolution of 3.3 cm−1. Since our calculations do not include the COOD vibration observed in experimental spectra at ~1725 cm−1, we removed it from the experimental spectra for comparison purposes through a Gaussian fit as detailed in Figure S1 in the Supplemental Information (SI).


### Bayesian refinement of the conformational ensemble.
  To determine the populations of conformers based on the experimental data, we divided the 500 ns MD trajectory for each force field into 5000 non-overlapping, sequential 100-ps segments and calculated the isotope-labeled spectra of each segment as described above. Given a trajectory segment, x, and label, l, the computed segment spectrum is denoted Ix,lsim(ω). We then calculate the normalized overlap integral75 sx,l between each isotope-labeled segment spectrum and its corresponding experimental spectrum Ilexp(ω):  (1) sx,l=∫Ix,lsim(ω)Ilexp(ω)dω∫Ix,lsim(ω)2dω∫Ix,lexp(ω)2dω

  This overlap integral quantifies the similarity between the simulated and experimental spectra, with values ranging from 0 (no agreement) to 1 (perfect similarity).

  To reweight the simulation to match experiment, we reweight each stretch based on sx,l using:  (2) Lx(θ)=θ∑l∈labels1-sx,l   (3) wopt,x∝w0,xexp-Lx(θ)

  Here, w0,x=1/5000 is a uniform prior, and θ is a confidence parameter which reflects the confidence in the experiment. When θ=0, we maintain infinite confidence in the simulation (unperturbed prior). As θ grows larger, more weight is placed on the experiment. We note that the definition of θ is different than that of Feng et al. Each stretch is assigned a vector of populations px=px,Epx,Npx,Cpx,NC, where px,i denotes the fraction of time spent in state i and ∑ipx,i=1. We note that most stretches correspond to a single state, as shown in Figure S7. With wopt,x determined from equation (3), we calculate the refined populations by:  (4) piopt=∑x=15000wopt,xpi,x

  As described in the Results and Discussion, we consider populations estimated from different sets of spectra. For example, for ensemble refinement using each isotope label independently, we restrict the sum in equation (2) to a single term. Remarks on the selection of the hyperparameter θ with the effective sample size76 are in Figure S9.


## Results and Discussion
  The experimental IR spectra in the amide I region for nine isotopologues of the ELP GVGVPGVG acquired by Lessing57 are reproduced in Figure 1, along with the position of the labelled amide carbonyl groups for different isotope-labeled samples. The broad, asymmetric peak from 1630 to 1680 cm−1 corresponds to the main band, which arises from the delocalized amide I’ vibration. The shoulder on the red edge of the main band at around 1614 cm−1 corresponds to the proline vibration, which is inherently red shifted by around 27 cm−1 as it is a tertiary amide. Isotope labeling effectively isolates the vibrational signal of an individual amide oscillator by increasing its reduced mass, redshifting it away from the main band. The peaks from 1540 to 1600 cm−1 arise from these shifts, with the 13C18O and 13CO corresponding to shifts of about 60 and 41 cm−1, respectively. The V4 label produces the largest redshift since it corresponds to the proline vibration.

  Participation of a carbonyl group in a hydrogen bond redshifts the amide I frequency as well. In the spectroscopic map, this shift arises from the change in the electric field at the carbonyl group. The VPGV motif codes for a type II β-turn77, so it is expected that the V4/V7 labels probe hydrogen bonding across this turn, with G6 being an indirect reporter. Similarly, the G1/G3 N-terminal labels were selected to probe the N-terminal dynamics, although in the case of G1 the labeled oscillator is not distinguishable from the main band.


### Spectral ensembles
  As described in Molecular Dynamics Simulations, we performed separate 500 ns MD simulations with the CHARMM27 and CHARMM36m force fields and divided each resulting trajectory into 5000 non-overlapping, sequential 100-ps segments. As described in Spectral Simulation, we calculated the isotope-labeled spectrum of each segment. We denote the full set of segment spectra for a given label as its spectral ensemble, which reflects how conformational heterogeneity manifests spectrally. Figures 2 and S2 show the spectral ensembles. In comparing them with the experimental spectra, we observe that the 5000 segment spectra can exhibit multiple peaks which vary in their placement by tens of cm1, but that the ensemble-averaged spectra agree with the experiments well overall, despite consistent differences in the main band lineshape. In the case of the UL spectrum, we observe a featureless main band for frequencies >1630 cm−1 and a shoulder at 1610 cm−1 corresponding to the prolyl carbonyl. For the other spectra, the frequencies of peaks from 1540 to 1600 cm−1 are reproduced, though there are notable differences in their relative intensities.


### Dimensionality reduction reveals four kinetically resolvable states.
  To characterize and refine the conformational ensemble of the ELP, we sought to identify a basis set of representative states which capture the dominant conformers present. Since IDPs sample a wide range of structures, it is useful to group rapidly interconverting conformers into kinetically resolvable macrostates. This approach limits the number of populations to be inferred and reflects the assumption that the IR spectroscopy is sensitive to rearrangements of the peptide backbone and not fast, local fluctuations.

  To identify these states for our system, we employed the Integrated Variational Approach to Conformational Dynamics78 (IVAC). IVAC solves for the eigenvectors of a Markov transition matrix, which correspond to the slow modes of the system and can be used as the basis for clustering to identify kinetically resolvable states. Using a 5μs trajectory with CHARMM36m featurized by interatomic distances of amide C atoms at least two residues apart, we found that two implied time scales were well-separated from others (Figure S3). Visual inspection of the associated eigenvectors (slow modes) suggested that they track (1) the formation of a sharp backbone bend near the N-terminus caused by the rotation of the ψ angle of V3, and (2) the formation of a β-turn on the C-terminal end of the peptide (Figure S4). To facilitate further analysis, we examined histograms of interatomic distances between amide atoms and selected two representative distances which correlate strongly with projections on the first two eigenvectors (Figure S5). These are (1) the distance between the amide nitrogen of V2 and amide hydrogen of V4 (r24) and (2) the distance between the carbonyl oxygen of V4 and amide hydrogen of V7 (r47).

  Figure 3a shows a two-dimensional free energy surface as a function of these two distances. We observe a free-energy barrier at r24≈6Å that cleanly separates states which differ by the presence or absence of the backbone bend near the N-terminus. Similarly, a free energy barrier at r47≈5Å that separates states which differ by the presence or absence of the β-turn between V4 and V7. Based on the free energy surface, we define four states: an extended state (E), a state with a bend at the N-terminus (N), a state with a beta turn at the C-terminus (C), and a state exhibiting both features (NC). Representative structures for these states are illustrated in Figure 3b.

  Additionally, we note the presence of substructure within each of the four defined states. The first minimum in r24 and the second peak in r47 are split into two sub-minima, which correspond to the rotation of the G3 ϕ and G6 ψ angles, respectively. This results in a more fine-grained 12-state model, as shown in Figures S7–S8. For clarity, we focus on the four-state model in the main text.

  Although the four-state decomposition effectively characterizes the ensembles obtained with both force fields, we observe a significant discrepancy in the state populations (Fig. 4). Notably, the NC state is the most populated in the CHARMM27 trajectory but nearly absent from the CHARMM36m trajectory; by contrast, the E state is the least populated in the CHARMM27 trajectory and the most populated in the CHARMM36m trajectory. Overall, the CHARMM27 trajectory more evenly samples the four states, explaining its greater spectral heterogeneity (Figures 2 and S2). Additionally, we observe that the substructure within the states also varies considerably between the two force fields, with the CHARMM27 force field producing more pronounced conformational substructure and compact states than CHARMM36m. These inconsistencies highlight the necessity of using experimental data to validate IDP simulations.


### Ensemble refinement results in a predominantly extended state.
  To obtain experimentally informed estimates of the populations of the states, we refined the conformational ensembles using the Bayesian refinement method from Feng et al. As described in Bayesian refinement of the conformational ensemble, each of the 5000 segments was reweighted to give higher weights to those with calculated spectra that better matched the experimental data, as measured by the normalized overlap integral (Figure 5). The extended state is the dominant conformer, with its population increasing from the MD prior in both cases (though still differing by almost 30% between the two force fields). The population of the NC state significantly decreases to a negligible amount in both force fields despite CHARMM27 originally predicting it to be the most populated. The results align with the CHARMM36m prior, suggesting that its sampling of less compact states is more physically accurate.

  The refinement above equally weights all spectra in all regions. However, a given isotope label is only expected to be sensitive to its local environment and not to differences beyond it. As such, the equally weighted refinement can artificially favor some conformers over others when they are spectrally degenerate in regions. Furthermore, varying accuracy in the spectroscopic model between labels can cause the refinement procedure to overfit to label-specific artifacts. Both factors can lead to a biased fit.

  To assess whether the fit is biased in this way, we perform the ensemble refinement separately for each isotope label (Figure 6). In addition to the systematic discrepancy between the two force fields due to the reasons mentioned earlier, we see quantitative inconsistencies in the refined populations between the labels. The refinements based on almost all labels for both force fields agree that the extended state is dominant with the notable exception that the refinement based on the triple label predicts that the C state is the most populated. The C state is generally the second most populated, but the absolute populations vary significantly, especially for the CHARMM27 ensemble. Since isotope labeling does not alter the conformational ensemble, the differences between the refined populations indicates the need for a more careful approach.


### Analysis of state spectra reveals informative labels and frequency regions.
  To investigate the effects of spectral similarity between states and label-specific errors on the refinement, we calculate the spectra of the four states across all isotope labels. We focus on the CHARMM27 results for this analysis, where the prior populations of the four states are more equal and the inconsistencies of the refined populations between labels are more pronounced. Figure 7 shows the state spectra for all the isotope labels with the experimental spectra overlaid. Each state spectrum is calculated by averaging the spectra of all segments which spend at least 95% of their time in that state (Figure S12).

  We observe that individual labels cannot distinguish all four states equally well. For example, the G3 label, positioned close to the N terminus, can easily distinguish the presence of the N-terminal bend (distinguishing E/C from N/NC). However, it produces nearly identical spectra for the E/C states and for the N/NC states, indicating that it is not sensitive to the presence of the C-terminal turn. Conversely, the C-terminal labels V4, G6, and V7 are sensitive to the presence of the C-terminal turn but not the N-terminal bend. This pattern aligns with the physical intuition that a label can only distinguish structural features in its local environment.

  Figure 7 also demonstrates that the accuracy of the spectral simulation varies between labels. This results in the labels having different distributions of the error term Lx,l (Figure S11). Therefore, an unweighted sum over errors results in the labels with larger systematic errors disproportionately influencing the results. Comparing the simulated state spectra to experiment reveals how systematic errors in the forward model bias the fit. Notably, the simulated spectra systematically underestimate the peak broadening, especially in the main band region (≥1625 cm−1). This results in an artificial preference for the states without the N-terminal bend (E/C), which have a lower intensity in this region and therefore appear less inconsistent with experiment. While one can perform the refinement without the main band (Figures S13–S14), analogous issues arise at other frequencies.

  Improving the forward model would be one way of addressing the above issues but would also be a major undertaking (though methods for simultaneously refining the ensemble and forward model exist79,80). Here, we instead sought to mitigate the errors by modifying our refinement approach to restrict the fit to regions where (1) the states are spectrally distinguishable and (2) the simulation agrees reasonably with experiment. For the elastin-like peptide there are two classes of labels: those that can distinguish the presence or absence of the N-terminal bend and those that can distinguish the presence or absence of the C-terminal turn. Accordingly, we defined coarse-grained states by grouping states with a feature (N-terminal bend or C-terminal turn) and those without it, as well as corresponding difference spectra:  (4) Idiff,N(ω)=IN(ω)+INC(ω)-IE(ω)+IC(ω)   (5) Idiff,C(ω)=IC(ω)+INC(ω)-IE(ω)+IN(ω)

  To select against the regions where the simulation is most incorrect, we defined an information function D(ω) that penalizes the deviation δ(ω) between the experiment Iexp(ω) and average simulated spectrum Isim(ω):  (6) δ(ω)=Iexp(ω)-Isim(ω)   (7) D(N,C)(ω)=Idiff,(N,C)(ω)exp-(δ(ω))22σ2  where σ is a tuning parameter; we use σ=0.0005. The spectral regions with high D(ω) are therefore those which distinguish the conformers and are least affected by simulation error. We define the informative regions to be points with D(ω)>0.0015 in the label region (<1625 cm−1) and include all points within 5 cm−1 of these identified points. Examples of the thresholding process and the resulting informative labels and regions are shown in Figure 8. Plots of D(ω), rationale for the threshold selections, and results for all labels are shown in Figure S15.

  We used the informative regions to recalculate the populations using the coarse-grained states by restricting the overlap integral to those regions. The results are shown in Figure 9. For the C-terminal labels (V4/V7), the populations of the two coarse-grained states are close between the two force fields, showing consistency between the labels. This two-state decomposition enables comparison with ref. 19, where the authors study the turn extension distance and report that about 60% of the ensemble corresponds to extended structures (E/N). This new refinement with V4 and V7, which reports on the C-terminal turn, yields a similar population for the same force field (CHARMM27). Additionally, this finding contrasts with Selig et al.81 who reported that the shorter ELP VPGVG primarily adopts a turned (rather than extended) structure due to closer agreement between the simulated IR spectrum of the turned structure and the experiment. However, direct comparison is not possible since their experiments were performed at a different temperature, protonation, and solvent conditions.

  However, the refined populations are still inconsistent between labels and force fields. Notably, we see a consistent difference between the populations derived from the single and multiple labels, where the latter predicts higher populations of the turned/bent states. This overestimation is likely a result of the inaccuracies in the coupling model in the spectral simulation. In the singly labeled peptides, the isotope-labeled peak is decoupled from the rest of the spectrum, decreasing the impact of coupling errors. In contrast, the isotope-labeled peaks from the multiple labels become recoupled to each other, reintroducing these errors. These inaccuracies are likely also responsible for the incorrect intensities in the main band19. We therefore conclude that the singly labeled spectra serve as more reliable constraints with the current state of spectral simulation.

  Since we previously noted (Figure S11) that the spectroscopic simulation yields varying accuracy between different labels, we do not simply combine the error terms in the refinement and instead rely on these individual labels to determine the relative populations of the coarse-grained states. While V7 contains an informative region selected by the thresholding procedure, we discarded it since the identified region is small. Therefore, we chose to trust V4 and G3 as the most reliable reporters for the relative populations of the C-turn/no C-turn states and G3 for the N-bend/no N-bend states, respectively.

  We also see that the incorporation of selectivity in the refinement aids with consistency in the results. The initial equally weighted refinement predicted that the population of C was much larger than that of N for CHARMM27. This overestimation was likely due to the inaccuracy of the spectral simulation in the main band. However, our more robust strategy shows that they are both minor and approximately equal in population. This finding holds true for both force fields.

  Unfortunately, we cannot uniquely determine the four-state populations from the two-state estimates. With four unknown populations and only three equations (the two labels and the sum-to-one constraint), the system remains underdetermined and requires the choice of a free parameter. Figure 10 shows this approach using the results from G3 and V4 with pNC as a free parameter. The differences in populations between the two force fields arise from the differences in the sampled ensembles (priors). Even when the confidence in the experimental data is maximized, the sampled ensembles significantly affect the results owing to spectral similarity between states. Nevertheless, we can conclude that the E state is the dominant conformation, followed by roughly equal populations of the N and C states, with a negligible population of the NC state. Between the two force fields, CHARMM36m likely provides a closer estimate to the true populations due to the systematically smaller errors and greater similarity between the prior and posterior.


## Conclusions
  This study introduces considerations for using isotope-labeled spectra as structural constraints for the quantitative characterization of IDP ensembles, applied to the elastin-like peptide GVGVPGVG. Using MD simulations, we find that the ELP adopts four kinetically resolvable states, defined by the presence or absence of a backbone bend near the N-terminus and a β-turn near the C-terminus. Our Bayesian refinement approach discretizes the MD trajectories into segments which are reweighted based on the similarities of their calculated spectra to the experimental data. We find that the factors which influence the results of the refinement are the definition of states, internal competition between segments in the prior distribution, systematic errors in the forward model, and spectral similarity between states.

  By calculating the spectra of the four states for each label, we see that errors in the forward model (spectral simulation) can bias the fit toward various conformers. Notably, the inclusion of the main band results in an artificial preference for the states without the N-terminal bend. Additionally, we see that the accuracy of the simulation is inconsistent between labels and frequency regions. The state spectra are also degenerate since a given label can only report on its local environment: the labels close to the C-terminus cannot distinguish states that differ only with respect to the presence of the N-terminal bend, and the labels close to the N-terminus cannot distinguish states that differ only with respect to the presence of the C-terminal turn.

  We therefore chose to restrict the fit to the labels and frequency regions which maximize the spectral differences between states while minimizing the error from the spectral simulation. However, while we achieve the same relative ordering in the refined populations between the two force fields, their refined populations do not quantitatively agree due to their differing prior distributions. Nevertheless, we conclude that the extended (E) state is the dominant conformer, followed by small, roughly equal populations of the N and C states and a negligible population of the NC state. The refined populations more closely resemble the CHARMM36m prior populations, which favor more extended conformers. This validates the improvements made to the parametrization of the CHARMM36m force field, which was designed to improve the sampling of IDPs, mitigating the overstabilization of compact structures and transient secondary structure from CHARMM27.

  Future studies undertaking comparable refinements with isotope-labeled data will likely encounter similar challenges. An important area for improvement is the spectral simulation, as errors can bias the fit to a particular conformer. However, even if a perfect simulation is achieved, the spectral information remains fundamentally congested; the isotope-labeled peaks span a window of 40–50 cm−1 with homogeneous linewidths of 10–15 cm−1 which only shift a few wavenumbers with conformational variation. Extending these refinement methods to include 2D IR spectroscopy will provide some additional help, since 2D lineshapes reveal broadening due to conformational variation and cross peaks that arise from coupling between amide groups. However, homogeneous linewidths ultimately limit the degree of spectral separability, and both experimental and simulation errors (such as the coupling) are much more pronounced in 2D IR.

  The definition of states adds another layer of complexity, as the global changes which distinguish them do not always correspond to the local, short-ranged information in the labeled spectra. This is especially problematic with the approach of defining states through free energy basins, which can be heavily force field dependent for IDPs. In principle, we can address this by binning each state by the short-range contacts which give rise to the frequency shifts in the spectra, or even more directly by performing unsupervised learning (such as independent component analysis) on the spectral ensemble itself to explicitly determine which structural features correlate most with the spectral variation.

  Most importantly, this study underscores the importance of integrating experiment and simulation for the interpretation of isotope-labeled spectra. Indeed, our results suggest a rational approach for planning an isotope-labeled IR spectroscopy experiment with computation. First, the representative conformers should be identified independently of spectral data through simulations. Once the conformers are defined, the next step is to calculate the spectra of each state for hypothetical single isotope labels. Since only one MD trajectory is needed to generate multiple isotope-labeled spectra, this procedure is computationally inexpensive and has merit due to the cost of synthesizing an isotope label. Finally, the most informative isotope-labeled spectra and frequency regions should be identified. Intuitive placements are not always optimal—while G1 was synthesized on the N terminus to probe the dynamics, isotope labeling redshifted it into the congested main band, rendering it ineffective.

  A future direction involves extending this framework to larger proteins with more complex conformational ensembles and a greater number of potential isotope labels. As spectroscopic maps continue to improve, we can expect higher accuracy in ensemble refinement, reducing uncertainty in quantitative estimates and mitigating overfitting to the forward model. However, for much larger IDPs with hundreds to thousands of residues, performing spectral simulation with isotope labels at every residue would become computationally prohibitive. This highlights an opportunity to establish a data-driven approach to isotope labeling, connecting to the broader “sensor placement problem” of predicting the most informative labeling sites a priori.
