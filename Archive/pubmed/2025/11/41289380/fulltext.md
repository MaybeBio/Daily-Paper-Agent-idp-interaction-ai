# A machine learning protocol for predicting structural distributions of amyloid-forming proteins from 2D IR spectra


## Significance
  Protein misfolding contributes to the development of numerous neurodegenerative and amyloid-related diseases, yet its structural characterization remains difficult due to the transient and heterogeneous nature of misfolded states. We present a machine learning framework that decodes two-dimensional infrared (2DIR) spectra to reconstruct the three-dimensional conformational ensembles of key amyloidogenic segments from Aβ42, α-synuclein, hIAPP, and TTR. The model generalizes across diverse systems and ligand-bound states, and its attention mechanism identifies spectroscopic features most indicative of local folding transitions. This model demonstrates that it is possible to accurately interpret 2D IR spectra, thereby providing insight into misfolding structures and aiding therapeutic development.


## Results

### Spectral-Driven Machine Learning Protocol for Amyloid Segment Conformational Prediction.
  The machine learning workflow comprises three stages: “Dataset construction”(Fig. 1A), “Machine learning protocol” (Fig. 1B), and “Model application” (Fig. 1C). For dataset construction, atomic-resolution structures of misfolding-related proteins—including Aβ42, hIAPP, α-synuclein, and the TTR (105 to 115) fragment—were obtained from publicly available all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations that capture the conformational evolution of amyloidogenic protein segments over microsecond timescales (10, 12, 28, 29). To minimize redundancy and reduce temporal correlation in the training data, we performed uniform sampling by extracting one frame every 10 to 355 femtoseconds, depending on the specific system (MD details provided in the SI Appendix). In total, 160,910 conformational snapshots were collected from four amyloidogenic proteins. These trajectories capture notable conformational transitions of monomeric segments, reflecting their intrinsic structural heterogeneity, as evidenced by the RMSD profiles shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. While our training dataset was derived from microsecond-scale simulations, the essential requirement for applying this machine learning framework is that the MD trajectories used for training adequately sample the relevant conformational transitions of the protein system, regardless of the absolute simulation length. The peptide segments, their parent proteins, and associated pathological relevance are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1.

  We constructed a foundational machine learning dataset by theoretical simulations. For each protein conformation, 2DIR spectra were generated within the amide I region using the Frenkel exciton Hamiltonian formalism. The Hamiltonians were computed from established vibrational spectroscopic maps (30, 31) that have been shown to accurately reproduce experimental results. Subsequently, 2DIR signals were simulated using the NISE code developed by Jansen and colleagues (32, 33). Given the challenges in predicting full-length protein structures and the functional importance of these misfolding-associated amyloid segments, we focused our subsequent structural analysis on the key segments by computing alpha carbon (Cα) distance maps for each fragment conformation using Visual Molecular Dynamics software (34). This fragment-centric approach can capture essential structural features while avoiding the complexities of modeling entire protein systems. Finally, these simulated 2DIR spectral data, along with the corresponding Cα distance maps, formed the training dataset, providing a correlation between spectral features and structures for subsequent machine learning models (Fig. 1A).

  In the “Machine Learning Protocol” stage (Fig. 1B), we employed a transformer-in-transformer (TNT-S) model (35), a hierarchical architecture designed for multilevel local-to-global representation learning. This framework enables the model to extract fine-grained features from high-dimensional 2DIR spectra and map local spectral signatures to spatial structural patterns. Each input spectrum was reshaped into a 224 × 224 matrix and partitioned into 16×16 outer patches, each comprising 4 × 4 inner patches. The inner patches were first embedded with positional encodings and processed by an inner Transformer block, which used multihead self-attention and feed-forward layers to learn localized spectral features. These were then aggregated into outer patch representations and passed through an outer Transformer block to capture global spectral context. After 12 layers of outer block processing, the output features were refined via a convolutional layer to enhance spatial consistency. The final model output was a predicted interresidue distance map of amyloid segments, which was subsequently used to reconstruct their 3D structures via a gradient-based folding algorithm (36, 37).

  In the “Model Application” stage (Fig. 1C), the trained model was applied to 2DIR spectra obtained from molecular dynamics simulations of the Aβ42 aggregation-prone segment bound to the small-molecule inhibitor 10074-G5. The model successfully captured dynamic conformational transitions induced by ligand binding. To improve interpretability, an attention mechanism was integrated into the model (38). This mechanism enables the network to assign higher weights to the most informative spectral regions during prediction (38). The resulting attention maps provide intuitive visualizations that highlight which parts of the spectrum (e.g., diagonal or cross-peaks) contributed most to the structural inference, offering mechanistic insights into amyloid misfolding processes.


### Predicting the Structural Evolution of Amyloid Segments From 2DIR Spectra.
  We have evaluated the ML model predictive performance in reconstructing misfolding-associated amyloid segments by assessing both the predicted Cα distance maps and the full three-dimensional backbone structures. The distance map’s accuracy was quantified using mean absolute error (MAE), while the structural fidelity of the 3D reconstructions was evaluated by RMSD. To elucidate the model’s learning dynamics during training, we analyzed the progression of training and validation losses over epochs. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, both curves exhibit a uniform decline, eventually converging to low error values with closely aligned trajectories. This convergence pattern indicates that the model successfully captured the mapping from spectral inputs to structural outputs, without signs of substantial overfitting—i.e., a situation where the model fits the training data well but fails to generalize to unseen data (39). To further improve generalization and reduce the risk of overfitting, we employed a validation-based checkpointing strategy and an adaptive learning rate schedule (40, 41). In brief, the checkpointing strategy saved the model whenever the validation loss improved, while the adaptive learning rate schedule gradually reduced the learning rate to stabilize convergence (details provided in the SI Appendix). These techniques enhanced training stability and contributed to the overall robustness of the model.

  On the independent test set, the model exhibited strong generalization capability, achieving an average MAE of 0.93 Å in Cα distance map prediction (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3) and an average Cα RMSD of 1.87 Å in the reconstruction of three-dimensional backbone structures (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). A comprehensive summary of performance metrics across all tested amyloidogenic segments is provided in SI Appendix, Table S2, further demonstrating the model’s predictive accuracy and consistency across diverse protein systems. To evaluate the model’s ability to capture conformational dynamics, we examined its predictions along the folding trajectories of several representative amyloidogenic segments. Five characteristic conformational states were selected and compared against reference structures from MD simulations. As shown in Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5, the predicted structures closely matched the corresponding MD snapshots, and structural overlays revealed that the model reliably captured intermediate and transient folding states.


### Model-Informed Prediction of the Structural Evolution of Aβ42 During Molecule Inhibitor Binding.
  To evaluate the model’s application and generalization capacity, we applied it to a biologically relevant scenario: the interaction between the Aβ42 and the small-molecule inhibitor 10074-G5. In this case, the molecular dynamics trajectory of the Aβ42-10074-G5 complex was used exclusively at the application stage to provide a continuous conformational landscape under ligand-binding conditions (10), while only the monomeric trajectories were included in the training dataset. This separation ensured no overlap between training and evaluation, and the successful application to the complex further demonstrates that the framework can be extended to other protein–ligand systems beyond the training set. As shown in Fig. 3A, the RMSD trajectory reveals recurrent fluctuations, reflecting dynamic and reversible transitions between compact and extended states. We quantitatively assessed the model’s predictive performance and application by comparing its outputs to reference MD conformations across the trajectory. For the 17 to 23 segment of Aβ42 (denoted KE7), the model achieved an MAE of 1.00 Å and backbone RMSD of 1.57 Å. Notably, for the more flexible 37 to 42 segment (GA6), the model delivered even greater accuracy, with an average MAE of 0.37 Å and RMSD of 0.69 Å (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These results demonstrate the model’s robustness in resolving fine-scale local structural variations across diverse conformational states.

  To further evaluate the model’s ability to resolve intermediate conformations, five representative time points (t1–5) were uniformly selected at equal intervals along the MD trajectory to provide snapshots of conformational evolution, rather than focusing on a single conformation. At each time point, the trained model was used to reconstruct the structures of the two aggregation-prone regions of Aβ42. As illustrated in Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7, the predicted structures closely match their MD references, accurately capturing both folded and disordered states in response to ligand-induced perturbations. These findings collectively underscore the model’s ability to generalize to unseen conformational contexts and to resolve segment-specific structural transitions informed by simulated spectral descriptors, offering a computational route for probing local folding behavior during small molecule–protein interactions.


### Attention-Guided Interpretation of Spectral–Structural Relationships.
  Although 2DIR spectroscopy encodes rich structural information through features such as vibrational couplings and cross-peaks, interpreting these complex patterns and directly linking them to specific protein motifs remains a substantial challenge. To enhance the interpretability of our deep learning framework and gain mechanistic insights into its decision-making process, we implemented an attention-based analysis strategy grounded in transformer-in-transformer architecture. In deep learning models, attention maps represent internal weight distributions that indicate which input regions the model considers most important when making predictions (38, 42–43). In our case, these maps reveal which portions of the 2DIR spectrum contribute most strongly to the predicted protein structure, thereby enabling human-understandable interpretations of the model’s reasoning.

  We applied this analysis to the Aβ42-10074-G5 interaction system and extracted attention maps from the Transformer encoder layers. As shown in Fig. 4A, we selected four representative time points (t1–5) from the molecular dynamics trajectory to monitor how spectral and structural features evolve upon ligand binding. At each time point, we visualize 1) the protein–ligand complex (surface representation), 2) the corresponding simulated 2DIR spectrum, and 3) the attention map from the sixth Transformer layer, highlighting spectral regions most relevant to structural prediction. As shown in Fig. 4, the α-helical content of Aβ42 increased from 9.5% to 59.5% along the binding process (Fig. 4B), reflecting a progressive coil-to-helix transition induced by inhibitor association (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Table S3). Correspondingly, the model’s attention maps (Fig. 4 A, Bottom row) exhibited a systematic shift toward lower vibrational frequencies, particularly near 1,650 cm−1, consistent with the amide I redshift typically associated with α-helix formation (44–47). This spectral redshift was accompanied by spatially localized attention focused on key aggregation-prone segments, highlighting the model’s capacity to capture helix-related vibrational signatures in step with major conformational reorganization. Although the model targets amyloidogenic aggregation-prone segments, the observed coil-to-helix conversions in these segments mirror the broader structural transitions of the full-length Aβ42 molecule (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Table S3). The alignment between attention patterns, spectral shifts, and secondary structure evolution demonstrates that the model has learned a physically meaningful mapping between 2DIR spectral features and conformational states—supporting both its mechanistic interpretability and biophysical relevance.


## Discussion
  This computational study introduces a machine learning framework that leverages 2DIR spectroscopy for accurately predicting the conformational ensembles of aggregation-prone segments implicated in amyloid protein misfolding. By focusing on key aggregation-prone segments—including Aβ17-23, Aβ37-42, α-Syn66-74, α-Syn71-82, hIAPP22-27, and TTR105-115—the model characterizes representative conformational intermediates in different protein systems. In the case of the Aβ42-10074-G5 complex, the model also captures ligand-associated structural variations without requiring retraining, indicating its potential applicability to related systems. Although the proteins examined in this study are amyloid-forming, their monomeric segments share physicochemical characteristics with many hydrophobic regions in nonamyloid proteins. Therefore, the proposed framework is expected to be broadly applicable beyond amyloid-forming systems. To aid interpretation of the model’s predictions, an attention-based analysis module highlights the spectral regions most relevant for structure prediction. This provides a transparent link between spectral features and structural variations in amyloidogenic segments. While our demonstration used simulations to validate the method, our work puts in place a method for interpreting experimental 2D IR data. Experimental 2D IR spectra will contain noise and require a more sophisticated Hamiltonian, but our procedure shows that accurate structures are possible, which sets the stage for many useful studies in the future. Together, this work establishes a computational pipeline that bridges simulated spectroscopy and structure prediction, offering a useful approach for probing protein misfolding, supporting the design of small-molecule inhibitors, and enabling segment-specific structural monitoring.


## Methods

### 2DIR Spectra Simulation.
  2DIR spectra were simulated within the framework of the Frenkel exciton Hamiltonian, following a five-step procedure:


#### Construction of the exciton Hamiltonian.
  The Hamiltonian was constructed to include the vibrational frequencies of the local modes, the couplings between different modes, and an anharmonic term, expressed as follows:H=∑iNωibi†bi+∑i,jNJijbi†bj-∑iNΔi2bi†bi†bibi,

  where bi† and bi represent the creation and annihilation operators for the peptide unit, respectively, ωi is the vibrational frequency of the local mode, Jij is the vibrational coupling between modes i and j, and Δi is the anharmonicity of the mode i.


#### Calculation of vibrational frequencies.
  The vibrational frequencies of the local modes were calculated using the Skinner map (48), expressed as follows:ω=ωmap+∑iPi,mapPi+(Ei,map·Ei),

  where ωmap, Pi,map, and Ei,map are the predefined vibrational frequency, electric potential, and electric field in the map, respectively. Pi and Ei are calculated as follows:

  P=∑jqj|rj|,

  Ei=∑jqj|rj|3(rj·i^).


#### Calculation of vibrational coupling.
  The interactions between oscillators were evaluated using different approaches depending on their relative positions. For adjacent oscillators, the transition charge coupling (TCC) method was employed, while for nonadjacent oscillators, the glycine dipeptide (GLDP) model was adopted, as described in references (49, 50). The TCC formulation is expressed as follows:J=14πε∑a,bdqadqb|rab|-3qaqb(va·rab)(vb·rab)|rab|5-dqaqb(vb·rab)-qadqb(va·rab)-qaqb(va·vb)|rab|3,J=(1-u)(1-t)·mapϕ30ψ30+(1-u)t·mapϕ30ψ30+u(1-t)·mapϕ30ψ30+u·t·mapϕ30ψ30.


#### Calculation of dipole moment.
  The dipole moment of each oscillator was determined from the relative positions of the C, O, H, and N atoms (51) and is expressed asμ=2.73s-(CO·s)+|s|2-(CO·s)2tan10CO.


#### Calculation of 2DIR signals.
  The 2DIR spectrum was obtained by summing the imaginary parts of the rephasing (photon-echo, kI=-k1+k2+k3) and nonrephasing signals (kII=k1-k2+k3). The total signal was further decomposed into contributions from ground-state bleach (GB), stimulated emission (SE), and excited-state absorption (EA).I2D(ω3,t2,ω1)=SI(ω3,t2,ω1)+SII(ω3,t2,ω1)S(I)(ω3,t2,ω1)=∫0∞∫0∞dt3dt1(SGB(I)(t3,t2,t1)+SSE(I)(t3,t2,t1)+SEA(I)(t3,t2,t1))exp(i(ω3t3-ω1t1)),SII(ω3,t2,ω1)=∫0∞∫0∞dt3dt1(SGBII(t3,t2,t1)+SSEII(t3,t2,t1)+SEAII(t3,t2,t1))exp(i(ω3t3+ω1t1)).


### Machine Learning Protocol.
  In this study, the TNT-S (Transformer in Transformer) model architecture was applied to process 2DIR spectral data and generate protein distance maps. Initially, the input 2DIR spectral data were transformed into a 224 × 224 matrix. To meet the input requirements of the TNT-S model, the matrix was divided into 16 × 16 outer patches, each further subdivided into 4 × 4 inner patches to facilitate fine-grained information processing.

  In the TNT-S model, the inner patches were first linearly projected, and positional encodings were added to generate the inner patch embeddings. These embeddings were then processed through the inner Transformer module, where multihead self-attention (MSA) and feed-forward neural networks (MLP) were employed to extract and aggregate fine-grained local features from the inner patches. The processed features from the inner patches were aggregated into outer patch representations and further refined by the outer Transformer module. In the outer Transformer module, the outer patch embeddings were enhanced with positional encodings, allowing the model to capture global information from the spectral data. Through the collaborative function of both the inner and outer Transformer modules, the model effectively extracts multiscale features from the spectral data. The inner Transformer module focuses on aggregating fine-grained local features, while the outer Transformer module further integrates and captures global contextual information. This multilevel feature extraction mechanism ensures that the model can efficiently interpret and process the spectral data at different scales, while maintaining high resolution and data integrity.

  After processing through 12 layers of the outer Transformer module, the extracted high-level features were passed through a convolutional block (ConvBlock), which consisted of two convolutional layers. Each convolutional layer included convolution operations, ReLU activation functions, BatchNorm2d normalization, and AdaptiveAvgPool2d pooling. These convolutional layers served to extract spatial features and enhance the model’s nonlinear representation capability, ensuring stability in the processed data. Finally, the protein distance map predicted by the model was used to construct the three-dimensional protein backbone structure by integrating a gradient-based folding algorithm.
