# ricePSP: a database of rice phase separation-associated proteins


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03842-w.


## Background
  In live cells, many proteins with intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) or prion-like domains (PrDs) can undergo LLPS and form dynamic membraneless biomolecular condensates that compartmentalize specific biochemical reactions in space and time [1–3]. These dynamic structures are formed through multivalent weak interactions between proteins and/or nucleic acids, resulting in a dense phase enriched in specific molecules, distinct from the surrounding cytoplasm or nucleoplasm [3, 4]. Unlike membrane-bound organelles, LLPS-driven condensates exhibit liquid-like properties such as fusion, fission, and dynamic exchange of components with their environment, enabling the spatial and temporal organization of cellular biochemistry without requiring membrane boundaries and allowing them to concentrate specific biomolecules, buffer fluctuations, and facilitate rapid and reversible cellular responses to internal and external stimuli [3–6]. A growing body of evidence indicates that these condensates play critical roles in a variety of biological processes. For instance, LLPS has been implicated in ribosome biogenesis within nucleoli, RNA metabolism regulation through stress granules and processing bodies, RNA splicing in nuclear speckles, transcriptional activation in super-enhancers, DNA damage repair at damage foci, and even large-scale chromatin compartmentalization that shapes 3D genome architecture [1, 7–15]. Dysregulation of LLPS is also linked to various diseases [16–19], including neurodegeneration and cancer, highlighting its biological importance. As such, understanding the roles of LLPS in other kingdoms, such as plants, is of considerable scientific interest.

  Over the past decade, thanks to advances in imaging, biophysics, and proteomics, significant progress has been made in elucidating LLPS mechanisms and identifying phase separation-associated proteins (PSPs) in animal systems, leading to the characterization of hundreds of PSPs involved in various physiological and pathological processes [20–22]. In plants, LLPS research is still in its early stages, but increasing evidence suggests that this mechanism is conserved and functionally relevant [23]. To date, more than 70 PSPs have been identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, many of which are involved in stress responses, RNA metabolism, and chromatin regulation that localize to biomolecular condensates such as stress granules, mRNA processing bodies, or photobodies [7, 8, 23–25]. Additionally, large-scale experimental methods such as b-isox-mediated precipitation assays have been used to capture potential PSPs in a few other plant species, revealing a broader prevalence of LLPS-like behavior in the plant kingdom [26]. However, PSPs in agriculturally important crop species remain largely unexplored, and their potential roles in biological functions, especially in developmental regulation and stress adaptation, are still largely unknown. Given the critical importance of crops in global food security, there is an urgent need to perform systematic, genome-wide identification of PSPs in key model crops such as rice (Oryza sativa), uncover their possible functions, and lay the foundation for LLPS-based crop improvement strategies.

  In this study, we predict the PS scores of all the annotated proteins and construct a database of rice PSPs. We validate the predicted PSPs through in vivo condensate imaging assays and AlphaFold protein structure predictions. Additionally, we demonstrate a case study using ricePSP to identify flowering-related PSPs in rice. Several heading-date-related proteins exhibit high PS scores, and many of them can form liquid-like condensates, indicating that LLPS play a crucial role in rice flowering regulation. Taken together, our ricePSP database may serve as a valuable resource for protein function research and facilitate our understanding of their molecular mechanisms underlying complex biological process regulation through the lens of PS.


## Construction and content

### Protein data collection and preprocessing
  The rice protein sequences and functional annotations were obtained from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.uga.edu/) and funRiceGenes (https://venyao.xyz/funRiceGenes/) databases. After initial filtering to remove incomplete or low-confidence entries, a total of 66,338 annotated proteins and 3763 functionally characterized proteins were retained for analysis (Fig. 1A). Each protein was assigned to a unique gene ID and associated with available descriptions, functional annotations, and sequence features. For functionally cloned proteins, annotations included known interacting proteins, functional pathways, and regulatory networks, as well as documented associations with rice traits. To provide additional biological context, many proteins were grouped into more than 70 protein families based on shared sequence domains or biological function. Furthermore, a curated set of functional tags was obtained from the funRiceGenes to classify the proteins according to their involvement in key biological processes such as flowering regulation, RNA metabolism, chromatin modification, hormone signaling, and biotic/abiotic stress response [27]. ### Phase separation and prion-like domain prediction
  To predict the potential of each protein to form LLPS-mediated condensates, we applied the PSPredictor (http://www.pkumdl.cn:8000/PSPredictor/) and MolPhase (Molecular Condensation and Phase Separation Predictor, https://molphase.sbs.ntu.edu.sg/) algorithms, which integrate sequence-derived features such as amino acid composition, low-complexity regions, and intrinsic disorder scores [28, 29]. The custom pipeline for rapid and high-throughput prediction of PS scores and MolPhase scores has been deposited on github (https://github.com/wzouyang/ricePSP). PS score and MolPhase score ranging from 0 to 1 were assigned to each protein. Proteins with PS scores above 0.7 were classified as high-confidence PSPs. In parallel, PrDs were identified using PLAAC (Prion-Like Amino Acid Composition, http://plaac.wi.mit.edu/), a tool that calculates a log-likelihood ratio based on known yeast PrDs [30]. Proteins with significant PLAAC cores were recorded as PrD-containing proteins (PrDPs). Totally, we predicted 10,654 PSPs (Additional file 2: Table S1) and 1394 PrDPs (Additional file 2: Table S2), which distribute in > 6100 protein families (Additional file 2: Table S3).


### Web interface design and implementation
  The ricePSP repository was initially forked from the funRiceGenes (https://github.com/funRiceGenes). The website is implemented as a static web application built using the Jekyll static site generator, which is written in Ruby. Briefly, Ruby provides the runtime environment for Jekyll to transform source files, including Markdown content, HTML templates, configuration files, and plain text data files, into a fully static website composed of HTML, CSS, and JavaScript files. This static site generation approach allows ricePSP to be hosted efficiently on the GitHub Pages without requiring a backend server. On the client side, the website leverages web technologies including HTML5 and CSS3 for markup and styling, and JavaScript libraries such as jQuery and DataTables.js to provide dynamic and interactive features. DataTables.js specifically enables advanced table functionalities like searching, filtering, sorting, and pagination, which are essential for exploring the large datasets presented in ricePSP. Responsive design principles are implemented through the Bootstrap framework, ensuring the site is accessible across various devices and screen sizes. All curated data are maintained primarily in Markdown and plain text formats, which are processed during site generation and rendered efficiently on the client side. This approach streamlines data management and maintenance, while delivering fast page loads and a smooth user experience without relying on server-side data queries. ricePSP is designed with seven main modules, including HOME page, PSP, PRDP, FAMS, KEYS, DOCS, and LINK, providing functions for hosting, searching, downloading, visualizing, and exploring the functions of PSPs in rice (Fig. 1B). Overall, the integration of Ruby-based Jekyll for static site generation with rich client-side interactivity through JavaScript frameworks results in a performant, easily maintainable, and user-friendly web platform for ricePSP.


### Search, accessibility, and continuous updates
  To maximize usability and transparency, ricePSP supports multi-keyword searches, real-time filtering, and full-site text queries via a Bing-powered search bar. Each detailed gene page integrates PS, PrD, and MolPhase scores, AlphaFold model status, family membership, functional annotations, and literature references, enabling comprehensive exploration of candidate PSPs. All datasets, source code, and documentation are openly accessible through GitHub [31] and Zenodo [32], with version control ensuring reproducibility and transparency. The site is deployed via GitHub Pages to guarantee stable availability, and a downloadable PDF manual guides users through module functions, data interpretation, and example applications. The database will be continuously updated as new rice genome annotations and LLPS prediction tools emerge, with planned expansions to incorporate transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, in vivo and in vitro validation results, cross-species comparisons, and additional crop genomes.


### AlphaFold protein structure prediction and PS validation
  It has been reported that IDRs overlap with low-confidence protein structure regions predicted by AlphaFold [33–35]. To support and complement computational LLPS predictions, we predicted protein structures using ColabFold, an AlphaFold-derived method that is tens of folds faster than AlphaFold2 [36]. AlphaFold2 provides highly accurate protein structure predictions, often rivaling experimental results and outperforming other prediction strategies for most proteins [37], but requires substantial computational resources and time [36]. ColabFold enables efficient prediction of the structures and per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) for 3763 functionally characterized rice proteins (Additional file 2: Table S4). The parameters used for ColabFold were msa_mode: mmseqs2_uniref_env, pair_mode: unpaired_paired, model_type: auto, num_recycles: 3, and relax_max_iterations: 200. pLDDT scores for each residue were extracted to identify IDRs, which are often critical for LLPS behavior. Proteins with extensive disordered regions, indicated by low pLDDT values, were flagged as structurally compatible with PS, providing an additional layer of validation for the predicted PSPs.


### In vivo condensate validation
  Protoplast isolation and transient expression assays were performed to experimentally validate the PS behavior of candidate proteins, following a previously reported protocol [38]. Seeds of the Xian group rice variety Minghui 63 were germinated and grown on MS medium for 10–15 days. Seedlings were cut into 0.5–1 mm segment and immediately transferred to 0.6 M mannitol solution for 10 min. The tissues were then incubated in 10 ml enzymatic solution (0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES pH 7.5, 1.5% Cellulose RS, 0.75% Macerozyme, 0.1% BSA, 1 mM NaCl) under vacuum for 30 min, followed by shaking incubation at 28 °C for 4–5 h in the dark. After digestion, 10 ml of W5 solution (1.54 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MES pH 5.7) was added, and the lysate was filtered through a 350-mesh sieve. Protoplasts were collected by centrifugation at 100 g for 8 min at room temperature with minimal acceleration and deceleration.

  For transient expression, coding sequences of the candidate proteins were cloned into GFP reporter vectors. Plasmid DNA was extracted and transfected into rice protoplasts using the PEG-CaCl2 method [38]. Briefly, protoplasts were resuspended in W5 solution and incubated in the dark for 30–60 min, followed by resuspension in MMG solution (0.6 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, 4 mM MES pH 5.7). A mixture containing 5 µg (10 µl) of plasmid DNA and 100 µl of protoplasts was gently combined with 110 µl of freshly prepared PEG-CaCl2 solution (0.6 M mannitol, 100 mM CaCl2, 40% PEG 4000) and incubated for 15 min. Transformation was stopped by adding 440 µl of W5 solution, followed by centrifugation to remove ~ 600 µl supernatant. Protoplasts were then resuspended in 800 µl WI solution (4 mM MES pH 5.7, 0.6 M mannitol, 4 mM KCl) and incubated at 28 °C in the dark for more than 12 h. Fluorescence signals of GFP fusion proteins were observed using a Leica TCS SP8 STED confocal microscope to assess the formation of in vivo condensates.


## Utility and discussion

### Database organization and web interface
  The ricePSP database is organized into seven main modules: HOME, PSP, FAMS, PRDP, KEYS, DOCS, and LINK (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). The HOME page provides access to the complete datasets, including sequences of all annotated proteins, predicted PSPs and PrDPs, protein family information, homologous protein sequences, and literature references on experimentally reported PSPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). All datasets are freely downloadable to facilitate downstream analyses.

  The PSP module lists predicted PS scores for approximately 4000 functionally characterized rice proteins (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), while the PRDP module catalogs proteins with high PLAAC scores (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C). Each protein entry includes detailed information such as MSU and RAPdb gene loci, PS/MolPhase and PLAAC scores and pLDDT values, relevant literature, observed condensate evidence, and homologs in other species. The FAMS module presents predicted PS and PLAAC scores for over 6000 protein family members (Additional file 1: Fig. S1D). Notably, certain families display a high prevalence of PS potential. For example, 75% of AGO, 64.3% of ALOG, 71.4% of WOX, and 100% of dehydrin (OsjDHN) family members are predicted to be PSPs. The KEYS module links PSPs to specific agronomic traits or biological processes using curated keywords derived from the funRiceGenes database (Additional file 1: Fig. S1E), enabling users to quickly identify candidate regulators for traits such as flowering time, stress tolerance, or hormone signaling. The DOCS module compiles more than 200 published studies reporting biomolecular condensates in plants (Additional file 1: Fig. S1F), serving as a valuable reference library. The LINK module provides links to the database authors’ contact information and related resources, facilitating communication and collaboration.


### Comparison with other prediction methods
  To evaluate the performance of ricePSP predictions, we compared the PSPredictor results with two other prediction tools, PLAAC and MolPhase (Fig. 2A and B). PLAAC predicts PrDs, which are often associated with PS, whereas both PSPredictor and MolPhase directly estimate PS propensity based on sequence features and disorder-related characteristics. The comparison with PLAAC revealed 766 proteins consistently identified as PSPs by both methods, while 9888 and 628 proteins were uniquely predicted by PSPredictor and PLAAC, respectively (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the overlap with MolPhase was substantially larger, comprising 9890 common predictions, with 764 and 23,618 proteins uniquely predicted by PSPredictor and MolPhase, respectively (Fig. 2B), reflecting that not all PrDPs necessarily exhibit PS potential, and that some PSPs lack typical PrD signatures. Functional enrichment analysis of the PSPs and their overlaps with PLAAC predictions revealed significant enrichment in RNA-related biological processes, including spliceosome, mRNA surveillance pathway, RNA degradation, and RNA transport (Fig. 2C), in line with previously reported results of phase-reparation-associated proteins via biotinylated isoxazole precipitation [26]. ### Validation using AlphaFold protein structure prediction
  To strengthen the validation of PSP predictions, we systematically compared AlphaFold-predicted protein structures with PS scores (Fig. 3). Because IDRs lack stable three-dimensional conformations, their structures cannot be reliably predicted by AlphaFold, typically resulting in low-confidence pLDDT values in these regions [33, 34]. As representative examples, we examined two biologically well-characterized rice proteins with contrasting PS profiles. Hd3a, a florigen critical for flowering regulation, had an extremely low PS score (0.0205) and displayed a stable structure with uniformly high pLDDT values (> 90) across its entire sequence, indicating a high-confidence folded conformation with negligible intrinsically disordered content (Fig. 3A). The PS score profile was flat and well below the disorder threshold, consistent with its stable structural role in protein–protein interactions rather than condensate formation. In contrast, OsAlba3, a member of the Alba family of nucleic acid–binding proteins, showed an exceptionally high PS score (0.9976) and contained a large C-terminal segment with persistently low pLDDT values (< 60), indicative of extensive IDRs (Fig. 3B). The PS score profile for OsAlba3 closely matched these IDR-rich regions, reflecting the capacity for multivalent, flexible interactions that facilitate PS.   Extending this analysis genome-wide, over 3000 proteins revealed a negative correlation between PS score and mean pLDDT score (R2 = 0.4224) (Fig. 3C), supporting the expectation that high PS potential is associated with low structural confidence and higher IDR content. Classification into high PS score proteins (HPSPs) and low PS score proteins (LPSPs) further confirmed that LPSPs had significantly higher pLDDT values (median = 82.42) than HPSPs (median = 62.36) (p < 2.22 × 10−16) (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, to assess prediction reliability on known examples, we collected previously reported rice PSPs such as FZP [39], EHD6 and YTH07 [40], and DRG9 [41], and calculated their PS and MolPhase scores, and pLDDT values (Additional file 1: Table S5). The observed patterns, such as consistently high PS scores coupled with lower pLDDT values for intrinsically disordered proteins, align with established PS principles, further supporting the predictive validity of ricePSP. Interestingly, we also identified several experimentally reported PSPs with high MolPhase scores but relatively low PS scores, accompanied by comparatively high pLDDT values. Such cases may correspond to proteins with more structured domains or context-dependent PS behavior, which might be more readily captured by MolPhase’s broader disorder-related features but not by PSPredictor’s specific training set. For example, proteins such as OsU2AF35a and OsU2AF65b exhibit low PS scores, suggesting limited intrinsic PS propensity, yet they may still participate in biomolecular condensates through interactions with other high-confidence PSPs (e.g., OsU2AF35b), acting as partners rather than primary drivers of condensate formation [42]. This partner-mediated recruitment mechanism has been reported in other systems and highlights the complex interplay between IDRs and structured domains in condensate assembly [43]. These discrepancies emphasize the complementary strengths of different prediction tools and the need to interpret scores in a functional context. Therefore, ricePSP provides PS and MolPhase scores, PLAAC scores, and pLDDT values for each protein entry, enabling users to make an informed and comprehensive assessment of its PS potential.

  To extend this validation at the protein family level, we compared two contrasting gene families (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The 14-3-3 family, known for stable, well-folded dimeric structures that interact with Hd3a, displayed uniformly low PS scores (average 0.0281) and high pLDDT values (average 90.56), indicating no detectable IDR content and negligible PS potential (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A–C). In contrast, the dehydrin (OsjDHN) family, which functions in abiotic stress tolerance, showed exceptionally high PS scores (average 0.9753) and low pLDDT values (average 54.82), suggesting extensive IDRs (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A–C). These family-level comparisons reinforce the mechanistic link between intrinsic disorder and high PS propensity, highlighting that the PS scoring framework not only aligns with AlphaFold structural predictions but also distinguishes biologically meaningful differences across protein families.


### PS-associated regulatory module in rice flowering
  As a proof of concept, we demonstrate ricePSP’s utility in hypothesis generation for functional studies. To obtain a global view of LLPS research in plants, we collected more than 200 publications related to plant PS from 2017 to 2024 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A) and extracted keywords to generate a word cloud (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B). Notably, many enriched terms, such as flowering, light, photobody, PhyB, Cry, and PIF, are closely associated with photoperiodic flowering regulation [44]. Motivated by this observation, we focused on evaluating the PS potential of rice proteins involved in flowering time regulation, a developmental process previously linked to LLPS in Arabidopsis [45]. Expectedly, many rice flowering-related proteins [46, 47], such as OsPhyB, OsGI, Ghd7, Ghd7.1, Ghd8, and Hd1, displayed low pLDDT scores and high PS potentials (Fig. 4A), suggesting the presence of extensive IDRs. Network analysis revealed that these proteins form a densely connected regulatory module with extensive protein–protein interactions, linking photoreceptors, circadian clock components, and downstream floral regulators into a coherent PS-associated pathway (Fig. 4B). Importantly, recent findings indicate that under long-day conditions, Hd1, Ghd7, and Ghd8 form repressive complexes that delay flowering, whereas under short-day conditions, Ghd7 expression is suppressed and Hd1 functions as a transcriptional activator to promote flowering [47–49]. Together with our prediction results, this photoperiod-dependent functional switch highlights how LLPS may provide a dynamic regulatory mechanism for precise temporal control of flowering.   To test these predictions, we performed transient GFP-fusion expression assays in rice protoplasts for a subset of candidate proteins. Several flowering-related PSPs, including OsPIL13, OsPIL15, OsCO3, OsCCT38, ELF3-2, and OsIDS1, exhibited distinct punctate nuclear condensates in vivo (Fig. 4C, Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Notably, many of these proteins belong to transcription factor families enriched in IDRs, supporting the mechanistic model in which IDR-rich transcriptional regulators assemble into dynamic nuclear bodies to coordinate gene expression timing. Together, these computational and experimental results provide evidence for potentially conserved LLPS-mediated flowering control in plants and establish ricePSP as a powerful resource for uncovering novel condensate-forming proteins in crop plants.

  Recent work by Cui et al. provides experimental support for LLPS-mediated flowering regulation [40]. They identified EHD6 as a flowering-promoting factor in rice, which encodes an RNA-binding protein harboring both an RNA-recognition motif (RRM) and a prion-like domain (PrLD). Via its PrLD, EHD6 undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) to assemble cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein (RNP) condensates. Within these condensates, EHD6 directly interacts with the m6A reader YTH07, forming an EHD6-YTH07 complex that exhibits enhanced affinity for m6A-modified RNAs. Consequently, the flowering repressor OsCOL4 mRNA is sequestered and translationally repressed in the condensates, leading to decreased OsCOL4 protein levels. This releases transcriptional suppression of the downstream activator Ehd1, promotes florigen gene expression, and ultimately accelerates heading. Consistent with this model, both ehd6 and yth07 loss-of-function mutants exhibit delayed flowering. These findings provide a concrete example of how perturbation of a specific phase-separating protein can elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which LLPS regulates flowering and other biological traits. In the future, integrating ricePSP predictions with targeted disruption of phase separation in candidate proteins may serve as a powerful approach for understanding the roles of LLPS in controlling diverse biological processes and genetic traits.


### PSPs related to stress response
  The word cloud analysis revealed that, in addition to flowering-related terms, keywords such as stress, heat, temperature, salt, stress granule, immune, virus infection, and environmental response were also strongly enriched (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B), highlighting the close association between PS and both abiotic and biotic stress adaptation in plants. Accumulating evidence indicates that LLPS plays a critical role in plant responses to abiotic stress, including heat, drought, cold, and salinity [50–53]. Through the formation of biomolecular condensates such as stress granules and processing bodies, LLPS enables the dynamic compartmentalization of RNA-binding proteins, mRNAs, and regulatory factors, thereby modulating translation, RNA stability, and proteostasis under stress conditions [50–54]. Guided by these findings, we examined Arabidopsis PSPs previously reported to function in stress responses (both abiotic and biotic stress) and identified their rice homologs in ricePSP. Strikingly, most of these rice homologs also exhibited high PS scores and MolPhase scores (Table 1), consistent with the presence of extensive IDRs or PrDs. These include stress protein early responsive to dehydration (ERD14), DEAD-box helicases (RH6/8/12), and acetylation lowers binding affinity proteins (ALBA), as well as homologs of well-characterized Arabidopsis PSPs such as ELF3, FCA, dehydrin, and RBGD, which have been shown to undergo LLPS in response to stress. The conservation of high LLPS potential across species suggests that PS-mediated stress regulation is an evolutionarily robust mechanism. Keyword tags such as “heat stress” and “abiotic stress” in ricePSP enable users to efficiently identify and explore these proteins, providing a powerful resource for studying LLPS-based stress adaptation in crops and guiding future efforts to enhance stress resilience through targeted manipulation of PSPs. ## Conclusions
  In summary, we undertake a global examination of PSPs in rice and construct a comprehensive database incorporating sequences, predicted PSPs and PrDPs, protein families, homologous proteins, and related literatures of the rice PSPs. The predicted PSPs could be invariably validated by protein structure data and in vivo condensate imaging assays. Importantly, ricePSP provides a versatile platform for investigating PS in rice and potentially other crop species. It enables functional exploration, prioritization, and hypothesis generation in the context of membraneless organelles and LLPS-mediated gene regulation, bridging computational predictions with future experimental discovery in plant biology. This resource opens exciting avenues for crop improvement by identifying LLPS-related proteins that could be targeted for enhancing stress resilience and developmental control in plants.
