# Molecular drivers of RNA phase separation


## Significance
  RNA molecules are central organizers of membraneless compartments that coordinate essential cellular processes. While many condensates form through interactions between RNA and RNA-binding proteins, recent discoveries show that specific RNA sequences can self-assemble into dense condensate structures without protein assistance. This study used atomistic simulations to investigate how sequence, temperature and ionic conditions affect RNA-driven condensate formation. We find that RNA condensates display base specific lower critical solution temperature which is largely due to multivalent interactions with magnesium ions. Moreover, we demonstrate that chemical modifications and ionic conditions can fine-tune the stability and dynamics of RNA condensates thereby facilitating a wide range of thermo-sensory capabilities which we suspect could underlie their biological functions.


## Results

### On the Role of Mono- and Divalent Ions in RNA Condensate Formation.
  Given divalent ions’ crucial role in driving RNA condensate formation, we first dissect the role of ions and ionic interactions on RNA phase separation. To this end, we have constructed three different systems of U4 under varying ionic conditions: i) neutralization with Na+ ions, ii) neutralization with Mg2+ ions, and iii) neutralization with Na+ ions combined with an additional 20 Mg2+ ions and 40 Cl−. We have also simulated these systems at different temperatures, quantifying cluster size (calculated as the cluster percentage, defined by the fraction of RNA molecules within the droplet), density, and hydration, which reports on the interplay of enthalpic and entropic forces. Systems with only Na+ ions at 270 K temperatures form more porous clusters with significant water content, indicating a percolation of RNA (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). As the temperature increases to 290 K, RNA clusters promptly dissolve, suggesting the presence of an upper critical solution temperature (UCST) (Fig. 1A). This narrow window of the phase separation indicates that RNA alone cannot sustain phase separation in the presence of only monovalent ions. However, increasing the length of RNA or introducing crowding agents can help RNA form condensates, as has been demonstrated in experiments (33–35).

  In divalent ion Mg2+-only systems, we have chosen a wider range of temperatures (270 K, 300 K, 330 K, and 390 K) to conduct simulations because of the larger coexistence window. Unlike monovalent systems, cluster size and density consistently increase with temperature (Fig. 1B). At 390 K, the RNA clusters become extremely large, dense, and compact, indicating a LCST for RNA in the presence of Mg2+ (Fig. 1B). At the lower temperature of 270 K, the cluster remains porous with considerable water content, again highlighting the percolated nature of RNA condensates (Fig. 1 B and D). A similar pattern is also observed when analyzing cluster size; as the temperature increases, one observes the growth of the cluster size (Fig. 1B).

  Finally, we created a system neutralized with both Na+ and 20 Mg2+ ions (65 mM), which more closely mimics cellular conditions where monovalent ions like Na+ and K+ are abundant (~100 to 150 mM), and divalent ions such as Mg2+ are present at lower concentrations. We conducted simulations at T = 290 K, 330 K, and 390 K. The results show that increasing temperature leads to a shrinking of cluster size and an increase in density (Fig. 1C). Adding a small amount of Mg2+ ions promoted RNA phase separation, highlighting the essential role of divalent ions in this process. These findings suggest that even minimal concentrations of Mg2+ can significantly impact RNA phase behavior, enhancing its capacity for forming condensates and stabilizing multivalent interactions at lower temperatures.

  Overall, in a system containing only Na+ ions, a percolated RNA cluster forms at lower temperatures but dissolves as the temperature increases. Conversely, in a system with only Mg2+ ions, the cluster persists and becomes larger and denser as the temperature rises (Fig. 1 A–C). This behavior can be attributed to two opposing forces: The entropic drive from increased temperature, which tends to dissolve the cluster, and the energetic interaction between Mg2+ ions and RNA, which promotes cluster formation. A mixed behavior emerges in a mixed system where Na+ ions neutralize the charge, and 20 additional Mg2+ ions (65 mM) are present. As the temperature increases, the cluster density rises while its size decreases (Fig. 1C). These observations suggest that the key factor behind these distinct behaviors is the interaction between Mg2+ ions and the phosphate groups of RNA, which intensifies at higher temperatures, driving cluster formation even as the entropic effects push toward dissolution. Additionally, we find that regardless of ion conditions, at lower temperatures (270 K), a significant amount of water becomes trapped within the RNA cluster due to percolation (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Figs. S11 and S12). As temperature increases, especially in the presence of Mg2+ ions, this water is expelled from the cluster, leading to an entropically favored condensed state. This effect can be quantified by examining the water Number density across the simulation box (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Figs. S11 and S12). Consistent with expectations, water density decreases toward the cluster’s center at all temperatures. Notably, the water density profile displays an irregular well at lower temperatures, whereas at higher temperatures, it sharpens into a distinct single well (Fig. 1D). To further support this observation, we have calculated the average number of water molecules around 3.5 Å of RNA across different temperatures. In all systems studied, the number of surrounding water molecules decreases with increasing temperature (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). While phase separation at higher temperatures appears entropically unfavorable for RNA alone, the expulsion of water molecules appears to compensate for this entropic loss. This interplay between water dynamics and RNA condensation highlights the critical role of entropic contributions in driving RNA phase separation.

  We next analyzed the ion distribution in all three systems along the box to understand how monovalent and divalent ions drive RNA condensation. The ionic atmosphere analysis shows that both monovalent and divalent ions were densely packed within the RNA clusters (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Figs. S14–S16), and notably, Mg2+ ion density increased with temperature (Fig. 2B).

  Ions interact with RNA in various ways, and the classification of ions around RNA is based on their mode of interaction with electronegative groups such as oxygen and nitrogen. These interactions can be categorized into three primary types: i) Inner-sphere ions; ions directly coordinate with RNA by forming bonds with electronegative groups, such as the phosphate backbone or bases of the nucleotide. A specific subset, known as chelated ions, establishes multiple coordinate bonds with RNA, displacing water molecules from their first hydration shell and forming strong interactions. ii) Outer sphere ions; these ions interact with RNA indirectly, remaining separated by a single hydration layer. While they do not form direct coordinate bonds with RNA, they still influence its electrostatic environment. iii) Diffuse ions; these ions exist further away from RNA in a highly dynamic state within the solution, contributing to the broader ionic atmosphere around the molecule (36, 37).

  When analyzing the ion interactions within the RNA clusters, specifically Mg2+ ions, we observed that outer-sphere Mg2+ ions were more prevalent at lower temperatures. However, as the temperature increased, there was a significant rise in the number of inner-sphere Mg2+ ions coordinating with one, two, or even three phosphate groups. By 390 K, the proportion of Mg2+ ions coordinating with phosphate groups had surpassed that of outer-sphere Mg2+ ions (Fig. 2C).

  This shift in ionic condensation further underscores the role of temperature in driving the coordination of Mg2+ with phosphate groups, which, in turn, influences the phase behavior of RNA, particularly its LCST in the presence of divalent ions. Increasing temperatures lead to the Mg2+ ions driving the water molecules out from the dehydrated shell around phosphate groups, resulting in more compact clusters. This process is particularly evident in the shift from outer-sphere Mg2+ ions, where Mg2+ is surrounded by water molecules, to inner-sphere Mg2+ ions, where Mg2+ directly interacts with the phosphate groups, often forming chelation with multiple phosphate groups (Fig. 2 C–G). A similar observation was observed in our previous work, where RNA and tetrapeptide of lysine showed reentrant transitions due to desolvation around the phosphate groups of RNA, with increased contacts between phosphate and lysine side chains (25).

  We next counted the inner- and outer-sphere Na+ ions in U4 systems containing both Na+ and Mg2+ ions. Unlike Mg2+ ions, however, the number of inner- and outer-sphere Na+ ions remained unchanged with increasing temperature (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). As expected, inner-sphere interactions consistently dominated at all three temperatures, as Na+ is more readily chelated by RNA atoms due to the lower energy barrier for water removal from its first solvation shell. This contrast further reinforces the temperature-dependent chelation of Mg2+ with RNA phosphate groups as a key factor driving the LCST behavior of RNA. In sum, we have quantified the release of hydration water and the redistribution of ions around RNA with increasing temperature, thereby providing although indirect but compelling evidence that the origin of LCST behavior is entropic. Specifically, the transition from outer- to inner-sphere Mg2+ coordination displaces water molecules from the phosphate hydration shell, while simultaneously reorganizing the ion atmosphere. Both processes contribute substantial translational and rotational entropy gains to the surrounding solvent–ion system, which compensate for the configurational entropy loss of RNA chains in the condensed state. The fact that cation-induced hydration effects can cause LCST behavior has been demonstrated for protein solutions (38, 39). Our quantification of water release and ion redistribution during RNA condensation shows all the same hallmarks of LCST behavior, thereby providing strong evidence that LCST behavior in RNA condensates originates from entropic contributions. This interpretation is consistent with classic thermodynamic cycle-based models of ion release and hydration entropy in RNA (40) and aligns with recent work emphasizing entropy-driven LCST condensation in RNA-containing condensates (24, 27).


### On the Role of Base-Specific Interactions On RNA Phase-Separation.
  To disentangle the impact of distinct base chemistry on phase-separation affinities, we conducted phase coexistence simulations for four separate systems, each containing 50 chains (approximately 160 mg/mL) of one type of tetranucleotide: guanine (G4), adenine (A4), cytosine (C4), and uracil (U4). Na+ ions were included in each system for charge neutralization, along with 20 additional Mg2+ ions (65 mM). This ion setup mimics cellular conditions while minimizing phosphate chelation through limited Mg2+ ions, enhancing the differentiation in phase separation behavior driven by the bases. These simulations were conducted at two temperatures, 330 K and 390 K, to examine temperature-dependent effects. Our findings reveal that the cluster size (quantified as the cluster percentage, defined by the fraction of RNA molecules within the droplet) and characteristics of the formed clusters strongly depend on the RNA bases.

  At T = 330 K, Guanine and Adenine form larger clusters, with most nucleotides in the cluster, than Cytosine and Uracil, which form comparable cluster sizes. The cluster size order follows G4 ~ A4 > C4 ~ U4 (Fig. 3C). As temperature rises to 390 K, Uracil’s cluster size decreases faster, and cluster size follows: G4 > A4> C4 > U4 (Fig. 3C). In all bases, cluster size decreases with temperature rises while cluster density increases (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S18). At both temperatures, 330 K and 390 K, the condensate state density of all bases is approximately the same. This is because Mg2+ ions play a central role in condensate formation through chelation with the phosphate group. When comparing the condensate slab width, it follows G4 > A4 > C4 > U4 in both temperatures (Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Also, the vapor phase density in both temperatures shows that guanine and adenine exhibit lower cluster densities than cytosine and uracil. Therefore, the overall condensation ability is G4 > A4 > C4 > U4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).

  We calculated microscopic interactions, including hydrogen bonding and pi-stacking to delve deeper into the base-dependent phase separations. At both temperatures, the trend for hydrogen bonds strictly follows G4 > A4> C4 > U4 (Fig. 3D). Similarly, pi-stacking follows the same trend. At lower temperatures T = 330 K, G4, A4, and C4 form a larger number pi-stacking interactions than Uracil (Fig. 3E). Guanine forms the most extensive hydrogen bonds and pi-stacking interactions. In terms of base pairing, at 330 K it follows A4 > G4 > U4 > C4, and at 390 K, the trend is G4 > A4 > C4 > U4. (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Notably, guanine and adenine exhibit a higher propensity for base pairing than cytosine and uracil. Overall, the interaction strength across all microscopic parameters follows the trend: G4 > A4 > C4 > U4 aligns well with the observed condensation tendencies of these polynucleotides, where guanine-rich sequences display the strongest phase separation behavior, followed by adenine, cytosine, and uracil.

  We divided each nucleotide into its phosphate, sugar, and base groups to further investigate the specific interactions driving RNA phase separation. We calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) for each pair. This analysis reveals that guanine exhibits the strongest phosphate–base interactions, followed by cytosine, owing to the presence of the NH2 group in the base. The phosphate–base interaction strength follows the order: G4 > C4 > A4 > U4 in both temperatures 330 K and 390 K (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Adenine prefers to form strong base–base interactions through the NH2 group in its base, as reflected in its RDF, with the base–base interaction strength following the order of G4 > A4 > C4 > U4 (Fig. 4 B and E). Guanine’s ability to form strong phosphate–base and base–base interactions explains its enhanced phase separation potential, whereas uracil, lacking precise specific interactions, exhibits a weaker tendency to phase separate.

  When examining the temperature dependence, in all four bases, phosphate–base interactions increase with rising temperature, while base–base interactions decrease (Fig. 4 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). This observation underscores the role of phosphate in driving the LCST behavior. Finally, we analyzed the interactions of Na+ ions around the phosphate and base groups of RNA. Across all four bases, the Na+ distribution around the phosphate groups increased with rising temperature. Around the RNA base, Na+ distribution also increased with temperature in all cases except adenine (SI Appendix, Figs. S21 and S22). Interestingly, the interaction of Na+ with the adenine base decreases as the temperature rises (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). At both temperatures, the distribution of Na+ around both the phosphate and base groups followed the trend: G4 > C4 ~ U4 > A4. This suggests that adenine interacts less with ions than other nucleotides due to its base’s absence of a C=O group.


### The 2’- OH Group of Ribose Sugar Enhances RNA Phase-Separation.
  Beyond base-specific interactions, we closely examined the role of ribose sugar in RNA phase separation by comparing A4 RNA with its single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) counterpart, dA4. The only structural distinction between these two nucleotides is the presence of a 2’-OH group in RNA (Fig. 5A). Recent studies have highlighted that atomic-level differences between ssDNA and RNA of the same sequence significantly impact their interactions with proteins, leading to the formation of multiphase droplets containing distinct RNA- and DNA-rich phases (41). Furthermore, experimental findings suggest that the 2’-OH group lowers the LCST of RNA in the presence of Mg2+ ions (42). To gain deeper atomic-level insights, we performed slab simulations with 50 chains of dA4 at a concentration of ~160 mg/mL. Na+ ions were included for charge neutralization, along with 20 additional Mg2+ ions (65 mM). Simulations were conducted at two temperatures, 330 K and 390 K, and the results were directly compared to those of A4 RNA under identical conditions.

  We calculated the cluster density and size and compared it with A4. We find that at lower temperature 330 K, dA4 formed smaller and less dense clusters than A4 (Fig. 5 C–E). However, as the temperature increased to 390 K, both dA4 and A4 exhibited similar clustering behavior. This suggests that the presence of ribose sugar indeed enhances phase separation at lower temperatures, effectively lowering the LCST, in agreement with experimental observations.

  To further investigate the microscopic interactions underlying these differences, we analyzed hydrogen bonding and ion interactions. The 2’-OH group in A4 facilitates a significantly greater number of hydrogen bonds compared to dA4, as shown in Fig. 5F. At both 330 K and 390 K, RNA consistently forms more hydrogen bonds than DNA, while the extent of π-stacking remains comparable between A4 and dA4. This is expected, as π-stacking is largely unaffected by the presence of the 2’-OH group (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S23). To better understand the role of the 2’-OH group, we dissected each nucleotide into its sugar and nonsugar components and computed hydrogen bonding between these regions. In RNA, we observed a significant number of hydrogen bonds between the sugar and nonsugar parts, whereas in DNA, such interactions were virtually absent. Importantly, hydrogen bonding between nonsugar regions remained similar in both RNA and DNA. Representative snapshots of inter- and intramolecular hydrogen bonding are shown in Fig. 5H and SI Appendix, Fig. S23. Notably, the 2’-OH group in RNA can form extensive intramolecular hydrogen bonds, contributing to the increased structural rigidity of RNA relative to DNA.

  Additionally, ion interactions differ between dA4 and A4. The 2’-OH group enables A4 to interact with outer-sphere Mg2+, leading to a greater condensation of Mg2+ around A4 compared to dA4 (Fig. 5 G and H). Likewise, Na+ ions exhibit a higher degree of condensation around A4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S23). This trend is consistent with previous studies on RNA and DNA duplexes, where RNA was found to capture more cations than DNA (43). Overall, we conclude that the 2’-OH group in RNA enhances its phase separation by promoting increased hydrogen bonding and stronger ion condensation, effectively lowering the LCST.


### Effects of Adenine Methylation on RNA LLPS.
  The cellular environment is a highly dynamic and chemically active space where proteins and RNA undergo various posttranslational and posttranscriptional modifications, such as phosphorylation, methylation, and acetylation (44, 45). These chemical modifications shape biomolecules’ behavior, dynamics, and phase separation properties. In this study, we have extensively explored the effects of ions and base chemistry on RNA’s ability to undergo condensation and phase separation. Equally important, however, is understanding how chemical modifications on RNA impact these processes. RNA can undergo several types of methylation, with N6-methyladenine (m6A) being one of the most common modifications involved in various cellular processes (Fig. 6 A and B) (46, 47).

  To investigate the effects of N6-methylation on RNA interactions and its role in liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), we performed slab simulations containing 50 chains of m6A-modified RNA tetramers (m6A4, approximately 160 mg/mL) in the presence of Na+ ions for charge neutralization with 20 additional Mg2+ ions (65 mM) at two different temperatures: 330 K and 390 K. To assess the impact of N6-methylation on RNA condensation, we calculated the cluster density and size and compared it with A4. We find that m6A4 forms smaller clusters at both temperatures than A4 (Fig. 6D). Additionally, across both temperature conditions, the clusters formed by m6A4 are less dense than those formed by A4 (Fig. 6 E and F).

  These findings suggest that the m6A modification suppresses phase separation, reducing the overall condensation ability of RNA. We further examined microscopic interactions: hydrogen bonding, pi-stacking, and base pairing. Our results reveal that the m6A modification reduces overall hydrogen bonding more than A4 at 330 K and 390 K. Similarly, m6A4 exhibits lesser pi-stacking interactions and fewer hydrogen bonds than A4. (Fig. 6 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S24). It explains that substituting NH2 with NHCH3 in m6A hinders the hydrogen bonding and Pi-stacking interaction ability, suppressing RNA condensates’ stability.


## Discussion
  Protein and RNA-containing condensates have been shown to play key roles in transcriptional regulation, stress response, and sensing functions (33, 48–50). Recent experiments have further demonstrated that RNA molecules can form condensates without protein assistance (27). Moreover, the strong coupling between RNA conformations and the ionic environment can give rise to LCST behaviour which is tunable by sequence and posttranslational modifications. To gain a deeper understanding of the molecular forces that underlay condensate formation in pure RNA systems, we employed atomistic simulations of RNA tetranucleotides and their chemically modified analogs. Through systematic variation of sequence and external environment we shed light on emergent structures, hydration, and ion binding profiles that underly the thermal stability and LCST behaviour of RNA condensates.

  We find that in Na+ containing solutions without any divalent cations, RNA clusters readily dissolve upon heating. Meanwhile, in the presence of Mg2+, one observes LCST behaviour where RNA cluster size and density initially increase with increasing temperature followed by a slow dissolution. This behavior stems from the coordinated dehydration of phosphate groups and Mg2+ chelation with RNA, which reorganize hydrogen-bonding networks and release bound water into the bulk. The release of large number of waters quantified in our simulations provides strong evidence for the entropic origin of LCST behavior. It is remarkable that our simulations recapitulate the experimental observation of RNA-driven LCST behaviour and align with previous computational studies of single RNA chains using thermodynamic cycles (40) and enhanced sampling (28). This consistency indicates that, despite neglecting polarizability and specific coordination effects of multivalent ions, the classical force field is still able to capture the essential physical interactions underlying RNA LCST behaviour (51–53).

  We have explored how the chemical nature of the nucleobases affects the stability of RNA condensates/clusters by simulating solutions containing guanine (G4), adenine (A4), cytosine (C4), and uracil (U4) in the presence of Na+ for neutralization and additional Mg2+ ions (65 mM). Our findings align with experiments (27), showing that the overall condensation ability follows the order G4 > A4 > C4 > U4.

  By carrying out a detailed analysis of hydrogen bonding and π-stacking interactions in RNA condensates, we found that the strength of all these interactions follows the same thermal stability order G4 > A4 > C4 > U4. Guanine exhibits the strongest phosphate–base interactions, due to the NH2 group on its base. Cytosine follows closely, while adenine favors strong base–base interactions through its NH2 group. These insights align with previous computational studies on 20-polynucleotide sequence repeats (28), which showed that strong pi-stacking interactions combined with hydrogen bonds between bases and phosphates can cause well-organized stems or hairpin loop structures in G4. In A4, base pairing dominates, driving the formation of stem and pseudoknot structures, while in C4, hydrogen bonding between the NH2 groups of the base and phosphate is prominent. In contrast, U4 shows minimal interactions, resulting in a highly mobile structure. Interestingly, we observe that phosphate–base interactions increase with increasing temperature. On the other hand, base–base interactions decrease with temperature, highlighting the key role of phosphates in driving the LCST behavior. Additionally, as expected, adenine interacts less with ions than other nucleotides due to the absence of a C = O group.

  To illuminate the role of ribose sugar in RNA phase separation, we have done additional simulations to compare A4 RNA with its single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) counterpart, dA4. We find that the 2’-OH group in RNA enhances phase separation by facilitating increased hydrogen bonding and stronger ion condensation, thereby effectively lowering the LCST threshold. We have also examined the effect of posttranslational modifications on RNA phase separation, focusing on N6-methyladenine (m6A), a common RNA modification. Simulations of m6A-modified RNA tetramers (m6A4) at lower and higher temperatures showed that substituting the NH2 group with NHCH3 in m6A reduces hydrogen bonding potential of RNAs, thereby also weakening RNA clusters. This suggests that m6A modification suppresses phase separation compared to unmodified adenine. Interestingly, experimental studies show that while m6A modifications reduce RNA–RNA contacts they can enhance RNA–protein interactions (54). For example, m6A-binding proteins YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3 all undergo LLPS in vitro and in cells, with phase separation significantly amplified by mRNAs containing multiple m6A nucleotides (54–57).

  Finally, while we have used fixed RNA length in this study, it is important to note that RNA chain length can modulate conformational entropy and promote more stable condensed phases, particularly in homotypic RNA in the presence of multivalent cations such as Mg2+(27). Molecular simulations using intermediate-resolution coarse-grained models have indeed revealed length-dependent LCST behavior and recapitulated how RNA sequence, length, Mg2+ concentration, and temperature collectively influence phase transitions (26).


## Methods

### Atomistic Molecular Dynamic Simulations.
  We first generated A-form helical structures of single-stranded RNA - G4, A4, C4, U4 - as well as single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) - A4 tetranucleotide chains, using the X3DNA library (58, 59). For the m6A4 system, a methyl group was added to the adenine bases using PyMOL. Fifty tetranucleotide chains were then tightly packed within a cubic box of 4.5 nm. This cubic box was subsequently extended into a slab with 4.5 × 4.5 × 25 nm (approximately ~160 mg/mL density). Depending on the specific system, ions (Na+, Cl−, and Mg2+) and water molecules were added to fill the box while maintaining system charge neutrality. Using the conjugate-gradient algorithm, the solvated box containing RNA, ions, and water was subjected to energy minimization. The Amber RNA force field parameters (parmBSC0χOL3) (51–53) and the OPC water model were employed (60). For the Mg2+ ions, we used optimized parameters specifically designed for the OPC water model (61), known as microMg, which exhibits a microsecond water exchange rate consistent with experimental observations. The Na+ and Cl− parameters were taken from Young and Cheatham (62).

  Following the energy minimization, we have performed a 4 ns NVT simulation followed by a 4 ns NPT simulation at a temperature of 300 K using the Langevin Middle integrator with a time step of 2 fs (63). Finally, NPT production runs were performed for 3 to 6 microseconds, depending on the system. A Monte Carlo barostat, as implemented in OpenMM, was used to maintain an average pressure of 1 bar across all systems. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three spatial dimensions.

  Tetranucleotides are the shortest RNA fragments that retain sequence-specific base stacking and hydrogen bonding patterns, making them ideal for probing sequence-dependent interactions while avoiding the excessive conformational complexity and entanglement seen in longer strands. Their limited length enhances conformational entropy, which helps distinguish the contributions of individual bases to phase behavior. Additionally, the density of systems was chosen to be approximately 160 mg/mL pragmatically, which is well below the typical condensate phase density (~500 mg/mL) but significantly higher than the solution phase density used in the experiments (~1.5 mg/mL), thereby facilitating the formation of the stable cluster in a reasonable time.


### Analysis.
  Simulations were run until convergence was achieved, and all analyses were performed on equilibrated trajectory, which was assessed by bootstrapping on the time series of cluster sizes using the last two microsecond simulation trajectories (SI Appendix, Figs. S1–S7). For the A4 systems with Na+ and Mg2+, we performed three independent replicas at two temperatures 330 K and 390 K, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For the rest of the systems, we randomly chose distinct nonoverlapping frames of 200 ns. The consistency in cluster sizes across these replicas supports the convergence of our results. Additionally, we have calculated the radial distribution function (RDF) between RNA and Na+ ions at regular intervals across the replicas to further substantiate convergence (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) and Time evolution of number of inner Na+ and Mg2+ ions is showing convergence shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S8.

  The error bars presented in our analysis were computed using a bootstrapping approach. Specifically, we used the final 2 microseconds of each equilibrated simulation trajectory and randomly selected four nonoverlapping 200 ns segments from this window. For each of these segments, we calculated the relevant property and then derived the mean and SD across the four segments to obtain the average value and the associated error bar.

  To analyze cluster size, quantified as the cluster percentage, defined by the fraction of RNA molecules within the droplet. We employed the DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise) to extract the clusters in a frame-by-frame analysis to analyze the size. DBSCAN relies on two key parameters: eps, the maximum distance between two points to be considered neighbors, and min_samples, the minimum number of neighboring points required for a point to be classified as a core point. We found that setting eps = 12 Å and min_samples = 2 provided the most effective separation between the condensed and dilute phases.

  A detailed contact analysis was also performed using ProLIF (64) to investigate the linkages between the cluster characteristics and the various interactions (hydrophobic, electrostatic, and water-bridged interactions) responsible for cluster formation and maintenance. ProLIF classify atomic-level contacts into nine distinct types: hydrogen bond donor, hydrogen bond acceptor, hydrophobic, Pi-stacking (contains edge-to-face π-stacking, face-to-face π-stacking). Further computational details are provided in the SI Appendix.
