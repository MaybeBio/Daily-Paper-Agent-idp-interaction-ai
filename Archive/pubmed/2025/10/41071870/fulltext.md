# Mechanism of USP21 autoinhibition and histone H2AK119 deubiquitination


## Abstract
  Monoubiquitinated histone H2A lysine 119 (H2AK119ub) is a modification associated with transcriptional silencing and heterochromatin formation. Ubiquitin-specific protease 21 (USP21), one of four major H2AK119-specific deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), plays critical roles in diverse cellular processes. However, the mechanisms by which USP21 specifically deubiquitinates H2AK119ub and is regulated are unknown. We determined the cryo-EM structure of the USP21 catalytic domain bound to an H2AK119ub nucleosome, which revealed a recognition mode that differs from that of other H2AK119-specific DUBs. We unexpectedly found that the N-terminal IDR of USP21 inhibits the enzyme’s activity. Using AlphaFold-Multimer to perform a virtual screen of USP21 interactors, we identified kinases that phosphorylate the USP21 IDR and thereby relieve autoinhibition. AlphaFold3 modeling of USP21 suggests a structural model for autoinhibition. AlphaFold analysis suggests that phosphorylation-regulated autoinhibition may be a feature of various USP enzymes. These findings shed light on the mechanisms of H2AK119 deubiquitination and reveal a previously unexplored mode of phosphorylation-dependent DUB autoregulation.


## INTRODUCTION
  Reversible posttranslational modification of histone proteins plays a central role in regulating eukaryotic gene transcription. The monoubiquitination of histone H2A K119 by the Polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) is a defining modification associated with transcription silencing in multicellular organisms (K119 in vertebrates, K118 in Drosophila, and K121 in Arabidopsis) (1, 2). In humans, deubiquitination of H2AK119 is mediated by four deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs): breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (BRCA1)-associated protein 1 (BAP1) (3), Myb Like, SWIRM and MPN domain-containing protein 1 (MYSM1) (4), ubiquitin-specific protease 16 (USP16) (5), and USP21 (6). Each H2AK119-specific DUB regulates distinct cellular processes and is enzymatically regulated differently. BAP1 is classified as a ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase (UCH), whereas USP16 and USP21 are ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs). Although all three are cysteine proteases, they adopt structurally distinct folds characteristic of their respective families. By contrast, MYSM1 is a zinc-dependent metalloprotease that is a member of the Jab1/Mov34/MPN+ (JAMM) metalloenzyme family. Whereas MYSM1, USP16, and USP21 function as single-subunit DUBs, BAP1 heterodimerizes with ASXL1/2/3 to form the active Polycomb repressive deubiquitinase (PR-DUB) complex (3). In addition to targeting H2AK119, all four DUBs can disassemble ubiquitin chains on other substrates (7–10).

  USP21 facilitates the activation of genes during transcriptional reprogramming by deubiquitinating H2AK119ub (11). The deubiquitination of histone H2AK119ub by USP21 promotes transcription activation through trans-histone cross-talk in hepatocytes by promoting H3K4 methylation by the Mixed-Lineage Leukemia 1 (MLL1) complex and concomitant activation of genes associated with hepatocyte regeneration (6). In addition to its role in deubiquitinating H2AK119, USP21 disassembles K48-linked ubiquitin chains on absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) (12), breast cancer type 2 susceptibility protein (BRCA2) (13), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 2 (MEK2) (14), and Nanog (15), thereby rescuing these proteins from proteasomal degradation. USP21 also disassembles K63-linked ubiquitin chains on receptor-interacting protein 1 (RIP-1) to inhibit nuclear factor κB signaling (16) and K27/K63-ubiquitin linkages on stimulator of interferon genes (STING) to down-regulate innate immune response to viral DNA (17). USP21’s activity on multiple substrates allows it to play a central role in an array of cellular processes, ranging from inflammasome activation to stem cell pluripotency.

  Previous structural studies of USP21 focused on complexes of the C-terminal USP family catalytic domain bound to di-ubiquitin (18) or an inhibitor (19), but the manner by which USP21 recognizes its ubiquitinated nucleosomal substrate was unknown. USP21 has a ~200 residue N-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR) containing a nuclear export sequence (20, 21), but the role of the IDR, if any, in modulating USP21 enzymatic activity was unknown. We report here the structure of the USP21 catalytic domain bound to a H2AK119-ubiquitinated nucleosome. The structure reveals that USP21 simultaneously recognizes the nucleosome acidic patch and nucleosomal DNA. Structural analysis of cancer mutations in the USP21 catalytic domain provides mechanistic insight into the effect of missense mutations on USP21 stability and deubiquitinase activity. In solution-based experiments with the intact USP21 protein, we unexpectedly found that the N-terminal IDR autoinhibits USP21 activity. Modeling with AlphaFold3 suggested that the IDR may occlude the ubiquitin-binding pocket of USP21, potentially inhibiting enzymatic activity. By performing an in silico screen of 152 known USP21 interactors using AlphaFold-Multimer, we identified several kinases predicted to interact with IDR residues that were known USP21 phosphorylation sites. Biochemical assays confirmed that the phosphorylation of residues in the USP21 IDR by microtubule affinity-regulating kinase (MARK1) and protein kinase C iota (PRKCI) relieves autoinhibition. The analysis of AlphaFold3 models of other DUBs suggest that this autoinhibition mechanism may be present in other USP family members, potentially explaining how these enzymes may be regulated.


## RESULTS

### Structural overview of USP21 bound to a H2AK119-ubiquitinated nucleosome
  To learn how USP21 specifically deubiquitinates H2AK119ub, we determined the cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the human USP21 catalytic domain (196 to 565) bound to a nucleosome containing ubiquitin linked to H2A K119 via a nonhydrolyzable dichloroacetone (DCA) linkage (Fig. 1, A and B) (22). Because of the high affinity of the complex for the carbon support, we collected two datasets on sample prepared on graphene oxide grids to obtain a sufficient number of particles. The final map had an overall resolution of 2.98 Å. Although the density for USP21 and ubiquitin was resolved at a low resolution in the global refined map (~7 to 8 Å), we were able to improve this region to a resolution of 5.33 Å using focused refinement (figs. S1 and S2). Regions of the USP21 catalytic domain that were unresolved in previously reported crystal structures remained ambiguous in our maps (residues 196 to 202, 320 to 349, and 559 to 565) (18, 19), indicating conformational plasticity of these regions. Nonetheless, we were able to unambiguously position the crystal structures of USP21 and ubiquitin into the map and model interactions between USP21 and the nucleosome (Fig. 2, A to C).

  The structure shows that the H2A C terminus undergoes a conformational change to enable the ubiquitin conjugated to K119 to bind USP21. Ubiquitin binds to the S1 site of the enzyme in a manner virtually identical to that seen in the crystal structure of USP21 bound to ubiquitin aldehyde (fig. S3) (18, 19). Ubiquitin residues through R74 were resolved, whereas there was no clear density defining the two C-terminal ubiquitin residues, G75 and G76. We note that the corresponding regions of histone H2A and ubiquitin were not well-resolved in the structure of USP16 and one of the two PR-DUB structures (23–25). In addition to the density for ubiquitin, we identified a short stretch of residues at the C terminus of histone H2A (residues 109 to 113) that is oriented toward the active site of USP21 (Fig. 1C). The conformation of these H2A residues differs markedly from that in the crystal structure of an unmodified nucleosome [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 1KX5] (26), which shows the C-terminal tail of histone H2A through residue K128 lying flat on the surface of the histone octamer near the nucleosome dyad (Fig. 1C). Model building shows that the conformation of H2A in the unmodified nucleosome would not allow a ubiquitin linked to K119 to bind to USP21. Thus, for USP21 to engage ubiquitin that is covalently linked to K119, histone H2A must undergo a conformational rearrangement, its tail lifting off the histone octamer surface. This positions S113 to be 21 Å away from the USP21 catalytic cysteine (C221), while ubiquitin R74 is placed 12 Å away from C221 of USP21 (Fig. 1D). The positioning of the last visible residues of histone H2A and ubiquitin is consistent with the presence of an isopeptide linkage between H2AK119 and ubiquitin within the active site of USP21. The observed conformational change in H2A parallels similar observations in the PR-DUB complex bound to H2AK119ub, where an even larger conformational change of the H2A C terminus is required for BAP1 to engage H2AK119ub (24).


### USP21 interacts with the nucleosome acidic patch and DNA
  USP21 is anchored to the nucleosome by a pair of basic residues, R470 and K474, that interact with the conserved nucleosome acidic patch. R470 serves as a canonical arginine anchor (Fig. 2B), as has been observed for many chromatin-binding factors that engage the nucleosome acidic patch (27, 28). Specifically, R470 is poised to interact with both H2A E61 and H2B E102 of the acidic patch, as we were able to observe strong connecting density between the three residues (Fig. 2B). In addition, we identified density for the K474 side chain and modeled it to interact with H2A E64, for which there is partial side chain density. To test the importance of the acidic patch contacts for deubiquitination of H2AK119ub, we assayed the activity of single and double charge-reversal mutants of USP21 residues R470 and K474 on nucleosomes containing ubiquitin conjugated to H2AK119 via a native isopeptide linkage. As shown in Fig. 2D, both single and double substitutions of R470 and K474 abolished the activity of USP21 on H2AK119ub. To rule out the possibility that these mutations disrupt the overall catalytic activity of USP21, we tested the effects of R470E, K474D, and R470E/K474D substitutions on USP21 cleavage of K48-linked di-ubiquitin (Fig. 2E). All USP21 substitutions at residues implicated in binding the nucleosome acidic patch had activity similar to that of the wild-type enzyme in this assay, indicating that R470 and K474 are required for interactions with the nucleosome, but not for overall enzymatic activity.

  In addition to the interaction of USP21 with the nucleosome acidic patch, there is density bridging USP21 and nucleosomal DNA at super-helical location (SHL) 6.5, suggesting that there is at least one point of contact between USP21 and the negatively charged sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA (Fig. 2C). We modeled the connecting density as a contact between R536 and the DNA backbone and used model building to identify R215 as an additional potential point of contact. We tested the activity of charge-reversal mutants of R215 and R536 and found that both single and double substitutions at these residues abrogated USP21 activity on H2AK119ub nucleosomes (Fig. 2F). To test whether these mutations specifically affected USP21 activity on H2AK119ub, we assayed the ability of the mutant proteins to cleave K48-linked di-ubiquitin (Fig. 2G). We found that, while R215E displayed activity similar to the wild-type protein, both R536E and R215E/R536E had reduced activity on di-ubiquitin, although the decrease in activity is modest as compared to the effects of these mutations on deubiquitinating H2AK119ub. Further inspection of R536 revealed that this residue is in close proximity to the USP21 catalytic site, forming direct contacts with N533 (fig. S4). N533 is positioned to form direct contacts with H518, one of the catalytic triad residues of USP21. We speculate that the R536E mutation could form more favorable interactions with N533, potentially repositioning N533 and thereby altering its interaction with H518 in a way that reduces catalytic activity.

  In profiled samples from patients with cancer, USP21 is altered via amplification, mutation, or deletion in ~5% of all samples, a higher prevalence than other established cancer-driving DUBs such as USP1 (2%) and BAP1 (3%), and tumor suppressors such as BRCA1 (3%) (fig. S5). At present, all USP21 mutations are identified as variants of unknown significance. The structure of USP21 bound to an ubiquitinated nucleosome provides insights into the effects of cancer-associated mutations in the catalytic domain that would be predicted to disrupt activity by interfering with substrate binding and not simply inactivating the enzyme by destabilizing the fold of the catalytic domain. Of the 373 USP21 mutations found in the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) database and visualized in ProteinPaint (fig. S6) (29, 30), 100 are located within the catalytic domain of USP21 (residues 212 to 565) (Fig. 3A). Of these, Q300H, S391F, E395K, and Y519C are predicted to disrupt polar contacts with ubiquitin (Fig. 3A). Mutations R470Q, G512F, or V514F would disrupt interactions with the nucleosome acidic patch or cause a steric clash with the H2B C-terminal helix, while R215Q and R536C/L would abolish interaction of USP21 with the nucleosome DNA (Fig. 3A). We identified E276K as a mutation that could potentially improve H2AK119ub recognition by enhancing USP21’s affinity for the nucleosome DNA.


### Comparison of USP21 binding with other H2AK119-specific DUBs
  Despite their common substrate, the three H2AK119-specific enzymes—USP21, USP16, and the PR-DUB complex (23–25)—have divergent modes of binding their ubiquitinated nucleosome substrate. In each structure, ubiquitin is in a different position, and the enzymes are positioned in a different manner on the nucleosome (Fig. 3B). The differences are particularly notable in comparing USP21 and USP16, as both enzymes contain a conserved USP catalytic domain, whereas the BAP1 catalytic domain belongs to the ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase (UCH) family. Despite belonging to different fold families, USP21 and BAP1 bind the nucleosome acidic patch with a canonical arginine anchor (27), whereas USP16 does not contact the acidic patch. In addition, although all three DUBs contact the DNA at SHL 6.5, both PR-DUB and USP16 have a second contact point at SHL 0 that is absent in the USP21 complex.

  To better understand the structural basis for differences between USP16 and USP21 recognition of H2AK119ub despite the similarity in their catalytic domains, we examined the potential contacts that could be formed by USP21 and USP16 in each of the complexes with H2AK119ub nucleosomes. A sequence alignment of USP21 and USP16 reveals limited sequence similarity between the two DUBs (28.3%), including the nucleosome-binding contacts of each DUB (fig. S7). Whereas USP16 uses basic residues to interact with DNA at SHL 0 and 6.5 and with the C-terminal helix of histone H2B, USP21 does not contain any basic residues at the corresponding positions. In addition, a structured region in USP16 that mediates binding to the C-terminal helix of H2B is not conserved in USP21 and the corresponding residues are disordered (residues 250 to 319) (fig. S8, A and B). We note that these residues were unresolved in our maps and in the reported crystal structures (18, 19). Conversely, USP16 lacks key basic residues that position USP21 on H2AK119ub nucleosomes, including the arginine anchor and arginine residues that contact DNA in the USP21 complex (fig. S8, C and D). These differences in nucleosome engagement highlight how divergence in both structure and sequence contributes to distinct substrate recognition mechanisms.


### USP21 is autoinhibited by its N-terminal disordered region
  In preparing USP21 protein for structural studies, we optimized the purification of USP21 expressed from bacteria, as a previous study had found full-length USP21 to be unstable (see Materials and Methods) (18). We expressed and purified full-length USP21 (residues 1 to 565) and a series of fragments containing successive N-terminal truncations of the IDR (Fig. 4A). Activity assays of the minimal catalytic domain (196 to 565) using the ubiquitin-aminomethylcoumarin (Ub-AMC) fluorescent reporter showed robust cleavage activity, as has been previously reported (18). However, we unexpectedly found that the full-length enzyme had virtually no activity in these assays (Fig. 4B). N-terminal truncation mutants lacking residues 1 to 47 or 1 to 121 of the IDR similarly had little to no deubiquitinating activity, whereas further truncations removing residues 1 to 158 and 1 to 184 of the IDR had 53 and 77% of the activity of the USP21 catalytic domain, respectively (Fig. 4B). To verify that the observed autoinhibition was not specific to the Ub-AMC assay, we tested the ability of the USP21 constructs to cleave K48-linked di-ubiquitin chains, which had been previously reported as a USP21 substrate (12–15). As shown in Fig. 4C, the full-length protein had no deubiquitinating activity in this assay, whereas successive deletions showed increasing activity, with maximum activity observed for the minimal catalytic domain.

  Although the full-length USP21 protein appeared to be well-behaved in solution, we wished to rule out the possibility that the full-length protein was inactive due to misfolding. We therefore engineered a full-length USP21 construct in which residues 190 to 195 were substituted to create a Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease cleavage site (USP21I-T-C) (fig. S9). This design made it possible to assay the activity of the same USP21 protein before and after cleavage with TEV protease to separate the IDR from the catalytic domain. As shown in Fig. 4 (D and E), preincubating USP21I-T-C with TEV protease led to a marked increase in activity as assayed by cleavage of Ub-AMC, indicating that the presence of the IDR inhibits USP21 activity (Fig. 4E). The USP21I-T-C protein exhibits somewhat higher activity than the native full-length protein, suggesting that the altered amino acid sequence in residues 190 to 195, which are immediately N-terminal to the catalytic domain, may in some way reduce the ability of the IDR to autoinhibit USP21.

  We also assessed binding and activity of full-length USP21 on H2AK119ub nucleosomes. Intact USP21 containing the full-length IDR showed no detectable binding of USP21 to H2AK119-ubiquitinated nucleosomes, whereas the catalytic domain alone bound to ubiquitinated nucleosomes with a Kd (dissociation constant) of around 0.6 μM (Fig. 4F). Similarly, full-length USP21protein showed only minimal activity in cleaving H2AK119 ubiquitinover a 1-hour time course, whereas the catalytic domain alone fully deubiquitinated nucleosomal histone H2A over the same period (Fig. 4G). Together, these experiments indicate that the USP21 N-terminal IDR inhibits the enzyme’s deubiquitinating activity in a substrate-independent manner.


### In silico pulldown identifies kinases and phospho-binding proteins as potential IDR interactors
  Since USP21 must be active to carry out its functions, we considered several possible mechanisms by which autoinhibition could be relieved. As has been found for other DUBs, autoinhibition can be regulated by oligomerization state (31–33), phosphorylation (34–36), or interaction with partner proteins (37, 38). Since full-length USP21 migrates as a monomer in size exclusion chromatography (fig. S10), we therefore speculated that USP21 may be regulated either by phosphorylation or by binding to partner proteins.

  To identify potential binding partners or enzymes that interact with the USP21 IDR, we used an in silico approach to rapidly screen for proteins that could potentially relieve autoinhibition of USP21. A total of 152 interacting partners of USP21 have been identified across multiple studies (39–43) and are tabulated in the BioGRID database (44). Using AlphaFold-Multimer (45) as implemented in the AlphaPulldown (46) pipeline, we predicted structures for all pairwise interactions between USP21 and its interactors (Fig. 5A). To identify proteins that interact with the USP21 IDR, we carried out pairwise predictions for all 152 interactors with the full-length USP21 protein (residues 1 to 565) and with the catalytic domain alone (residues 196 to 565). We reasoned that interactors that engage the USP21 IDR would have a higher interface predicted template modeling (ipTM) score for the USP211–565 model as compared to USP21196–565. We used this approach rather than searching for interactors predicted to bind the IDR alone because it was possible that some interactors could form multivalent interactions with both the USP21 IDR and catalytic domain. We generated five models for each interaction, yielding more than 1500 models, and used the ipTM score reported for the highest confidence model. To identify proteins predicted to interact with the USP21 IDR, we computed a ΔipTM score, which we define as the difference in ipTM between USP211–565 and USP21196–565 for a given interactor (table S3). Interactors with ipTM scores of less than 0.35 for both USP21 constructs were classified as low confidence due to the inability of AlphaFold to predict the interaction. Of the 152 interactors, 75 had low ipTM scores. An additional 57 interactors showed either no substantial difference (−0.15 < ΔipTM < 0.15), indicating that the IDR was not important for interactions with USP21, or had a negative ΔipTM score (ΔipTM < −0.15), indicating that the presence of the IDR worsened the prediction. The remaining 20 interactors had an improved prediction with the IDR (ΔipTM > 0.15), which we considered as candidate USP21 IDR interactors.

  To validate the utility of using ΔipTM as a metric for identifying proteins that bind to the USP21 IDR, we assessed whether known interactors were identified in our in silico screen. Previous work had shown that the nuclear export sequence of USP21 lies within the IDR (residues 134 to 152) (20). Exportin, one of the 20 known interactors, was predicted to form a canonical interaction with the USP21 nuclear export sequence (LXXXLXXLXL) with high confidence (fig. S11, A and B). Another known USP21 interactor identified in our screen was the MARK2 kinase (fig. S11, C and D). This enzyme had previously been shown to interact with IDR residues 48 to 121 (40) and was predicted by AlphaFold to bind within the same region of USP21. About half of the proteins predicted to interact with the USP21 IDR were either kinases, phosphatases, or 14-3-3 proteins, which bind phosphoserine and phosphothreonine (47), suggesting that the IDR may be regulated by phosphorylation (Fig. 5, B to E and fig. S11). Consistent with this hypothesis, the predicted complexes with the 14-3-3 isoforms showed S113 of USP21 bound to the canonical phosphoserine binding pocket of 14-3-3 proteins (Fig. 5C and fig. S11, E and F). An analysis of potential binding motifs with the 14-3-3 Pred server (48) showed USP21 residue S113 to be one of five predicted high-confidence 14-3-3 binding sites (fig. S12, A and B). Our predictions highlighted multiple kinases (MARK1, MARK2, MARK4, and PRKCI) poised to phosphorylate USP21 at S113 and S115 (Fig. 5, D and E and fig. S11, C and D), both of which had been shown to be highly phosphorylated in both human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (91.5 and 97.3%, respectively) and HeLa cells (57.9 and 82.5%, respectively) (40). The residues flanking S113 and S115 were analyzed using Kinase Library (49) to identify high-confidence phosphorylation sites for specific USP21 IDR interactors. For PRKCI (PKCI), S113 was ranked at the 98.4th percentile of substrates (fig. S12, C and D). For MARK1, S115 was ranked at the 98.5% percentile and was the top-ranked kinase for the motif (fig. S2, E and F). Together, our results suggest that autoinhibition of USP21 may be regulated by phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms.

  The IDR must in some way interfere with USP21 substrate binding or catalysis to inhibit deubiquitinating activity. To identify a potential self-interaction between the IDR and the catalytic domain, we predicted the structure of full-length of USP21 using AlphaFold3 (Fig. 6A) (50). Based on the predicted aligned error (PAE), we identified a high-confidence interaction involving residues 108 to 117 with the catalytic domain of USP21 (Fig. 6, B and C). This high-confidence interaction positions the IDR in the ubiquitin binding site in the USP21 catalytic domain. Notably, residues S113 and S115, which are known phosphorylation sites, are involved in the interaction. Based on this model, we hypothesized that the IDR autoinhibits USP21 by blocking access to the ubiquitin-binding interface and that this interaction could be disrupted by phosphorylation. We tested this hypothesis by using AlphaFold3 to predict the structure of USP21 with S113 and S115 phosphorylated and with a number of other known phosphorylation sites to see if phosphorylation of any residues resulted in a model in which the IDR no longer occluded the ubiquitin-binding site (fig. S13) (40). The only predictions that yielded a putative uninhibited model contained phosphorylated S93, S113, and S115 (Fig. 6, A and D, and figs. S13H and 14). Notably, these are residues previously identified as highly phosphorylated in HeLa and HEK293 cells (40).


### Phosphorylation by PRKC1 and MARK1 relieves USP21 autoinhibition
  Our in silico modeling suggested that phosphorylation of residues S93, S113, and S115 in the USP21 IDR could relieve autoinhibition of the enzyme and identified PRKC1 and MARK1 as the putative kinases. To confirm that S93, S113, and S115 were key residues mediating autoinhibition, we mutated S93, S113, and S115 to alanine (USP21-3A) and found that the mutant enzyme was highly active in cleaving Ub-AMC (Fig. 6E). To test whether phosphorylation relieved autoinhibition, we measured the activity of USP21 after treatment with human PRKCI, MARK1, or both (Fig. 6E). Incubation with each kinase increased USP21 cleavage activity on Ub-AMC while treating USP21 with both kinases resulted in the highest activity of the kinase-treated conditions. Similarly, USP21 treated with kinases had increased deubiquitination activity on H2AK119ub nucleosomes (Fig. 6F). To confirm that the activity increase was due to USP21 phosphorylation and not due to DUB contaminants present with PRKCI or MARK1, we incubated each kinase with Ub-AMC and observed no cleavage activity (fig. S15).

  To test the prediction that the IDR residues predicted by AlphaPulldown could be phosphorylated by PRKCI and MARK1, we incubated full-length USP21 with these enzymes and identified phosphorylation sites using mass spectrometry (MS) (Fig. 6G and table S4). We found that PRKCI was able to phosphorylate S113, while MARK1 phosphorylated S115, validating the predictions from AlphaFold-Multimer. In addition, PRKCI phosphorylated S93, suggesting that treating USP21 with both kinases could fully activate the protein. We speculate that the somewhat lower activity of phosphorylated USP21 as compared to the triple alanine mutant (Fig. 6E) may be due to incomplete phosphorylation of USP21. We note that we cannot rule out the possibility that there may be additional phosphorylation sites in the catalytic domain of USP21 that may negatively affect the activity, as has been observed for the phosphorylation of S538 in mouse USP21(15). Together, our findings support the model that S93, S113, and S115 are critical residues regulating USP21 autoinhibition and that phosphorylation of these residues activates the enzyme’s activity.


### Autoinhibitory IDR modules may be common across the USP family
  Autoregulation of DUBs by their IDRs may be a mechanism common to multiple members of the USP family. There are several DUBs in this structural class containing IDRs that are heavily phosphorylated and, in some cases, are known to be activated by phosphorylation (34, 35). PAE analysis of the top model of 51 USP DUBs in the AlphaFold2 database revealed multiple USP DUBS with IDRs predicted to contact the catalytic domain (Fig. 7 and table S5). Models for the candidate DUBs were predicted again using AlphaFold3 to generate five models of the DUB alone and with ubiquitin bound. We extracted high PAE contacts between the IDRs and catalytic domain in the apo structure, which we defined as contacts within 5 Å with a PAE cutoff of 20 Å found in at least two of the five models. All interactions that met this threshold were superimposed on the ubiquitin-bound structure to identify contiguous regions that would compete with ubiquitin binding and then these regions were analyzed to identify known phosphorylation sites. In total, 12 DUBs containing potential autoinhibitory regions were identified using this approach (Fig. 7B). Two of those identified, USP10 and USP37 (fig. S16), had previously been shown to be activated by phosphorylation of the IDR (34, 35). Because many classes of human enzymes contain IDRs, combining protein structure prediction tools with experimental data provides a rapid route to generating testable hypotheses regarding enzyme regulation.


## DISCUSSION
  Our structural study further highlights the plasticity in the recognition of nucleosomes by enzymes that remove ubiquitin from histone H2AK119. Although USP21 shares a common substrate with the previously studied enzymes, USP16 and PR-DUB, it differs from both in its interaction with nucleosomes and recognition of H2AK119ub. The finding that USP16 (23) and the PR-DUB complex (24, 25) differed from one another in the manner in which they bind to nucleosomes and recognize ubiquitin (Fig. 3A) was to be expected because USP16 belongs to the USP family of DUBs and PR-DUB belongs to the structurally distinct UCH family (51). Moreover, USP16 is a monomeric enzyme, whereas PR-DUB is a heterodimer comprising the BAP1 catalytic subunit and ASXL1, both of which mediate contacts with the nucleosome and with ubiquitin (24, 25). Although USP21 is, like USP16, a monomeric enzyme and a member of the USP family, the mechanism by which the two enzymes specifically recognize H2AK119ub is completely different. Apart from the conserved manner in which ubiquitin binds to the USP domain, the catalytic domains are docked on the nucleosome in entirely different orientations (Fig. 3A). Both catalytic domains contact the DNA but do so with different regions of the USP domain (fig. S8). Whereas USP21 contacts the nucleosome acidic patch, a hotspot for nucleosome interactions (27), USP16 does not. We note that the location of the K119 ubiquitination site in a conformationally flexible region of histone H2A makes it possible for proteins to bind the nucleosome in a range of orientations while simultaneously binding to the ubiquitin (fig. S17).

  Our finding that USP21 is autoinhibited by an N-terminal IDR and that this inhibition can be relieved by phosphorylation, highlight the power of combining protein structure prediction with curated experimental databases to uncover mechanisms of enzyme regulation. By using AlphaPulldown (46) to model complexes of experimentally identified USP21 interactors bound to the full-length enzyme and to a truncated version lacking the IDR, we were able to identify proteins predicted to interact with the IDR. This approach allowed us to identify phosphorylation as a likely mechanism for regulating autoinhibition by the IDR. We note that, whereas we demonstrate that phosphorylation of USP21 by PRKCI and MARK1 relieves autoinhibition in vitro, further work will be needed to confirm which kinases regulate USP21 in vivo. The ability to include phosphorylated residues in modeling as implemented in AlphaFold3 (50) made it possible to generate more specific models for how phosphorylation of particular USP21 residues could activate the enzyme. Our approach allows for rapid identification of protein interactors that can be directly validated by cell-based and biochemical assays. More generally, our approach to identifying IDR interactors can be applied to other systems to search for enzymes or binding partners that interact with specific protein sequences or domains within a larger protein.

  By performing AlphaFold3 predictions on the USP family of DUBs, we identified 12 DUBs that potentially contain autoinhibitory IDR regions. Of these, USP10 and USP37 are known to be regulated by phosphorylation (34, 35). Further studies will be needed to test whether the effects of phosphorylation on the activity of these DUBs are mediated through an autoinhibitory IDR. Understanding how IDR phosphorylation regulates the 12 identified DUBs will provide insights into the generalizability of our proposed autoinhibition model.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Expression and purification of ubiquitin
  Ubiquitin was expressed and purified as described previously (22). Briefly, His-TEV-ubiquitin containing a G76C substitution was expressed in Rosetta2 cells. Ubiquitin was first purified by Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography and then dialyzed into ammonium acetate pH 4.5 overnight. Dialyzed protein was loaded onto a SP-HP cation exchange column (GE), equilibrated in ammonium acetate pH 4.5, and eluted over a linear gradient to 750 mM NaCl. Fractions were dialyzed in ubiquitin storage buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.8) and 1 mM (tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine) (TCEP)], flash frozen, and stored at −80°C.


### Expression and purification of histones
  Histone purifications were performed as described previously (52, 53). Xenopus laevis histone plasmids containing H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 were a gift from Gregory Bowman.

  Histones were expressed in Rosetta2-pLysS cells and grown in 2x yeast extract tryptone (2xYT) media. Cells were induced with 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) between 0.4 and 0.6 and grown for an additional 2 to 4 hours at 37°C before harvested by centrifugation. Pellets were resuspended in histone wash buffer [50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF)], flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

  Resuspended pellets were thawed and lysed three times with a Microfluidizer (Microfluidics). The lysate was centrifuged, and pellets were resuspended with Triton wash buffer [50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 5 mM BME, and 1% Triton X-100]. The pellet was centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed and resuspended in histone wash buffer. Resuspended pellets were then centrifuged and the supernatant removed before storing at −20°C. Frozen pellets were later thawed and resuspended in dimethyl sulfoxide, followed by gradual titration of histone unfolding buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 7 M guanidine-HCl, and 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] at room temperature over 1 hour. Once resuspended, the lysate was centrifuged and the supernatant harvested. The supernatant was filtered before purification on a Sephacryl 26/60 S200 sizing column equilibrated in histone sizing buffer [10 mM tris (pH 8.0), 7 M urea, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM BME]. Fractions were pooled and injected onto a tandem-column series of a HiTrap Q-XL column connected upstream of a HiTrap SP-XL column using histone ion exchange buffer A [20 mM tris (pH 7.8), 7 M urea, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM BME]. The Q-XL column was removed from the system and then eluted over a gradient with histone ion exchange buffer B [20 mM tris (pH 7.8), 7 M urea, 1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM BME]. Purified histones were dialyzed in 5 mM BME, concentrated, lyophilized, and stored at −20°C.


### Preparation of nonhydrolyzable linkage H2AK119ub
  H2AK119ub containing a nonhydrolyzable DCA linkage between H2AK119C and G76C was prepared as described (22, 54). Briefly, 100 μM His-TEV-ubiquitin (G76C) and equimolar H2AK119C was mixed in crosslinking buffer [50 mM borate (pH 8.1) and 1 mM TCEP] and incubated at 50°C for 1 hour. The mixture was then cooled on ice for 10 min before initiating crosslinking. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 100 μM dichloroacetone (DCA). After 1 hour, the reaction was quenched with 50 mM BME, dialyzed into water, and then lyophilized. The lyophilized product was resuspended in Ni-NTA–6MU buffer [6 M urea, 50 mM tris (pH 7.8), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM BME, 20 mM imidazole, and 0.1 mM PMSF] and incubated with Ni-NTA resin. Unreacted H2A and crosslinked H2A were removed in the flow through. The desired product, unreacted ubiquitin and crosslinked ubiquitin-ubiquitin, was eluted in Ni-NTA–6MU buffer supplemented with 250 mM imidazole and dialyzed into TEV cleavage buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT]. The His-tag on ubiquitin was removed by the addition of TEV protease. The product was dialyzed into water, lyophilized, and redissolved in Ni-NTA–6MU buffer. The product was passed through Ni-NTA to remove uncleaved protein and His-tagged TEV protease. The flowthrough was dialyzed into water and lyophilized again. The lyophilized protein was dissolved in 7 M guanidine-HCl and separated on a Proto 300 C4 column (Higgins Analytical) in 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and a gradient of 0.1% TFA + 90% acetonitrile. Fractions corresponding to pure H2AK119ub were pooled and lyophilized.


### Purification of Widom 601 DNA
  The 147–base pair (bp) Widom 601 DNA sequence (55) was produced from the pST55-16x601plasmid (56). The pST55-16x601 plasmid was purified, and the 601 sequence was isolated as described previously (57).


### Reconstitution of H2AK119-ubiquitinated nucleosomes
  H2AK119-ubiquitinated nucleosomes were prepared essentially as described previously (54). In brief, lyophilized histone proteins (H2AK119ub, H2B, H3, and H4) were resuspended in histone unfolding buffer [20 mM tris (pH 7.5), 7 M guanidine-HCl, and 10 mm DTT] and combined in an H2AK119ub:H2B:H3:H4 equimolar ratios. Combined histones were dialyzed into histone refolding buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.5), 2 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM BME] to refold the histones into the histone octamer. The octamer was then purified using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200-pg size exclusion column in histone refolding buffer.

  For the reconstitution, H2AK119ub octamer, dimer, and Widom 601 DNA were combined in an octamer:dimer:DNA molar ratio of 1.1:0.2:1 in high-salt buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.5), 2 M KCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT) such that the final DNA concentration was 6 μM. The salt in the mixture was gradually reduced over 24 hours by salt gradient dialysis into low-salt buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.5), 0.25 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT] to assemble nucleosome. Any precipitate was removed by centrifugation, and the purity of the nucleosome sample was assessed using electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). If the nucleosome sample showed excess free DNA or higher-order species, then it was additionally purified by high-performance liquid chromatography using a SK DEAE-5PW column (TOSOH biosciences) with buffer A [10 mM tris (pH 7.5), 0.25 M KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT] and a gradient elution of buffer B [10 mM tris (pH 7.5), 0.6 M KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT] using an Agilent HPLC instrument. Purified nucleosome was dialyzed into Nuc freezing buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 25 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and 20% glycerol], flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.


### Expression and purification of USP21 constructs
  All human USP21 constructs were expressed in BL21(DE3)Rosetta2 Escherichia coli cells in 2X YT media and contains an N-terminal 6x-His-SUMO tag. The cells were induced overnight with 0.5 mM IPTG at an OD600 between 0.6 and 0.7 at 18° to 20°C. Pellets were resuspended in USP21 lysis buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 10 μM of ZnCl2, 40 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 2 mM BME] supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C.

  Pellets were thawed in a water bath and lysed by sonication for 2.5 min (5-s on, 5-s off) at 50% power or passed through a microfluidizer three times. Following, the lysates were then clarified by centrifugation (11,000 rpm at 4°C for 30 min). The supernatant was filtered and incubated with 3 to 5 ml of Ni-NTA resin for 1 hour at 4°C. Following, the supernatant-bead slurry was loaded over a gravity column to remove unbound protein, and the beads were resuspended in a wash buffer. Beads bound with USP21 constructs containing parts of the IDR (1 to 565, 48 to 565, 122 to 565, 159 to 565, and 185 to 565) were first washed with USP21 lysis buffer supplemented with 20 mM MgCl2 and 5 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) to remove any chaperone contaminants. For all constructs, the beads were washed twice with USP21 lysis buffer, in which the beads were incubated with 10 column volumes of wash buffer for 30 min at 4°C. Following, the beads were then resuspended in 10 ml of elution buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 10 μM ZnCl2, 300 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, and 2 mM BME] for 1 hour at 4°C and eluted. Another elution was performed with only a 30-min incubation.

  Eluted protein was pooled and mixed with 1 mg of 6x-His SUMO-specific protease (SENP) to cleave off the SUMO-tag overnight at 4°C. The next day, the eluted protein was dialyzed for 4 hours at 4°C in USP21 ion-exchange (IEX) buffer A [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 10 μM ZnCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol]. The sample was then loaded over a HiTrap SP-XL column with a gradient elution using USP21 IEX buffer A and USP21 IEX buffer B [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl, 10 μM ZnCl2, 2 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol]. Fractions of interest were concentrated and injected over a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 column or Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL equilibrated in 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol. Fractions of interest were pooled together, concentrated, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen as single-use aliquots, and stored at −80°C.


### Cryo-EM sample preparation of USP21-H2AK119ub nucleosome
  For assembly of the USP21-H2AK119ub complex, a final concentration of 240 nM H2AK119ub nucleosome was incubated with 1.2 μM USP21196–565 in 4.5 ml of crosslinking buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 2.5 mM MgCl2] for 1 hour on ice. Then 900 μl of 0.6% glutaraldehyde crosslinker, diluted in crosslinking buffer, was added to the reaction to obtain a final concentration of 200 nM H2AK119ub nucleosome, 1 μM USP21196–565, and 0.1% glutaraldehyde. The crosslinking reaction was allowed to continue on ice for 15 min before quenching with 600 μl of 1 M tris-HCl (pH 7.5), reaching a final tris-HCl concentration of 100 mM. The sample was then concentrated and dialyzed against sample buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, and 2.5 mM MgCl2] for 2 hours. The dialyzed sample was further concentrated before freezing. Within 30 min of freezing, 0.25% octyl-glucopyranoside prepared in sample buffer was added to the sample, reaching a final concentration of 0.05% of detergent.

  Sample was vitrified on Quantifoil 400 mesh R1.2/1.3 containing a graphene oxide support layer prepared by the manufacturer. A 3.5 μl of USP21-H2AK119ub nucleosome sample at a concentration of 0.6 mg/ml was applied onto the grid and plunge froze at 4°C and 100% humidity using a FEI Vitrobot Mark IV.


### Cryo-EM data collection and processing
  Two cryo-EM datasets were collected at the Beckman Center for Cryo-EM at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine using a Titan Krios at 300 kV equipped with a Falcon 4 Direct Electron Detector with a Selectris Energy Filter. The first dataset contained 10,737 micrographs, and the second dataset contained 10,184 micrographs. Both datasets were collected in counting mode and recorded in electron event representation (EER) format using a magnification of ×130k, a pixel size of 0.97 Å, a nominal dose of 40 e−/Å2, a defocus range of −0.5 to −2.5 μm, and an energy filter slit width of 10 eV.

  The first dataset was processed first in cryoSPARC v4.2 (58). Exposures were imported with an EER upsampling factor of 2 and cropped to one-half of their original resolution using patch motion correction, followed by CTF correction. Blob picker was first performed to generate two-dimensional (2D) classes to use for template picker. A total of 7.9 million particles were picked and extracted with a 270 pixel box size. A subset of particles was used to generate multiple ab initio classes. The particle set was cleaned by “decoy classification” through heterogenous refinement, where all 7.9 million particles were assigned to either one of six “junk” classes or one good class (ab initio volume resembling a nucleosome). Seven rounds of decoy classification were performed until 441,849 particles remained. The particles were subjected to two rounds of focused 3D classifications, followed by two rounds of variability analysis (3DVA) (59). The remaining 29,977 particles were subjected to nonuniform refinement (NU-refinement) (60), giving a low-resolution structure of USP21 bound to H2AK119ub nucleosome. To improve the resolution of the map, a second dataset was imported as described for the first dataset. TOPAZ was used to train ~6000 particles from the map produced from the first dataset to automate particle picking in both datasets, selecting 6.08 million particles. The particles were extracted with a 270 pixel box size and were subjected to four rounds of decoy classification using one good class and two “junk” classes. Tossed particles subjected to stringent 2D classification to rescue any good particles. Particles that were assigned to classes that were at the Nyquist resolution were reintroduced to the particle stack before a final round of heterogenous refinement, leaving 803,684 particles. The particles were then subjected to two rounds of focused 3D classification with a spherical mask around the USP21 density. This approach originally yielded poor density for USP21 and H2AK119ub; however, after upgrading to cryoSPARC v4.6, which has an improved 3D classification algorithm, we were able to obtain clear density for USP21 and H2AK119ub. After two rounds, the particles were refined by homogenous refinement followed by NU-refinement, yielding a 2.98-Å map from a 83,810 particle stack. A mask was generated around USP21-H2AK119ub and was subjected to local refinement, yielding an USP21-H2AK119ub focus map at a 5.33-Å resolution.


### Model building and refinement
  PDB coordinates of the covalent ubiquitin-USP21 complex (PDB: 3I3T) (19) and nucleosome (PDB: 8G6Q) were rigid-body fitted to the map using UCSF ChimeraX (61). The model was then rebuilt manually and subjected to real-space refinement using Coot and Phenix (62–64). The final coordinates were validated using comprehensive validation (cryo-EM) in Phenix before deposition in the PDB. All figures were generated with UCSF ChimeraX.


### Immunoblotting H2AK119 DUB assay
  H2AK119 DUB assays were done at a concentration of 50 nM USP21 and 400 nM recombinant human H2AK119ub biotinylated 147-bp nucleosomes (EpiCypher #16-0395). All reactions were done in a volume of 16 μl in USP21 activity assay buffer [25 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 2 μM ZnCl2, and 5 mM DTT]. Reactions were incubated at 30°C for 60 min. At each listed time point, 4 to 6 μl of the reaction was quenched in 2x lithium dodecyl sulfate (LDS) buffer. Quenched reactions were resolved on a 4 to 12% bis-tris Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membrane using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in 1X tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) buffer, probed with 1:2500 anti-H2AK119ub (Abcam, #ab193203) or 1:2500 anti-H3 (Abcam, #ab1791) primary antibodies in 5% milk in 1X TBS-T overnight at 4°C, and then probed with 1:5000 anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, #31460) horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody in 5% milk in 1X TBST for 90 min at room temperature. Membranes were stained with enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and imaged.

  For experiments done with phosphorylated USP21, USP21 (2 μM) was first treated with 100 nM glutathione S-transferase (GST)–PRKCI (Sino Biological) and GST-MARK (Sino Biological) in kinase activity buffer [25 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 20 mM ATP, and 5 mM DTT]. Phosphorylation reactions were done at 30°C for 60 min. The phosphorylated protein was then directly diluted in USP21 activity buffer and was used for activity assays as described above.


### K48 di-ubiquitin deubiquitination assay
  K48 di-ubiquitin assays were performed at a concentration of 100 nM USP21 and 5 μM K48 di-ubiquitin. All reactions were done in USP21 activity assay buffer at 30°C for 60 min. At each listed time point, 10 μl of the reaction was quenched in 2x LDS buffer. Quenched reactions were resolved on a 4 to 12% bis-tris gel by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie blue. For Fig. 4C, the band intensity of K48 di-ubiquitin and mono-ubiquitin was measured using Bio-Rad Image Lab and used to calculate the fraction of K48 deubiquitinated. The average and standard deviation (SD) are reported across technical triplicates.


### Ub-AMC deubiquitination assay
  Ub-AMC assays were performed with different concentrations of USP21 and 4 μM Ub-AMC in 8-μl reactions. For experiments measuring activity of USP21 truncations, all reactions were done using 500 nM USP21. All reactions were performed in USP21 activity assay buffer at 37°C with a Greiner 384-well small volume plate. The AMC fluorescence was measured every 10 s using a CLARIOstar Plus Plate Reader. All plots in the main text report the average relative activity and SD across technical triplicates. The normalization to determine relative activity was done using the minimum and maximum mean fluorescence across the triplicates.

  For experiments done using the USP21 TEV-cleavable (USP21I-T-C) construct, 500 nM USP21I-T-C was incubated with 500 nM TEV protease for 30 min at 30°C in USP21 activity assay buffer. The enzyme was then added to Ub-AMC, reaching a final concentration of 250 nM USP21 and 4 μM Ub-AMC. Activity on Ub-AMC was measured as described above.

  For experiments done with phosphorylated USP21, 2 μM USP21 was first treated with 100 nM human GST-PRKCI (Sino Biological) and human GST-MARK1 (Sino Biological) in kinase activity buffer. Phosphorylation reactions were done at 30°C for 60 min. The phosphorylated protein was then directly diluted in USP21 activity buffer and was used in Ub-AMC assays using a concentration of 500 nM USP21 and 4 μM Ub-AMC and measured as described above.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assays
  For EMSAs, 100 nM 147-bp H2AK119ub nucleosome was mixed with either USP211–565 or USP21196–565 at 30°C for 60 min in EMSA buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 5% sucrose, and 1 mM DTT] in 12-μl reactions. After incubation, 10 μl of the binding reaction was loaded onto a 6% tris-borate EDTA gel at 4°C and run for 90 min at 150 V. The gel was then visualized with SybrGold DNA stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### MS identification of USP21 phosphorylation sites
  USP21 (2 μM) was first treated with 100 nM human GST-PRKCI (Sino Biological) or GST-MARK (Sino Biological) in kinase activity buffer [25 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 20 mM ATP, and 5 mM DTT] at 30°C for 120 min. The sample was then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until used for further downstream processing.

  Sample preparation and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis were carried out by Poochon Scientific (Frederick, Maryland). The sample was divided into two portions and was processed for in-solution trypsin/lysC digestion and chymotrypsin digestion as per SOP-PS-6002 (standard operation of procedure for in-gel digestion). The digested peptide mixture was then concentrated and desalted using C18 Ziptip as per SOP- PS-6005 (standard operation of procedure for desalting digested peptides). Desalted peptides were reconstituted in 20 μl of 0.1% formic acid and 12 μl of peptides was analyzed by 60-min liquid chromatography tandem MS (LC/MS/MS) run.

  The LC/MS/MS analysis of samples was carried out using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Q-Exactive Mass Spectrometer and a Thermo Fisher Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano System. The peptide mixture from each sample was loaded onto a peptide trap cartridge at a flow rate of 5 μl/min. The trapped peptides were eluted onto an EasySpray column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a linear gradient of acetonitrile (3 to 36%) in 0.1% formic acid. The elution duration was 60 min at a flow rate of 0.3 μl/min. Eluted peptides from the EasySpray column were ionized and sprayed into the mass spectrometer using a Nano-EasySpray Ion Source (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under the following settings: spray voltage of 1.6 kV and capillary temperature of 275°C. Raw data file acquired from each sample was searched against human protein sequences database including target USP21 protein sequence using the Proteome Discoverer 2.5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) based on the SEQUEST algorithm. Carbamidomethylation (+57.021 Da) of cysteines was fixed, and modification, oxidation Met and Q/N-deamidated (+0.98402 Da), and Phospho/+79.966 Da (S, T, Y) were set as dynamic modifications. The minimum peptide length was specified to be five amino acids. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 15 parts per million, whereas fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.05 Da. The maximum false peptide discovery rate was specified as 0.01. The resulting Proteome Discoverer Report contains all assembled proteins with peptides sequences and peptide spectrum match counts (PSM#) and MS1 peak area.


### USP21 interaction screen using AlphaPulldown
  The sequences of all known interactors of USP21, identified using both low- and high-throughput techniques in the BioGRID database, were extracted and all duplicates were removed. All predictions were performed using the AlphaPulldown package (46), where all interactors were predicted with both USP211–565 and USP21196–565 using AlphaFold2. All AlphaFold2 predictions were performed with three recycles with five models generated for each prediction. The ipTM and PAE scores for the top model of each prediction were used in the analysis.

  To identify potential interactors of USP21’s disordered region, we first filtered out all interactors where the ipTM score of the interactor predicted with USP211–565 and USP21196–565 was below 0.35, indicating a low-confidence model. For the remaining interactors, we calculated a ΔipTM score, which we define as the difference in ipTM scores of the interactor predicted with USP211–565 and USP21196–565. We determined that any interactor with a ΔipTM > 0.15 to be a potential candidate interactor of USP21’s IDR.


### H2AK119ub conformation analysis and distance calculations
  For the analysis, we extracted the structure of H2AK119ub nucleosome alone (54), the H2AK119ub-bound structures of SSX1 (65), DNMT3A (66), PRC2 (67), RYBP-PRC1 (68), UbcH5b-PRC1 (69), PR-DUB (25), USP16 (23), and USP21 (this study). All H2AK119ub nucleosome containing structures were superimposed onto the crystal structure of nucleosome core particle (PDB: 1KX5) (26) using the core of the histone octamer for alignment. The center of mass using all ubiquitin heavy atoms in each structure was calculated, and the distance was measured to the center of mass of all heavy atoms of H2AK119 in 1KX5. All calculations were performed in ChimeraX (61).


### Structural search for autoinhibited DUBs
  All known human ubiquitin specific proteases were visually inspected using the AlphaFold2 database (70) to identify any DUBs that contain IDRs that overlapped the ubiquitin binding interface. This narrowed the initial list from 54 DUBs to 18. To verify that AlphaFold predicts the IDRs to occlude ubiquitin binding, we repredicted all the DUB structures using AlphaFold3 (50) with and without ubiquitin bound. The apo structure was then analyzed using the Predictomes server (71). We extracted all DUBs that had contacts between the IDR and catalytic domain, which we defined as any interaction that is within 5 Å with a PAE cutoff of 20 Å and is found in two or more models. To identify DUBs that have IDRs that occlude ubiquitin binding, all DUBs that met the above criteria were superimposed with the ubiquitin-bound model. Any high-confidence IDR interactions that did not sterically clash with ubiquitin were removed. This narrowed the list of candidate DUBs to 12.
