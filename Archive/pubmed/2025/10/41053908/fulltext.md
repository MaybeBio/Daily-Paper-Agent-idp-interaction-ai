# SpatPPI: a geometric deep learning model for predicting protein–protein interactions involving intrinsically disordered regions


## Supplementary Information
  The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03820-2.


## Background
  Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are fundamental to numerous biological processes, making the characterization of protein interaction partners essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying genetic and infectious diseases and for informing disease treatment strategies [1–3]. A significant portion of these interactions involves intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (IDPPIs, see Fig. 1a for details), with many disease-associated mutations occurring within these flexible segments [4–6]. Despite notable advances in PPI prediction methods, the unique structural characteristics of intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (IDRs) still pose considerable challenges for accurately predicting IDPPIs (Fig. 1b) [7].Fig. 1Why process intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). a Proportion of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in 20,487 protein structures sourced from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Notably, approximately one-third of these proteins contain over 50% IDR content, with nearly 21% exceeding 70% IDR content, underscoring the widespread occurrence of proteins with substantial IDRs. b IDRs adopting specific conformations upon binding to certain proteins, or remaining structurally undefined even in bound states. This dynamic nature presents a significant challenge for traditional models reliant on static structural representations

  Existing methods for studying PPIs can be broadly classified into two categories: biological experimental for direct interaction measurement and computational approaches for interaction prediction [8]. Experimental methods, such as coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET), are widely used for low-throughput studies, while high-throughput methods like yeast two-hybrid screens offer greater scalability [9]. However, low-throughput methods are labor-intensive and time-consuming, whereas high-throughput methods are plagued by relatively high rates of false positives/negatives, limiting both their accuracy and efficiency in predicting PPIs. Thus, computational approaches, leveraging the growing availability of protein data, have emerged as a more efficient and cost-effective complement to PPI prediction [10].

  Within computational approaches, methods can be further divided based on their design principles into structure assessment and feature inference techniques. Structure assessment methods, such as AF2Complex and Speed-PPI [11, 12], predict PPIs by constructing protein complex structures and evaluating their quality. However, these methods often struggle with IDRs due to limited co-evolutionary information in these regions, resulting in decreased accuracy for complex structure predictions and heightened false-negative rates for IDPPIs [13]. Feature inference methods, represented by various deep learning approaches, focus on extracting features from protein sequences or structures to calculate interaction probabilities. Early models like D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy focused solely on sequence data, neglecting the crucial role of protein structures in mediating interactions [14–17]. Recent advances in protein science have made it possible to predict 3D structures for folded proteins across entire proteomes, leading to the development of PPI prediction methods such as HIGH-PPI and SGPPI [18–20]. These models incorporate predicted 3D structures by converting them into residue contact maps and using graph neural networks (GNNs) to extract structural features. However, current models predominantly rely on inter-residue distances to define information propagation paths and interaction strengths between adjacent nodes, neglecting the inherent geometric properties of residue pairs. Crucially, they fail to account for spatial configurations (e.g., divergent side-chain orientations) that critically influence interaction patterns. Additionally, disordered regions exhibit highly dynamic distributions of backbone dihedral angles, yet conventional edge representations lacking angular features fail to encode domain-specific differential information flow. These limitations collectively contribute to suboptimal performance in IDPPI prediction.

  Although AlphaFold2 cannot directly capture the dynamic conformations of IDRs, its predicted static structures nevertheless preserve functionally relevant information, as demonstrated by three key observations [21–23]: (1) AlphaFold2 serves as an effective predictor of IDRs; (2) it accurately estimates inter-residue distances within IDRs, despite not being explicitly trained on disordered protein data; and (3) it reliably predicts folded conformations for conditionally foldable IDRs. Building on these insights, we propose SpatPPI, a geometric deep learning framework designed to elucidate how the functions of IDRs are modulated by adjacent folded domains [24]. SpatPPI enhances IDPPI prediction by leveraging high-accuracy folded domain information to guide the conformational refinement of dynamic IDRs. Specifically, SpatPPI represents protein structures as graphs, where nodes correspond to residues and edges encode spatial relationships. It employs local coordinate systems to embed backbone dihedral angles into multidimensional edge attributes, enabling automatic distinction between folded domains and IDRs. To further refine IDR flexibility in the context of neighboring folded domains, SpatPPI incorporates dynamic edge updates that reconstruct spatially enriched residue embeddings. Finally, a bilinear function is applied to model the differential contributions of residues from distinct structural regions (folded domains vs. IDRs) during the interaction process [25].

  We demonstrate the excellent prediction performance of SpatPPI on both PPI and IDPPI tasks. Compared to other leading methods, SpatPPI exhibits great advantages on benchmark datasets. Additionally, we assess SpatPPI’s adaptability to structural changes in IDRs. Through molecular dynamics simulations, we adjusted the predicted structures of 283 intrinsically disordered proteins and retested the 1100 pairs of interactions in which they are involved. SpatPPI’s predictions remained largely stable, underscoring its strong robustness. This stability under simulated structural perturbations demonstrates SpatPPI’s adaptability to structural changes in disordered regions. Visualizations of the final residue representations reveal clear clustering of residues in IDRs and those in stable regions, demonstrating SpatPPI’s powerful capacity to capture and understand spatial conformations.


## Results

### Overview of the SpatPPI model
  Similar to other deep learning-based PPI prediction models, such as PIPR and SGPPI, SpatPPI aims to generate detailed feature representations for each amino acid and utilize these features to predict protein–protein interaction likelihoods [26]. Leveraging the 3D protein structures predicted by AlphaFold2, SpatPPI converts these structures into directed graphs, where nodes represent amino acid residues and edges capture their relative spatial relationships through multidimensional edge attributes [27, 28]. Each node is encoded with attributes that reflect evolutionary information, protein secondary structures, and atomic chemical properties, thereby incorporating local and evolutionary insights relevant to protein binding.

  SpatPPI constructs a local coordinate frame for each residue to characterize the spatial relationships between neighboring residues. The edge attributes consist of seven dimensions: the first three dimensions capture the 3D coordinates of adjacent residues within the local coordinate frame, describing their relative positional relationships, while the remaining four dimensions represent the quaternion of a rotation matrix, encoding the orientational differences between residue geometries. SpatPPI incorporates a customized edge-enhanced graph self-attention network (E-GAT) that alternates between updating node and edge attributes. Following each round of information aggregation, it dynamically refines inter-residue distances and angular relationships based on the evolving node embeddings. This iterative process enables the fine-tuning of AlphaFold2-predicted structures, wherein structural adjustments are guided by learned node representations. Consequently, folded domains and IDRs undergo distinct refinement trajectories, with the optimization of IDRs being modulated by structural information propagated from the folded domains.

  Within the framework of a Siamese network, SpatPPI generates residue-level representations for protein pairs. To accurately model contact patterns across distinct structural partitions, SpatPPI adopts a two-stage decoding strategy. First, it generates a residue-level contact probability matrix that preserves partition-specific interaction modes including ordered-ordered, ordered-disordered, and disordered-disordered interactions, thereby mitigating signal dilution in disordered regions that can result from direct mixed embeddings. To eliminate input-order biases, the model performs bidirectional computation by evaluating both the forward and reversed protein pair orders, producing two contact matrices. These matrices are independently pooled to obtain global protein embeddings, which are then used for the final interaction prediction.

  The framework’s dynamic adjustment mechanism and residue-contact capturing architecture are specifically designed to accommodate the structural variability of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), ultimately enhancing SpatPPI’s performance in predicting IDPPIs (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic view of the SpatPPI architecture. SpatPPI consists of two main parts: embedding generation and residue contact capture. The embedding generation module processes the 3D structure of a protein into a directed graph, encoding node attributes to represent residue entities and edge attributes to capture spatial relationships. This module incorporates three layers of Graph Embedding and Aggregation (GEA) blocks. Each GEA block performs iterative updates of node and edge attributes, supplemented with batch normalization (BN), to produce discriminative embedding representations for each amino acid. To eliminate the effect of input order, the residue contact capture module performs two bilinear calculations for a pair of proteins to generate two residue contact matrices for pooling the residue embedding matrices separately. These pooled embeddings are subsequently used to derive the final protein embeddings. The resulting protein embeddings are summed and fed into the final decoder for interaction prediction


### SpatPPI shows the best performance in the prediction of IDPPIs
  To validate the predictive capabilities of SpatPPI in predicting IDPPI, we conducted evaluations from two key perspectives: (1) performance on HuRI-IDP dataset and (2) resilience to structural fluctuations within intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs).

  To ensure conceptual clarity in subsequent discussions, we operationally define IDRs as protein segments in which more than 70% of the full-length sequence is predicted to be disordered. Conversely, structurally stable proteins are defined as those with less than 50% predicted disordered residues. In this study, IDPPIs are specifically defined as physical interactions between an IDR and a structurally stable protein (detailed methods for disorder prediction are provided in the Methods section).

  We established the HuRI-IDP benchmark dataset derived from the high-confidence HuRI project, encompassing approximately 15,000 unique proteins and 36,300 experimentally verified protein–protein interactions (PPIs), with about 50% representing IDPPIs. The dataset was divided into a development set (for model training and validation) and two independent test sets A and B with distinct evaluation purposes. Test set A consists of 2200 PPIs (including 1100 IDPPIs) where all constituent proteins appear in the development set, enabling assessment of model performance in predicting novel links within partially characterized networks. In contrast, test set B contains 3300 PPIs (2200 IDPPIs) featuring completely novel IDRs absent from the development phase (though their structurally stable interaction partners may overlap with training data), thereby providing a rigorous test of model generalizability to unseen disordered regions. We compared SpatPPI against four state-of-the-art methods: SGPPI (a structure-based approach), D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy (both sequence-based), and Speed-PPI, which utilizes AlphaFold2’s predicted complex structures. Except for Speed-PPI, all models were trained and evaluated on the HuRI-IDP dataset. SGPPI focuses on binding interfaces using graph convolutional networks to generate protein embeddings, while D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy create amino acid embeddings from sequence data, with Topsy-Turvy additionally incorporating network connectivity predictions. Speed-PPI assigns interaction scores to protein complexes based on AlphaFold2’s predicted structures.

  To account for the inherent sparsity of authentic PPI networks and maintain biological plausibility, both test sets A and B were constructed with a positive-to-negative sample ratio being 1:10. Given this pronounced class imbalance characteristic of PPI data, conventional evaluation metrics like AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) fail to adequately capture model performance in identifying the minority class (positive interaction pairs) that constitutes our primary research focus. Consequently, this study employs Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) as core evaluation metrics (Additional file 1: Note S1). These metrics are particularly suited for imbalanced datasets as they provide more meaningful insights into model performance on rare positive instances. All models except Speed-PPI were rigorously evaluated using ten-fold cross-validation to ensure robust performance estimation (Fig. 3a, b) [29]. SpatPPI consistently outperforms the other models in both general PPI and IDPPI predictions across the test sets. The sequence-based methods, D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy, lagged significantly, likely due to the absence of co-evolutionary signals in intrinsically disordered proteins. Although SGPPI utilizes structural data, it still performs poorly in our IDPPI-focused experiment, possibly due to the difficulty of GCNs in handling IDRs. Speed-PPI excels in general PPI predictions but struggles with IDPPIs, aligning with previous observations regarding AlphaFold2’s challenges in predicting complexes involving IDRs. In contrast, SpatPPI consistently outperforms the other models in terms of both AUPR and MCC across test set A and test set B for both general and IDPPI predictions (Additional file 3: Table S1). Furthermore, Additional file 3: Table S2 presents the stratified performance analysis of test set B, categorized by the proportion of intrinsically disordered regions.   We sought to validate that SpatPPI’s superior performance in predicting IDPPIs stems from its ability to effectively leverage the limited folded structural information within IDRs and their interaction partners. To this end, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using GROMACS on 1007 IDRs from test set B under physiological conditions (300 K, 1 atm, aqueous solution) with each protein simulated for 3 ns [30] (Additional file 1: Note S4). From 300 snapshots, we selected the most structurally divergent conformations based on the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of non-hydrogen atoms. Figure 3c illustrates representative structural changes before and after simulation. Within the timescale of our MD simulations, stable folded domains exhibited negligible conformational changes, whereas structural variations were almost exclusively localized to IDRs. These simulations thus effectively served as targeted conformational perturbations on IDRs. The average RMSD between pre- and post-simulation structures was 14 Å. Graph-based analyses revealed an average edge set overlap of 0.714, a mean node in-degree change of 3.548, and a spectral distance difference of 0.287—indicating that the conformational alterations in IDRs were substantial enough to affect graph-based model inputs. Nevertheless, the ten-fold cross-validation revealed that the absolute average change in the model’s predicted scores was only 0.039 ± 0.004 (standard error, hereafter). Among the 2200 samples, only 69 ± 7 exhibited outcome reversals (where an originally predicted positive result turned negative, or vice versa).

  For comparison, we applied random perturbations exclusively to folded domains while keeping IDR coordinates unchanged in test set B. We systematically applied progressively increasing random displacements to the folded domain, starting from 0.5 Å with a step size of 0.5 Å. The results demonstrated that when the random displacement exceeded 4 Å, nearly all positive samples were predicted as negative, indicating a complete loss of predictive capability in the model. Finally, we randomly selected 15 IDRs with numerous interaction partners from the HuRI network and collected 250 associated IDPPIs. Each IDR underwent a 13-ns MD simulation, with conformations sampled every 0.1 ns after an initial 3-ns equilibration period, yielding 2000 snapshots and RMSD variations ranging from 10 to 50 Å. From these, 100 representative conformations per IDR were used to generate a total of 25,000 perturbed interaction predictions. Prediction reversal rates were calculated within 10-Å RMSD bins (Fig. 3d). Results showed that while reversal rates increased slightly with higher RMSD, they remained consistently low overall.

  Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SpatPPI exhibits remarkable robustness against drastic conformational changes in IDRs. Its predictions predominantly rely on the limited folded structural information within IDRs and their interaction partners, rather than transient IDR conformations.


### SpatPPI shows strong generalization abilities
  We examined SpatPPI’s residue embedding behavior using the t-SNE algorithm to visualize node embeddings at various stages [31]. However, as the graph aggregation proceeds, the two types of residues with different structural properties are gradually separated, and in the final residue embedding graph, the residues are classified into two categories according to their structural properties (i.e., whether they belong to IDRs or stable regions).   Although interacting proteins from different species or proteomes may exhibit significant differences in sequence or overall structure, the local conformations of residues involved in interactions often demonstrate conservation [32]. SpatPPI incorporates residue-level structural perception, conformational adjustment, and contact simulation in its design, enabling it to better learn the conserved features of local conformations. We hypothesize that this equips SpatPPI with strong generalization. To validate this, we designed two sets of experiments: (1) prediction of novel PPIs and (2) cross-species interaction network reconstruction.

  We retrained SpatPPI using the STRING 11.0 human dataset (a large-scale PPI dataset approximately 15 times the size of the HuRI-IDP dataset), where the ratio of IDPPI to PPI more closely reflects real-world conditions. This dataset was also used to train the D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy models (see Methods for details). A novel test set was constructed, comprising approximately 1500 proteins and 2200 PPIs, with all proteins excluded from the original STRING 11.0 training data. This test set was divided into eight independent subsets based on protein amino acid lengths, and the performance of each model across these subsets is presented in Fig. 4b. The AUPR values of SpatPPI were higher than those of other models across all groups, while its MCC values were slightly lower than Topsy-Turvy in the first four groups. However, as protein length increases, Topsy-Turvy—trained exclusively on short-chain proteins (amino acid length < 950) in the STRING 11.0 dataset—struggles to capture alignment information across sequences of varying scales, leading to a sharp decline in performance. In contrast, SpatPPI, trained on the same dataset, maintains stable prediction accuracy across the entire test set by focusing on local conformational features. Overall, SpatPPI achieves a superior area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR = 0.522) compared to Topsy-Turvy (AUPR = 0.271) and D-SCRIPT (AUPR = 0.292), demonstrating stronger generalization and accuracy, particularly for proteins outside the length constraints of the training data (Additional file 3: Tables S3 and S4).

  We extended the evaluation of SpatPPI’s generalization to cross-species predictions, using a sub-PPI network of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae) as an example (see Fig. 4c and Additional file 2: Fig. S1). The RRP4 and RPS19B proteins form two distinct communities within S. cerevisiae, with 65 and 56 known PPIs within the communities, respectively, and 48 known PPIs between them. We reconstructed this protein–protein interaction (PPI) network de novo, predicting all 253 possible PPI combinations. As shown in Fig. 4c, SpatPPI recovered approximately 75% of intra-community connections and over 80% of inter-community connections. In contrast, Topsy-Turvy restored only 55% of intra-community connectivity and 45% of inter-community connectivity. Although D-SCRIPT exhibited an exceptionally low false positive rate (FPR) of merely 2.3%—lower than both SpatPPI and Topsy (FPR: 37.5% each)—its conservative prediction strategy compromised the comprehensive reconstruction of community structures, recovering fewer than 30% of the original network connections. Furthermore, SpatPPI consistently outperformed other models in terms of the AUPR and MCC.

  SpatPPI demonstrates robustness across novel proteins and even in cross-species prediction scenarios.


### Conformation reorganization improves the performance of SpatPPI
  We investigated the role of the conformation reorganization module from two perspectives: (1) its impact on the model’s predictive performance and (2) the enhancement of model adaptability to structural changes in IDRs. Furthermore, we systematically investigated the impact of incorporating diverse protein conformations during the training phase on model performance.

  To explore the above aspects, we re-implemented the training and testing processes on the HuRI-IDP dataset and the STRING11.0 human dataset, removing the conformation reorganization mechanism while keeping all other conditions constant, resulting in the creation of the SpatPPI-NoCR model. In the ablation study section, we focused more on qualitative analysis rather than evaluating model generalization abilities, thus only the first fold of the ten-fold model was selected for ablation. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the AUPR and MCC values for SpatPPI-NoCR decreased by 3% and 6%, respectively, on test set A, and by 27% and 30%, respectively, on test set B. Notably, in test set B, which contains fewer interaction partners in the training set, the PPI prediction performance of SpatPPI-NoCR shows a significant decline, similar to its performance on IDPPIs. This outcome aligns with our earlier hypothesis that inadequately handled IDRs structure can adversely affect the model’s overall PPI prediction performance. For the novel test set (Fig. 5b), where the proportion of IDRs in the STRING11.0 human dataset used for training returned to normal levels, the impact on general PPI prediction was less pronounced, with the AUPR of SpatPPI-NoCR dropping by 2%, while the MCC value even rose by 2% (see detailed results in Additional file 3: Table S5).Fig. 5Ablation study on conformation reorganization and residue contact capture. a Ablation study results on test sets A and B, comparing the general PPI and IDPPI prediction performance. SpatPPI-NoCR refers to the model without conformation reorganization, and SpatPPI-NoCC refers to the model without residue contact capture. b Ablation study results on the novel test set, highlighting the impact of removing conformation reorganization (NoCR) and residue contact capture (NoCC) on model performance. c Reversal-threshold curve illustrating fluctuations in model predictions before and after molecular dynamics simulations. As the primary structural changes occur in IDRs during simulation, the degree of fluctuation reflects the model’s sensitivity to these dynamic alterations. d Impacts of introducing diverse IDR conformations and duplicated samples during training to the performance of SpatPPI

  The conformational reorganization mechanism in SpatPPI is designed to enhance the model’s capacity for modeling the dynamic nature of IDRs by introducing an adaptive strategy. This strategy leverages information from folded domains to guide structural adjustments in adjacent disordered regions. As demonstrated in the previous section, structural adjustments in protein IDRs produce minimal impact on SpatPPI’s interaction predictions, suggesting the model’s robustness to conformational variability in disordered regions. Here, we aim to validate that this stability is attributable to the conformation reorganization mechanism. Using the same experimental protocol, we employed SpatPPI-NoCR, a variant without conformation reorganization, to predict and analyze 2200 PPI pairs after structural adjustments. Post-evaluation, we identified a reversal effect in prediction pairs: the SpatPPI-NoCR model exhibited 136 reversed prediction pairs, representing a 3.4-fold increase relative to the full model (40 pairs). Further analysis revealed that the average absolute difference in prediction scores fluctuated by 0.074, over two times that of the full model (0.031). By adjusting the classification threshold from 0.1 to 0.9 and plotting the reversal-threshold curve (Fig. 5c), it became clear that the reversal rate of SpatPPI-NoCR consistently exceeded that of the full model across all thresholds. In addition, quantitative comparisons further highlighted a statistically marked disparity in score variability: the SpatPPI-NoCR group displayed a 100% elevation in standard deviation (SD = 0.004) compared to the original model (SD = 0.002). These findings strongly demonstrate that the SpatPPI-NoCR model is more sensitive to structural perturbations in IDRs, underscoring the crucial role of the conformation reorganization mechanism in improving the model’s resilience and adaptability to structural fluctuations in IDRs during PPI prediction.

  Finally, we investigated whether introducing structural diversity during training could help the model better learn the dynamic adjustment patterns of IDRs. To this end, we performed 2.7-ns molecular dynamics simulations for the 406 IDRs present in the positive samples of the development set, sampling one conformation every 0.3 ns, resulting in 9 additional conformations per protein. Accordingly, we duplicated each positive sample in the development set 9 times, with each copy paired with a distinct protein conformation. Based on this augmented dataset, we retrained the model to obtain SpatPPI-MS (multi-structure). To control for the effect of sample size, we also constructed SpatPPI-Dup by duplicating the positive samples 9 times using the same original structure. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, comparative analysis of model performance across test sets A and B reveals that while all three models (original SpatPPI, SpatPPI-MS, and SpatPPI-Dup) demonstrate comparable metrics within normal fluctuation ranges on test set A, both SpatPPI-MS and SpatPPI-Dup exhibit measurable performance degradation on test set B. The introduction of conformational diversity yields only a slight impact in predicting IDPPIs within the development set while simultaneously reducing the model’s generalization ability to unseen proteins. This slight impact likely stems from the inherent redundancy in PPI datasets, where identical proteins frequently interact with multiple partners and recur across samples, thereby providing the model with ample opportunity to learn their structural adjustment patterns without requiring additional conformational sampling. Conversely, the increasing repetition of conserved folded domains during training may inadvertently bias the model toward domain-specific features rather than IDR-mediated interaction patterns, ultimately promoting overfitting and reducing extrapolation capacity to novel targets.


### Residue contact capture enhances the generalization ability of SpatPPI
  We investigated the role of the residue contact capture module from two perspectives: (1) its impact on the model’s generalization capability and (2) its contribution to enhancing the model’s understanding of protein conformation.

  To explore the role of the residue contact capture module in SpatPPI, we removed this component and homogenized the amino acid residues of a protein pair before concatenating them as input to the MLP decoder, following the approach used in SGPPI. This modification resulted in a variant called SpatPPI-NoCC. As shown in Fig. 5a, SpatPPI-NoCC achieves a higher AUPR value than the full model on test set A, but a lower MCC value. This suggested that the simplified inference process made the model more aggressive in its predictions, leading to a higher false positive rate despite identifying more positive samples. However, on test set B, the inclusion of out-of-set data rapidly diminished the performance of SpatPPI-NoCC. The contrast became even more evident on the novel test set, where removing the residue contact capture module reduced the model’s AUPR to 0.125, nearly nullifying its predictive capability (Fig. 5b). The obvious performance drop underscored the critical importance of the residue contact capture mechanism in bolstering the model’s generalization performance (Additional file 3: Table S5).

  It is important to note that, as we did not provide any docking information to the model, the resultant residue contact matrix derived from the final predictions does not accurately reflect the actual physical docking mechanisms. The residue contact capture module generates a hidden representation of residue interaction pattern rather than revealing exact biophysical mechanisms. This module was specifically designed to facilitate interactions between two sets of residue representations from different proteins by modeling the residue contact process. The model used only the existence or absence of an interaction between the two proteins as supervisory information. Back propagation of this a priori knowledge enabled the model to discern residue combinations with conformations that are more conducive to interactions, which is pivotal for improving conformational understanding in PPI predictions.


## Discussion
  Integrating protein 3D structural information into PPI prediction has emerged as a cutting-edge trend driving advancements in this field. However, current PPI prediction models that leverage structural information often fail to adequately handle IDRs in proteins, leading to structural distortions that diminish model learning efficiency and predictive performance, particularly for IDPPIs.

  In this study, we introduce SpatPPI, a deep learning framework that offers profound insights into protein spatial structures with the aim of accurately identifying PPIs, especially IDPPIs. Based on AlphaFold2’s predicted protein structures, SpatPPI constructs detailed feature representations for each amino acid residue using a carefully designed graph attention network architecture and a conformation reorganization mechanism. By combining residue feature encoding with bilinear mapping techniques, it generates a residue contact probability map between proteins. This map is subsequently used to aggregate amino acid embeddings, ultimately producing protein representations for PPI prediction. Experimental results demonstrate that SpatPPI greatly outperforms all compared state-of-the-art PPI predictors in IDPPI recognition, showcasing remarkable resilience to structural perturbations and strong generalization capabilities.

  The strong predictive capability of SpatPPI can be attributed to three primary factors: (1) geometry-driven differentiation: construct local coordinate systems and encode angular information as edge features, enabling the model to discriminate between folded and disordered regions based on geometric and angular variability. (2) The conformation reorganization mechanism: iteratively updates residue geometry during message passing, allowing structure refinement tailored to local embeddings, with disordered region adjustments guided by neighboring folded domains. (3) The residue contact capture mechanism: introduces a two-stage decoding strategy that preserves region-specific interaction patterns through contact probability matrices, mitigating feature dilution in disordered regions.

  The prediction of SpatPPI relies on predicted protein structures and PSSM searches. Depending on the number of protein residues, obtaining these prerequisite files can take 0.5–1.5 h. However, these tasks represent a one-time investment, and the vast majority of predicted protein structures can be obtained from the AlphaFold Database. Additionally, PSSM searches can be significantly accelerated through parallel computing, greatly reducing the processing time per protein (e.g., the average time for generating a PSSM file on test set A is 4.6 min using 20 threads on our service). The strength of SpatPPI lies in its preprocessing module, which consolidates all required input features into a lightweight tensor. The total size of the tensor file generated for the 13,221 proteins in the HuRI-IDP dataset is less than 3 GB. In contrast, sequence-based methods such as Topsy-Turvy require precomputed embeddings for the same set of proteins, occupying approximately 150 GB of memory. These embeddings must be loaded during each prediction, posing an obstacle to large-scale applications. A detailed comparison of prediction efficiency is provided in Additional file 3: Table S7.

  Although we can see the great advantages made by the SpatPPI model, improvements are still needed in the future as follows. Firstly, the model’s reliance on extracting comprehensive features from PDB files is a more complex and time-consuming process compared to models that directly utilize pre-trained protein embeddings [33, 34]. The selected features employed in the model may not be sufficient to fully capture the necessary information for IDPPI prediction. Additionally, while SpatPPI adapts dynamically to single structural predictions, these may not provide sufficient detail to resolve the complexities of IDRs behavior. Emerging research suggests that IDRs often exist as dynamic ensembles of interchanging conformations, representing a range of accessible states [35]. Developing predictors that describe these ensembles through probabilistic models could offer a promising avenue for enhancing IDPPI prediction.

  Nonetheless, the achievements of SpatPPI clearly demonstrate its immense potential in the field of PPI prediction, particularly for IDPPI prediction. Its accuracy surpasses those of previous molecular docking methods and deep learning approaches based on known structural proteins, underscoring the vast potential and promising future of deep learning applications in PPI network reorganization. For the convenience of the users, we provide a user-friendly service for online using the trained SpatPPI models via http://liulab.top/SpatPPI/server. Users can easily predict PPIs by online submitting query protein structures predicted by AlphaFold2 (Additional file 2: Fig. S3).


## Conclusions
  SpatPPI advances the prediction of IDPPIs through three key innovations: (1) geometry-driven differentiation (local coordinate systems + angular features), (2) dynamic conformation reorganization (folded domain-guided optimization of disordered regions), and (3) residue contact capture (bilinear mapping for interaction preservation). As the first dedicated deep learning model designed for large-scale IDPPIs prediction, it proposes to utilize the dynamic information preserved in predicted folded structures adjacent to IDRs to guide structural adjustments of IDRs, effectively enhancing the model’s superior predictive accuracy, robustness, and generalization ability on IDPPIs.

  Selection of intrinsically disordered regions. Previous studies have shown that regions of low confidence in AlphaFold2 predictions are highly consistent with IDRs [37]. Window-averaged AlphaFold2 pLDDT scores can be an effective tool for predicting IDRs. Specifically, residues with pLDDT > 0.8, pLDDT < 0.7, and 0.7 ≤ pLDDT ≤ 0.8 were first labeled as folded, disordered, and gap regions, respectively. Next, folded and disordered regions shorter than ten residues were reclassified as gap regions. If a gap region (i) was surrounded by disordered regions or (ii) was located at the termini with adjacent disordered regions, it was relabeled as disordered. All other gap regions were relabeled as folded regions. Additionally, since AlphaFold2 sometimes assigns high pLDDT scores to certain residues in known IDRs, possibly related to specific conditions such as binding or post-translational modifications, we also referred to Tesei’s approach and used SPOT-Disorder v.1 to identify IDRs [38]. In this study, the concatenation of the prediction results of these two prediction methods was used as the final IDRs region.

  HuRI-IDP dataset. To evaluate the performance of SpatPPI in predicting IDPPI, we used the data from the HuRI Mapping Project, which detected 52,569 PPIs involving 8727 human proteins through high-throughput yeast two-hybrid screens. This project is both comprehensive and reliable, and importantly, it includes a higher proportion of intrinsically disordered proteins among the proteins involved, which facilitated the construction of our IDPPI dataset.

  We define proteins with IDRs accounting for more than 70% of their total residues as intrinsically disordered proteins, those with IDRs accounting for less than 50% as structurally stable proteins, and the remainder as transitional proteins. To minimize the impact of sequence similarity, we used CD-HIT [39, 40] to cluster the protein sequences, setting a sequence identity threshold of 40%. After stringent sequence similarity filtering, 6831 proteins remained.

  In this study, IDPPI refers to a PPI between an intrinsically disordered protein and a structurally stable protein. In the training set and test set A, IDPPI does not include transitional proteins. However, in test set B, due to the limited availability of proteins, approximately 10% of IDPPI pairs involve transitional proteins and structurally stable proteins. Our IDPPI training set contains 2800 positive and 28,000 negative protein interactions, with IDPPI accounting for 50% of both positive and negative samples. Test set A comprises 200 positive sample pairs (100 IDPPI) and 2000 negative sample pairs, while test set B contains 300 positive sample pairs (200 IDPPI) and 3000 negative sample pairs. The difference between them is that test set A was randomly divided along with the training set (ensuring an IDPPI proportion of 50%), with 95% of the proteins appearing in the training set, while test set B was constructed separately to ensure that the intrinsically disordered proteins had never appeared in the training set. For test set B construction, positive samples were rigorously curated to preferentially include IDRs with manually annotated disorder status, thereby mitigating the risk of misclassifying conventional PPIs as IDPPIs. All positive samples in the datasets were derived from the HuRI Mapping Project, with the exception of approximately 20% of positive samples in test set B, which originated from the STRING database. The number of negative samples is 10 times higher than that of positive samples, and each negative sample is randomly paired from all proteins [41]. Additionally, we introduced 10,000 extra proteins to generate negative samples, ensuring that no protein appeared too many times in the training set.

  STRING11.0 human dataset. To verify the generalization performance of SpatPPI, we trained a new SpatPPI model using the same dataset as D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy. The positive interactions in this dataset were extracted from the STRING v11 database, which covers various PPI networks and integrates comprehensive information from multiple major sources. The protein sequences in this dataset range from 50 to 800 amino acids in length, and the sequence similarity between protein pairs is strictly controlled below 40% to avoid the model over-relying on sequence similarity when predicting interactions. To better reflect the naturally observed frequency of positive interactions, the ratio of positive to negative samples in the D-SCRIPT dataset was set at 1:10. Negative interactions were generated by randomly pairing different proteins from the non-redundant interaction set.

  Novel protein test set. The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AFDB) initially provided 23,391 predicted human protein structures (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/alphafold/latest/UP000005640_9606_HUMAN_v4.tar). We applied a chain length filter (50–2000 residues) to exclude fragments and oversized assemblies, yielding a subset of 20,487 proteins. These structures were further processed through CD-HIT clustering with a 60% sequence similarity threshold, resulting in 16,576 non-redundant clusters. After removing proteins that appeared in the STRING11.0 human dataset, 1500 protein structures remained. We used these 1500 proteins to construct the novel protein test set. The STRING v12 database [42] contains richer protein interaction data than STRING v11. We queried the STRING v12 database for interaction data within the novel protein set, selecting 200 high-confidence interactions with a combined score greater than 0.90 as positive samples for the novel protein test set. For negative samples, we used an intra-group pairing strategy, generating 2000 pairs of negative samples while limiting each protein to participate in no more than six negative sample pairs to ensure diversity and validity.


### The SpatPPI architecture
  In this section, we provide a detailed description of the SpatPPI model framework, which comprises four main components: graph representation of protein structures, feature extraction, graph self-attention aggregation with conformation reorganization, and residue contact capture module.

  1) Graph representation of protein structures.

  2) Feature extraction.

  SpatPPI extracts 41-dimensional amino acid features V for each amino acid residue in a protein. Dimensions 1–20 represent the sequence evolutionary features of the amino acid residues, dimensions 21–33 represent structural features including secondary structure information and backbone torsion angles, dimension 34 represents the hydrophobicity feature, and dimensions 35–41 represent the physicochemical properties of the amino acid residues detailed as follows.

  Sequence evolutionary features. The Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM) provides conservation information for amino acid residues and is commonly used in PPI prediction tasks. It represents the likelihood of each amino acid appearing at every position in a protein’s amino acid sequence. In this study, the PSSM matrix was generated using the PSI-BLAST with three iterations and an e value parameter of 0.001, searching the Uniref50 database [48, 49]. We used only the first 20 columns of the PSSM matrix, corresponding to the 20 common amino acids. The value in the i row and j column reflects the probability that the amino acid at position i will mutate to the j amino acid during evolution; the higher the value, the greater the probability.   Solvent accessibility feature. Solvent accessibility reflects the surface area of an amino acid residue that can contact solvent molecules.   Atomic features of residues. For a residue, we extracted seven features for each atom belonging to that residue (excluding hydrogen atoms), including atom mass, B-factor, whether the atom is a side-chain atom, electronic charge, number of bonded hydrogen atoms, whether the atom is in a ring, and Van der Waals radius of the atom. Since different amino acid residues have varying numbers of atoms, SpatPPI takes the average of the corresponding features of all atoms as the value of that feature for the amino acid residue.

### Parameter settings of other compared methods
  All computational workflows were executed on standardized hardware featuring Intel® Xeon® Platinum 8358 CPUs and NVIDIA A100 GPU acceleration to ensure reproducible performance metrics.D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy. The training and evaluation procedures for both D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy were meticulously executed following their official documentation (https://d-script.readthedocs.io/en/stable/usage.html). All hyperparameters were strictly maintained at their recommended values throughout the experiments to ensure consistency with the original implementations. We rigorously performed ten-fold cross-validation for D-SCRIPT, training each fold for 15 epochs (as it was observed that both D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy exhibited noticeable performance degradation after an average of 8 epochs), and selected the model with the highest validation performance in each fold for final testing and comparison.SGPPI. For SGPPI implementation, we utilized the authors’ codebase (https://github.com/emerson106/SGPPI) while making one critical adjustment: extending the training epochs from the default 20 to 50 to address observed underfitting issues, as preliminary tests indicated the original epoch count yielded suboptimal convergence. All other hyperparameters and architectural components remained unchanged to preserve methodological integrity. The training data were exclusively generated from the author-provided dataset (10.6084/m9.figshare.20353353), ensuring consistency with the original study’s data processing pipeline.Speed-PPI. Our assessment of Speed-PPI leveraged the precomputed HuRI benchmark results (10.17044/scilifelab.16866202.v1), which serendipitously encompassed all positive samples in our HuRI-IDP test set. We adopted the authors’ recommended pDockq > 0.23 threshold for positive prediction identification. Speed-PPI employs AlphaFold to predict protein complex structures prior to PPI scoring. Although the original authors supplied precomputed predictions for approximately 60,000 samples (encompassing all positive samples in our test sets), these did not include the extensive collection of negative samples generated through random protein pairing. Conducting AlphaFold predictions at this scale for negative samples would have been computationally prohibitive. Consequently, Speed-PPI’s evaluation was limited to assessing accuracy solely on positive samples, while adopting the optimistic assumption of perfect prediction (100% accuracy) for all negative samples. Even under this artificially favorable evaluation framework, Speed-PPI’s performance on IDPPIs remained inferior to SpatPPI’s results.
