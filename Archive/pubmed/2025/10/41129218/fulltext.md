# Driving forces of RNA condensation revealed through coarse-grained modeling with explicit Mg2+


## Significance
  Dynamic RNAs and proteins are major drivers of biomolecular phase separation to form condensates that have been recently discovered to underlie numerous biological processes and be involved in many human diseases. Molecular simulation has an indispensable role to play in dissecting the driving forces and regulation of biomolecular phase separation. The current work describes an intermediate-resolution coarse-grained RNA model that is capable of describing the structure dynamics and complex sequence, concentration, temperature, and ion-dependent phase transitions of flexible RNAs. The study further reveals a central role of RNA folding in coordinating Mg2+–phosphate interactions, base stacking, and base pairing to drive phase separation, paving the road for studies of RNA-mediated phase separation in relevant biological contexts.


## Results

### Design of iConRNA: All-Atom to CG Mapping and Energy Function.
  Each ribonucleotide is represented by six or seven CG beads in iConRNA, similar to what is adopted in Martini-RNA (63). As illustrated in Fig. 1, the phosphate group is represented as one negatively charged bead (B1), while the ribose sugar (including the backbone methylene) is mapped to two beads (B2 and B3). For bases, we use four-bead and three-bead rings to model purines, namely, adenine (A) and guanine (G), and pyrimidines, namely, cytosine (C) and uracil (U), respectively. The detailed all-atom (AA) to CG mapping scheme is given in SI Appendix, Table S1. Note that, when converting the atomistic model to the CG model, B2 and B3 are fixed at the positions of C4’ and C1’ of ribose, while the center of mass is used for other CG beads.

  The total potential energy of iConRNA includes bonded (bond, angle, dihedral, and improper), nonbonded (Van de Waals and electrostatics), base stacking, and base-pairing interactions:[1]Utotal=Ubonded+Unonbonded+Ustacking+Upairing.

  Detailed functional forms of all energy terms are given in SI Appendix, Eqs. S1–S8. In the current version, only the backbone phosphate bead (B1) is charged, and the Debye–Hückel-type electrostatic potential is used. The stacking interaction was only assigned to two neighboring bases along the chain from 5’ to 3’ using virtual sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), to help mimic the A-form-like helical structure of RNA. Only the canonical Watson–Crick A-U and G-C base-pairing interactions are explicitly included (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), each captured using two effective pair potentials (SI Appendix, Eq. S8) to help maintain the planar configuration of pairing bases. Details about the design of all energy terms can be found in SI Appendix.

  To capture the temperature dependence of electrostatic screening of water medium, we adopt an empirical relation previously derived from experimentally measured dielectric constants (71),[2]εrT=87.74-0.40008T+9.398×10-4T2-1.41×10-6T3,

  where T is the temperature in Celsius. Because the value of εr=20.0 is used in iConRNA at 303 K, this relation was then scaled down as,[3]εrT=22.94-0.10466T+2.458×10-4T2-0.37×10-6T3.

  In addition, entropic contributions due to the release of counterions and coordination water molecules strengthen the interaction between oppositely charged groups at higher temperatures, which drives the LCST-type phase separation of RNAs and other polyelectrolytes (43, 72–77). As described below, the resulting increased interaction between Mg2+ and phosphate can be effectively captured by the temperature dependence of excess Mg2+ ions per phosphate.


### Model Parameterization and Validation: Folding and Dynamics of RNAs.
  The force field parameters for the iConRNA model were determined largely in a sequential manner, starting from bonded interactions, then nonbonded and base-stacking interactions, and finally, base-pairing interactions. The parameterization was guided by theoretical analysis, atomistic simulations, and experimental measurements (SI Appendix, SI Text). All modeled RNAs, as well as simulation configurations, can be found in SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3 (Materials and Methods).


#### Bonded terms.
  Parameters involved in bonded interactions were first iteratively optimized to match the corresponding CG and AA distributions, which are summarized in SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S6. Details of all bonded parameters are provided in SI Appendix, Tables S4–S7. Overall, the CG distributions match the reference AA distributions very well, but show fewer features in several cases, such as the B2-B3-A1/G1-A2/G2 dihedral angle distribution (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Note that the puckering of the ribose ring can affect the orientation of the base as well as backbone phosphate groups (78). For example, the minor peaks in B1-B2-B3-A1/G1/C1/U1 dihedral distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) are associated with C2’-endo pucker. The current iConRNA model only captures the preferred C3’-endo pucker (major peaks). At the atomistic level, the relative orientation of the base and ribose ring is highly flexible, and the B2-B3-A1/G1-A2/G2 dihedral angle could sample both cis- and trans-like configurations for ssRNAs, especially at the termini. Thus, a soft term is used to cover the main peak of the reference distribution.


#### Nonbonded and base-stacking interactions.
  For nonbonded parameters, the vdW radius (rimin/2) of each CG bead was assigned to reproduce the average total vdW volume of the corresponding groups of atoms derived from the tetramer simulations. The initial values of vdW interaction strengths (εi) were taken from the similar atom groups in the Martini force field (63, 79) and then scaled down to the same level of εi values in the HyRes protein model (80, 81) for future compatibility. The overall strengths of vdW interactions were further optimized by a single scaling factor during the optimization of base stacking and pair interactions to produce the structure and chain flexibilities of RNA homopolymers, including the radius of gyration (Rg), end-to-end distance (Re), and persistence length (Lp) (82, 83). For base stacking, the pair-dependent relative strength from the RNAJP model (69) is used (SI Appendix, Table S8), with the overall scaling determined together with vdW and base-pairing interactions. The optimized vdW and A//A stacking strength parameters are given in SI Appendix, Tables S8 and S9.

  The final iConRNA model shows a strong ability to describe the structure and dynamics of model ssRNAs and their salt dependence. Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 summarize simulated Rg and orientational correlation function (OCF; see the SI Appendix, SI Text for definition) of rU30 and Re and Lp of rU40 under different salt concentrations in comparison to available experimental data. Without any base stacking between uracils, rU30 is a random coil (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A) and its OCF profiles are featureless (Fig. 2B), also consistent with experiments (82, 84, 85). Even though Rg (and Lp) from iConRNA simulations is slightly overestimated, strong agreement on their salt dependence suggests that the model captures well the balance between electrostatic and vdW interactions. Curiously, it proved difficult to further reduce the overestimation of Re of rU40 (Fig. 2C). Similar Re deviations were reported in some other CG RNA models (56, 58). Our analysis suggested that this was mainly due to the strong repulsive interactions along the negatively charged backbone, leading to a longer persistence length (Fig. 2D). Weakening of electrostatic interactions could rescue Re of rU40, but this would severely reduce the ability of the model to capture salt dependence of structural properties. With the A//A stacking strength set to 2.05 kcal/mol, iConRNA correctly predicts the helical structure of rA30 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B), and both the OCF profiles and their salt dependences show high consistency with experimental results (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D–F). Besides, stacked bases were found to be slightly deformed because the stacking forces were assigned to the base edges instead of the centers. Thus, the strengths of the dihedral angles B1-B2-B3-A1(G1/C1/U1) and B2-B3-A1(G1/C1/U1)-A2(G2/C3/U3) were increased from 8.0 and 5.0 kcal/mol to 35.0 kcal/mol to reduce the deformation (SI Appendix, Table S6).


#### Base pairing.
  In iConRNA, the ratio between A-U and G-C pairing strength was fixed at about 2:3 as usually done in CG RNA models (69, 86). Both pairs form two pseudohydrogen bonds to help retain the planar geometry (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The final parameters are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S10. Additional adjustments were made to vdW parameters for beads involved in base pairing to improve the geometry (SI Appendix, Table S11). With these parameters, iConRNA reproduces the melting temperatures of both (CAG)20 (87) and hTR hairpin (88) (Fig. 3A) as well as the Beet Western Yellow Virus pseudoknot (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Note that experimental melting profiles frequently contain minor peaks, suggesting the existence of alternative structures and/or multiple unfolding intermediates. iConRNA cannot capture these details, which is not surprising due to the CG nature and absence of noncanonical base-pairing interactions.

  We further examine the ability of iConRNA to fold small structured RNAs. Starting from crystal structures and coil strands, we simulated a series of simple structured RNAs, including 2KOC, 1SDR, 1Q9A, 4FNJ, 1MNX, and 2NC1 (in PDB id). As illustrated in Fig. 3C, the model can basically predict the hairpin or duplex structures with A-form-like helices, although the detailed helical characteristics are not always consistent with those in crystal structures. For complex RNA like 2NC1, some special interactions, including intramolecular cross-base stacking (not the stacking between neighboring bases) and noncanonical base pairing, are necessary to retain the structure, which is not included in iConRNA. As such, the folded structure sampled by iConRNA displays large deviations. The ability of iConRNA to correctly fold small RNAs is notable, as the model has been mainly designed to capture the structural properties of dynamic RNAs. The apparent balance between structure and dynamics provided by iConRNA is an important strength for capturing the complex interplay of various interactions and structural propensities of RNA in phase separation.


### Treatment of Explicit Mg2+ Ions: Concentration, Sequence, and Temperature Dependence.
  Screening of electrostatic interactions due to monovalent ions can be treated effectively using the Debye–Hückel-type electrostatic potential (SI Appendix, Eq. S6). However, such a mean-field treatment is problematic for interactions involving divalent ions such as Mg2+ due to ion–ion correlations and complex coordination with the phosphate backbone of RNA (90–93). Mg2+ can interact with the phosphate group either as a fully hydrated ion, mostly through electrostatics, or through first-shell coordination, which has significant nonelectrostatic components such as polarization and charge transfer (94) (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Furthermore, magnesium binding with RNA depends strongly on the monovalent salt concentration, RNA sequence and structure, and temperature (94).

  To more accurately describe specific Mg2+ interactions in RNA structural dynamics and phase separation, we designed an explicit Mg2+ ion bead in iConRNA, denoted as Mg, which has a comparable volume as the fully hydrated [Mg(H2O)6]2+ cluster with a radius of 0.3 nm and +2 charge (95, 96). Mg is assigned a small ε of 0.1 kcal/mol for all nonspecific vdW interactions. The Mg2+-phosphate interaction follows UP-Mg = ULJ + λP-MgUelec, where ε = 0.5 kcal/mol for ULJ (SI Appendix, Table S11) and Uelec is the unscaled electrostatic interaction (SI Appendix, Eq. S6). λP-Mg is the key scaling parameter that could be calibrated to recapitulate conformational properties of model RNAs under different Mg2+ and salt conditions. Importantly, the complex dependence of Mg2+–RNA interactions could be effectively captured by the excess Mg2+ ions per phosphate, ΔnMg = ΔNMg/NP, where ΔNMg is the number of excess Mg2+ in the RNA-containing sample compared to the bulk buffer and NP is the number of phosphates (94, 95, 97, 98). Note that ΔnMg can be measured or predicted through atomistic simulations, theoretical models, or machine learning tools for a given RNA sequence of interest (83, 94, 95, 97–107). In the following, we derive empirical relationships between optimal λP-Mg and ΔnMg, as well as the dependence of ΔnMg on Mg2+ concentration and temperature.

  Determination of λP-Mg and relation to ΔnMg: We chose to fix the number of Mg in the simulation box as the number of phosphate groups, which can be considered as an excess Mg buffer. This helps overcome the finite size effects under low Mg2+ concentrations, where the number of Mg2+ ions might be too small. The actual effect of Mg2+ concentration (and temperature) on binding to RNA backbone is effectively modeled through scaling the phosphate–Mg interaction strength with λP-Mg. We first determined the optimal λP-Mg by reproducing the [Mg2+] dependence of Rg of rA30 and rU30 (Fig. 4 A and B) (83). The final optimized λP-Mg at various [Mg2+] are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S13. The OCF profiles derived from the simulations at 1 and 2 mM [Mg2+] are in reasonable agreement with the experimental references (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), but display larger discrepancies compared to Mg2+-free conditions (e.g., Fig. 2). This is likely due to that the effective Mg bead in iConRNA is as big as the fully hydrated Mg2+, which does not account for the dehydration of chelated Mg2+ ions and resulting structural differences.

  Experimental studies of rA30 and rU30 have shown that the dependence of ΔnMg on [Mg2+] can be captured using a noncooperative Hill equation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B and Table S12) (83),[4]ΔnMg=FMgMgM1/2n1+MgM1/2n,

  where FMg is the number of excess Mg per phosphate in the limit [Mg]→∞, n is the Hill coefficient, and M1/2 is the competition coefficient (with respect to Na+). FMg is generally ~0.5. Similarly, the optimal λP-Mg vs. [Mg2+] can also be captured using the Hill equation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 C and D). As expected, very different coefficients were obtained in the best fits for poly(rA) and poly(rU). Surprisingly, using the predicted values of ΔnMg and λP-Mg from the Hill equations in the concentration range of 0 to 100 mM Mg2+, we found that a single Hill equation could be applied for both poly(rA) and poly(rU) to fit λP-Mg against ΔnMg (Fig. 4C),[5]λP-Mg=1.255ΔnMg0.1720.6251+ΔnMg0.1720.625.

  It suggests that this Hill equation could be a general rule to determine λP-Mg through ΔnMg without the need for recalibration for different RNAs. For RNA triplet repeats to be studied in this work, we performed all-atom simulations to determine ΔnMg using (CAG)31 as a representative system. The calculated ΔnMg converges well within 100 ns (Fig. 5A), and fits well to the noncooperative Hill equation (Fig. 5B). The fit, given in the insert, will be used for the simulation of all RNA triplet repeats at various [Mg] in this work.

  Temperature Dependence of ΔnMg: The temperature dependence of ΔnMg was further investigated through all-atom simulations of short RNA triplet repeats (CAG)10. The distribution of Mg2+ around phosphate groups was quantified within the temperature range of 303 K to 353 K under 25 mM NaCl and 50 mM MgCl2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13) Similar to previous simulations of polyphosphate (43), the resulting radial distribution functions (RDFs) show that the binding of Mg2+ ions with phosphate groups strengthens with the increase in temperature (Fig. 5C). This increase is particularly pronounced in the inner-sphere binding within 4 Å (the first peak), where the Mg2+ ion was directly coordinated with the oxygen of the phosphate group (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) (108, 109). Interestingly, a linear dependence exists between ΔnMg and temperature (Fig. 5D),[6]ΔnMgT=ΔnMg303K+0.0012T-303,

  where T is the temperature in Kevin. The linear relationship suggests that the entropic effects do dominate the temperature dependence of Mg2+ binding to RNA backbone phosphates as expected. As such, the above relationship should roughly hold for all RNAs, and it will be used to model the temperature dependence of Mg2+–phosphate interaction for all subsequent simulations in this work.

  In our current protocol, the number of Mg is fixed to be the same as the number of phosphate groups and does not change with the box size. In principle, λP-Mg should be reoptimized for different box sizes to reproduce ΔnMg. Therefore, we further evaluated the effect of box size with a fixed number or concentration of Mg on RNA structure. The results show that, without adjusting λP-Mg, only up to 4% change was observed in Rg even with box sizes varying from 15 nm to 50 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Therefore, there is no need to correct for the box size dependence of λP-Mg with a fixed number of explicit Mg beads. The lifetime of Mg bead bound to phosphate is ~300 ps in iConRNA, allowing faster Mg exchange and coupling to RNA conformational dynamics. In summary, we have designed an explicit Mg model and developed a protocol to capture the complex dependence of Mg2+–phosphate interactions. Briefly, the number of Mg beads is first set as the number of phosphate groups. Next, ΔnMg is determined based on available experiments, theories, or atomistic simulations for the RNA of interest. If temperature dependence needs to be considered, ΔnMg at different temperatures could be predicted by Eq. 6. Finally, ΔnMg is converted into λP-Mg following Eq. 5 as an input parameter of the iConRNA simulation.


### Phase Separation of Poly(rA) and Poly(rU).
  Recent works have demonstrated that RNA homopolymers can undergo homotypic phase separation in the presence of multivalent cations and exhibit very different phase behaviors when composed of different nucleotides (42–44, 76, 110). For example, rA30 readily undergoes phase separation under 50 mM MgCl2, while poly(rU) remains soluble under the same conditions (44). We first examined the ability of iConRNA to recapitulate the phase separation of RNA homopolymers in the presence of Mg2+. Starting from dispersed initial states with 240 copies in a cubic box of 200 nm in dimension (50 μM), rU30 remained dispersed and could only form small oligomers (up to pentamers) with temperatures ranging from 303 to 353 K, even with 50 mM MgCl2 (and 25 mM NaCl) (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). In contrast, rA30 clearly shows LCST-type phase separation behaviors (Fig. 6 A and B), consistent with experiments (43). At lower temperatures (300 K and 313 K), only dimers and trimers were obtained. Starting from 323 K, stable droplets can be observed and the number of monomers in the dilute phase decreases rapidly with the increase in temperature (Fig. 6A). We note that experimentally rA30 undergoes phase separation as low as 303 K with 50 mM MgCl2 (44), which indicates that iConRNA slightly underestimates the phase separation propensity of rA30. A possible reason is that only the stacking between neighboring bases is included in our model, such that rA30 is unable to form intermolecular base-stacking interactions potentially involved in the phase separation of rA30 (75).

  Within the condensate, rA30 forms extensive intermolecular interactions, apparently through a wide range of interactions involving backbone phosphate, side chain bases, as well as Mg2+ ions (Fig. 6C). We further characterized the conformations of rA30 inside the dense phase. The results show that RNA chains within the condensates are more extended, exhibiting a larger average value and broader distribution of Rg (Fig. 6D), Furthermore, each individual Rg distribution (the light orange lines) is very close to the average one (bold orange line), which indicates a relatively homogeneous conformation distribution of rA30 inside the condensates. Additionally, we found that condensates are less favorable for the single-strand helical structure of rA30 compared to the dilute phase. OCFs of RNA chains within the condensates exhibit flatter trends and fewer structural details (Fig. 6E), indicating weakened intramolecular base stacking. In the crowded environment of condensates, intermolecular interactions among rA30 chains are enhanced, which results in the partial disruption of local intramolecular base stacking (111).


### Phase Transitions of RNA Triplet Repeats: Sequence and Length Dependence.
  Nucleotide triplet repeats have been implicated in several neurological and neuromuscular disorders, including Huntington’s disease (45, 47, 48). Recently, it was observed that CAG/CUG repeat-containing RNAs could undergo phase separation and gelation, which could be a contributing factor to neurological disease (39). The complex temperature, sequence, and Mg2+ dependence of the phase separation of CAG/CUG repeats and additional percolation transitions within the condensate have been further characterized in detail (43). The formidable complexity of trinucleotide repeat phase separation provides a challenging test case for evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of CG RNA models and for dissecting the molecular driving forces of RNA phase separation.

  We first examine the ability of iConRNA to capture the structures of these triplet repeats in the monomer state. It has been shown that CAG/CUG repeats can form stable hairpins, while the CUU repeat remains a single strand without any higher-order structure (48, 112, 113). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S16A, starting from single strands, iConRNA successfully predicts the hairpin structures of (CAG)31 and (CUG)31 and the disordered single strand of (CUU)31. Consistent with experimental observations (113), CAG hairpins were observed to be more stable than CUG ones. The backbone RMSD is 11.8 ± 3.5 Å for CAG, but 34.1 ± 12.4 Å for CUG. Although only canonical A-U and G-C pairing interactions are included in our model, some semistable A-A pairs can be found in CAG hairpins due to neighboring A-U and G-C pairs and base-stacking interactions, while U-U pairs are very dynamic in CUG hairpins.

  We then estimated the phase diagrams of (CAG)31 RNA as a function of magnesium concentration (Fig. 7A) and RNA concentration (Fig. 7B) by examining the cluster size distribution and concentration fluctuation from 10 to 15 μs simulations under series of different temperature, [RNA] and [Mg2+] conditions (see the SI Appendix, Figs. S18–S21 and SI Text for additional details). Here, ΔnMg and its temperature dependence were first determined through all-atom simulations (Fig. 5). Note that the current simulation timescales are not always sufficient to reach well equilibrated phase coexistence in all conditions (e.g., SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Coupled with the modest system size (100 copies of RNAs), we cannot resolve the percolation vs. phase transitions (55). Instead, we relied on the concentration fluctuation and appearance of a separate peak of large clusters to estimate whether phase transition likely occurred during the simulation or not (SI Appendix, Figs. S18, S19, and S21). The resulting phase diagrams show that, similar to rA30, (CAG)31 undergoes LCST-type phase separation, where the transition temperature decreases with the increase of [Mg], indicating the intrinsic entropic contribution of phosphate–Mg interactions to phase separation. Notably, an excellent agreement was observed between simulations and experiments for both [Mg] dependence and [RNA] dependence (43). For the [Mg] dependence, the critical temperature is slightly overestimated at the low-Mg2+ region (below 25 mM) but slightly underestimated at the high-Mg2+ region above 50 mM. It might result from the corresponding under- or overestimation of excess Mg2+ binding in the all-atom simulations. In the 1-Φ region, at temperatures close to the phase boundary, there always exist small RNA oligomers (from dimer to pentamer, Left panel in SI Appendix, Fig. S16B), which are dynamic and undergo fast exchange with monomers in the dilute phase.

  We further evaluated the ability of iConRNA to capture the length dependence of the LCST-type phase transition of CAG repeats observed experimentally (43). As summarized in Fig. 7B, decreasing the RNA length to 20 repeats dramatically weakens the propensity of CAG repeats phase separation. In particular, the critical temperature increases throughout the concentration range examined. For example, it increases from 40 to 70 °C at 100 μM RNAs in simulation, while in experiments, it increases from 38.0 °C for 100 μM (CAG)31 RNA to 59.9 °C for 155 μM (CAG)20. In addition, under all the conditions tested for (CAG)20 and (CAG)31 repeats, (CAG)10 repeats did not undergo any phase separation. For example, under the most favorable conditions, 80 °C, 100 μM RNAs, and 50 mM MgCl2, only a few dimers and trimers were found throughout the simulation box (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).

  Experimentally, it was reported that the purine-to-uracil substitution could suppress LCST phase transitions (43). Compared to (CAG)31, (CUG)31 exhibited slightly lower phase separation propensity, and (CUU)31 did not undergo phase separation under the same conditions. iConRNA simulations correctly predicted a significant reduction in the phase separation propensity of (CUU)31, which requires a much higher temperature (and thus stronger Mg2+/phosphate interactions) to phase-separate in simulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S16C). However, (CUG)31 shows a very similar phase boundary with (CAG)31, indicating the weakening effect of A-to-U substitution for (CAG)31 is not fully captured by the current model. The limited ability of iConRNA to capture the negative effect of purine-to-uracil substitution in phase separation of trinucleotide repeats suggests that there is still room for improvement in balancing various base-mediated interactions within iConRNA, particularly regarding the strength of nonspecific vdW interactions between uracil bases. Furthermore, the excess binding Mg2+ derived from atomistic simulations may have been overestimated (Fig. 5), leading to overestimated λP-Mg and Mg binding in iConRNA simulations.


### Molecular Driving Forces of RNA Phase Separation: The Role of RNA Folding.
  The ability of iConRNA to accurately recapitulate the length, concentration, and Mg2+ level dependence of RNA phase behaviors allows the dissection of the contributions of various molecular forces toward driving phase separation. Intermolecular contact analysis indicates that within the condensate, rA30 forms extensive intermolecular interactions through phosphate–Mg electrostatic interactions and base-mediated interchain vdW interactions (Fig. 8A). The latter class of interactions is the distinguishing feature between rA30 and rU30. The base–base interactions observed in the droplet resemble A-A stacking and noncanonical A-A pairing (Fig. 8B), although iConRNA does not contain these terms explicitly. In addition, there is a significant level of base–sugar interactions in rA30 condensates. Clearly, due to weaker base-mediated interactions, phosphate–Mg electrostatic interactions, the major driver of oligomerization and a common feature of RNA backbone, are not sufficient to drive phase transitions of rU30 by themselves. For CAG repeats, similar to poly(rA), two major contributions come from the Mg-mediated electrostatic interactions (P-Mg-P) and base–base interactions. The former indicates the necessity of divalent Mg2+ ions, which bridge RNA backbones to promote their condensation (Fig. 8C). Besides, this bridging effect also results in the preference for parallel arrangements of multiple hairpins, further promoting the probability of intermolecular base pairing. The base–base interactions were observed to be dominated by G-C pairing, which occurs through loop, overhang, and tail regions of hairpins (Fig. 8E). The latter promotes the hairpin to duplex transition during phase separation and further arresting and aging to gel. As a result, the RNA becomes more expanded within the condensate.

  The apparent competition between hairpin and duplex formation suggests an important role in the intramolecular folding of RNA in phase separation. This is particularly evident when examining the temperature dependence of the distributions of P-Mg-P interactions and base pairing in condensates. Below the melting temperature, the hairpin structures of CAG repeats are stable in both dilute and dense phases, where over 90% G/C residues form intramolecular pairing (Fig. 8D). Only less than 10% of base pairing is intermolecular, mainly found between loops and overhangs and between two overhangs. Therefore, Mg2+-mediated electrostatic interactions are the major driver of phase separation. At 368 K, above the (CAG)31 melting temperature, the hairpin structures were partially denatured and more dynamic, which resulted in dramatic increases of intermolecular base pairing in the condensates, to slightly above 50% (Fig. 8D). Interestingly, even though P-Mg interactions are also stronger at higher temperature, they account for a smaller percentage of intermolecular interactions at 368 K, whereas the base–base interactions become more abundant (Fig. 8C). Interestingly, when we anneal the condensates formed at 368 K to 333 K, we observed a significant increase of additional intermolecular base pairing, while Mg2+-mediated backbone interactions were further reduced (Fig. 8 C and D). The implication is that the condensate would undergo gelation, and the phase transition is not reversible as observed experimentally (43), even though our current simulations are not long enough to directly observe full gelation.


## Discussion
  We have developed an intermediate-resolution coarse-grained RNA model with explicit magnesium ions, named iConRNA, for molecular simulation of dynamic RNAs and their phase transitions. Representing each nucleotide using 6 or 7 CG beads, iConRNA is designed to explicitly describe a diverse range of interactions that RNA can make through its backbone phosphate and side chain bases, including electrostatics, vdW interactions, base stacking, and base pairing. With careful parameterization using theory, atomistic simulation, and experimental data, the model is able to capture the essential conformational features of flexible RNAs and, at the same time, fold small RNAs, including tetraloop, duplex, and hairpin. One of the major challenges in RNA modeling is describing the interactions with Mg2+ ions, which are important in RNA structure and phase separation and display complex structure, ion concentration, and temperature dependence. For this, we developed an efficient framework for including explicit effective Mg2+ ions in iConRNA. The sequence, concentration, and temperature dependence of Mg2+-mediated interactions are captured by parameterizing a scaling factor for effective Mg2+ ion/phosphate interaction strengths, based on excess Mg2+ binding to RNA. The latter property can be measured experimentally or derived from atomistic simulations and/or theory. The resulting iConRNA with explicit Mg2+ successfully captures the Mg2+ dependence of conformational properties of flexible RNAs and reproduces a wide range of nontrivial phase separation properties of homopolymer and trinucleotide repeat RNAs, including length, sequence, [Mg2+], and temperature dependence.

  The success of iConRNA in recapitulating the phase behaviors of homopolymer and trinucleotide repeat RNAs allows careful dissection of how the interplay of various backbone and base-mediated interactions contributes toward driving phase separation. The analysis reveals the major roles of intermolecular base pairing and Mg2+-bridged backbone phosphate interactions in RNA phase separation. For homopolymers, nonspecific base–base and base–ribose interactions are mainly responsible for supplementing Mg2+/phosphate interactions to drive phase separation, such as for rA30 vs. rU30. For trinucleotide repeats, intermolecular base pairing is the major driving force besides Mg2+/phosphate interactions in phase separation. RNA folding has a central role in controlling the competition between intramolecular and intermolecular base pairing, which is also a key process underlying the complex temperature and Mg2+ dependence of the phase diagram and likely gelation and reversibility. We note that cellular RNA foci form at room temperature, which is usually below the melting temperature of (partially) structured RNAs. The dynamic and structural heterogeneity of driver RNAs, as well as how these properties respond to cellular conditions and/or binding of ions and other small and macromolecules, can be expected to be crucial to the formation, regulation, and function of RNA foci. As an intermediate-resolution CG model, iConRNA is uniquely positioned to work hand-in-hand with experimental studies to understand the molecular basis of RNA condensation and its role in various biological processes (25).

  Our benchmarks also revealed areas for further improvement of iConRNA. In particular, iConRNA only considers the canonical WC base pairs and neglects the formation of noncanonical base pairs (114–116). It is a challenge to mimic each kind of base pairing by specific interactions. A simple and practical strategy may be to introduce a general hydrogen bond formation ability into each potential donor and acceptor that could contribute to the formation of noncanonical base pairing. However, it has proven challenging to balance the strength and propensity of different types of base pairing at the CG level. In addition, although the effective magnesium did perform well in the benchmarks summarized in this work, it is still limited in describing chelated Mg2+ binding through the inner sphere. Using the same effective concentration and temperature dependence for a given RNA also neglects the difference in Mg2+ binding depending on the local structural environment. The Debye–Hückel treatment of electrostatic interactions also has important limitations in capturing the complex environmental dependence of water and ion screening effects in dilute vs. condensed phases or intricate effects arising from differences in charge group geometries and sizes (117). The latter may be particularly important when integrating iConRNA with the HyRes protein model. Despite these limitations, the successes demonstrated in this work support the great potential of using intermediate-resolution CG models for simulating RNA dynamic conformation and phase separation. The current iConRNA model, albeit with limitations, already provides a powerful tool for studying RNA condensates with many greater details compared to the existing 1 or 2-bead models.


## Materials and Methods
  All atomistic simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2022 package (118–120) in the NPT ensemble. The initial structures of RNAs were generated using the Nucleic Acids Builder module available in the AmberTools package (121). The AMBER99 force field (122) with TIP3P waters (123) was used in combination with the parmbsc0 (124) and the parmchiOL3 (125) corrections for RNA. The “microMg” parameters from Grotz et al (126) were used for Mg2+ with the parameters of Cl− from Mamatkulov–Schwierz (127). Packmol (128) and CHARMM-GUI (129–131) were used to build the initial simulation boxes. Periodic boundary conditions (PBC) and the particle–mesh Ewald (PME) algorithm (132, 133) were applied to calculate the long-range electrostatic interactions, while the cut-off of both the short-range electrostatic and vdW potential was set to 1.1 nm. The V-rescale thermostat (134) was used to maintain the temperature with a coupling time constant of 1.0 ps, while the Parrinello–Rahman method (135) was used to maintain the pressure at 1 bar with a coupling time constant of 5.0 ps. All CG simulations were performed using OpenMM 8.1.0 (136) in the NVT ensemble. Langevin dynamics was used with a friction coefficient of 0.1 ps−1. In-house Python packages were used to create initial CG RNA structures or convert atomistic models into iConRNA representation. More details about simulations and analysis can be found in SI Appendix, SI Text.
