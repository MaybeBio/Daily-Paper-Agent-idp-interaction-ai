## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, data availability) as provided by the reviewer.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological validity, evidence quality, and clarity of claims as presented in the supplied text. No external data or unpublished materials were consulted.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DIRseq, a sequence-based method for predicting drug-interacting residues in intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs). The method is an adaptation of their earlier SeqDYN approach, which predicts transverse relaxation rates (R2) from sequence. DIRseq converts predicted R2 values into drug-interacting propensities via a sigmoid function. The authors validate DIRseq against NMR chemical shift perturbation (CSP) data for four IDPs and demonstrate applications in defining drug-binding motifs, guiding molecular dynamics simulations, and virtual screening.
- **Visible evidence base** The manuscript includes figures (Figures 1-5), figure supplements, and supplementary files referenced in the text. Numerical source data are stated to be available as Figure 2-source data 1, Figure 3-source data 1, and Figure 4-source data 1. The web server and source code are publicly accessible.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** The actual figures, figure supplements, and supplementary files were not provided to this reviewer. Numerical data files were not inspected. The manuscript text alone is insufficient to independently verify the quantitative claims, including correlation coefficients, true positive/false positive counts, and the visual matches between predicted and experimental data.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript presents a conceptually interesting and potentially useful tool for the IDP drug discovery community. The idea of leveraging sequence-predicted R2 values as a proxy for drug-interaction propensity is novel and builds logically on the authors' prior work. The validation against four well-characterized IDP systems is a reasonable first step. However, the evidence as presented in the text alone is not fully convincing. The parameter adjustments from SeqDYN to DIRseq are described as motivated by physical reasoning, but the quantitative justification is partially undermined by the fact that one of the four changes (Met downgrade) decreases both performance metrics. The manuscript would benefit from a more systematic and transparent evaluation of parameter sensitivity, a clearer statement of the method's limitations, and a more rigorous statistical treatment of the agreement between predictions and experimental data. The claims of broad applicability, while plausible, are not yet fully established from the provided evidence.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of IDP biology, drug discovery, and computational biophysics would be interested. Specifically, groups working on targeting IDPs with small molecules, those using NMR or MD to characterize IDP-drug interactions, and developers of sequence-based prediction tools would find this method a potentially valuable complement to existing experimental and computational approaches. The web server availability increases its potential utility for a broad community.
- **Major Strengths**
  - The conceptual premise is clear and well-motivated: leveraging the relationship between intramolecular interactions (which elevate R2) and intermolecular interactions with drugs.
  - The method is fast and requires only sequence information, which is a significant practical advantage over experimental and simulation-based approaches.
  - The validation against multiple IDP systems with different drug-binding characteristics adds some weight to the claims.
  - The authors are transparent about the parameter adjustments and test alternative models, which is commendable.
  - The web server and source code availability are strong assets for community adoption.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **R1-M1** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes): The quantitative validation of DIRseq against CSP data is not fully presented in the text. The authors report correlation coefficients and TP-FP counts, but without the actual figures and source data, it is impossible to assess the quality of the match. The claim that predictions "match well" is subjective and needs to be supported by a more rigorous statistical framework, such as confidence intervals for the correlation coefficients or a permutation test to assess significance.
  - **R1-M2** (Severity: Major, Blocking: Yes): The method's parameter choices appear to be tuned on the same four systems used for validation. The authors state that the Met downgrade decreased performance but was kept for "physical reasons." This raises a concern about overfitting and the generalizability of the method. A clearer separation between training and test sets, or a more robust cross-validation scheme, is needed to establish that the method's performance is not an artifact of parameter tuning on a small, specific dataset.
  - **R1-M3** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No): The claim that DIRseq can serve as a complement to CSP and MD for assigning drug-interacting residues is plausible but not fully demonstrated. The examples in Figure 3 and Figure 4 are presented as case studies, but the criteria for "consensus identification" are not defined. A more systematic protocol for combining CSP and DIRseq data, with clear decision rules, would strengthen this claim.
  - **R1-M4** (Severity: Major, Blocking: No): The application to virtual screening is only briefly illustrated with a single example (EGCG docking to p53). The authors do not provide any quantitative assessment of the docking results, such as docking scores or comparison to known binding modes. This section reads as speculative and does not yet establish the utility of DIRseq for guiding virtual screening.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **R1-m1** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Clarity): The description of the sigmoid function parameters (s1 and s2) is brief. A more detailed explanation of how these parameters affect the shape of the conversion and the resulting predictions would be helpful.
  - **R1-m2** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Reproducibility): The manuscript states that the source code is available, but the specific dependencies and instructions for running the code are not described. A brief usage example or a link to a documentation page would improve reproducibility.
  - **R1-m3** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Presentation): The use of "WK" and "WFT" notation for motifs (e.g., L22WK24) is unconventional and could be confusing. Standard single-letter amino acid notation with positions (e.g., L22-W23-K24) would be clearer.
  - **R1-m4** (Severity: Minor, Axis: Completeness): The Discussion mentions that "drug-binding motifs are anchored on one or more aromatic residues." This is an interesting observation, but the authors do not quantify how often this is the case across their dataset. A simple count or percentage would strengthen this point.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
  - Lack of a clear training/validation split for the parameter adjustments.
  - Absence of statistical significance testing for the reported correlations.
  - The virtual screening application is not quantitatively evaluated.
  - The criteria for combining CSP and DIRseq data are not defined.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality**: The concept of using sequence-predicted R2 as a proxy for drug-interaction propensity is original and distinct from existing sequence-based predictors. The adaptation of SeqDYN to a new application is a creative step.
  - **Scientific importance**: The problem of identifying drug-interacting residues in IDPs is of high importance for drug discovery. A fast, sequence-based method could have significant practical impact. However, the importance is somewhat tempered by the limited validation and the potential for overfitting.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership**: The work bridges IDP biology, computational biology, and medicinal chemistry. The web server and clear writing style could attract readers from these fields. However, the technical details of the method may be less accessible to experimentalists.
  - **Technical soundness**: The underlying idea is sound, but the technical execution has weaknesses. The parameter tuning is not rigorously separated from validation, and the statistical analysis is insufficient. The method's performance on a small number of systems does not yet establish its generalizability.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists**: The manuscript is generally well-written and the logic is easy to follow. The abstract and introduction are accessible. However, the results section assumes familiarity with NMR and MD concepts, which may be a barrier for some readers.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core idea is promising and the tool could be valuable, but the current evidence does not fully establish the method's accuracy and generalizability. The authors need to provide a more rigorous validation, address the overfitting concern, and provide a clearer demonstration of the practical applications.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that DIRseq predictions "match well" with CSP data is not quantitatively supported in the text provided.
- The claim that DIRseq can be used to guide virtual screening is not supported by any quantitative results.
- The claim that the parameter adjustments are "for physical reasons" is not sufficient to justify a change that decreases performance metrics.
- The generalizability of the method to IDPs beyond the four tested systems is not established.
- The claim that DIRseq can help define fragments for MD and NMR studies is plausible but not demonstrated with specific examples.
