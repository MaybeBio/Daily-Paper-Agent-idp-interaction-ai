## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusions) without figures, tables, or supplementary material
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific claims, methodological soundness, and interpretive logic as presented in the text; visual evidence (figures) and supplementary data not available for verification
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of a two-component polymer system with small-molecule cosolutes to show that condensate morphology (core–shell, dewetted, mixed) can be controlled by tuning additive–component interaction strengths and stoichiometry. They rationalize observed transitions using normalized second virial coefficients computed from two-chain umbrella sampling, and extend the framework to explain morphological changes in initially mixed condensates.
- **Visible evidence base** Text descriptions of simulation results, methodological parameters, and qualitative references to figures (Figure 1–5, S1–S7); no actual figure data, numerical values beyond those in methods, or supplementary content provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** All figures and supplementary figures; numerical data for virial coefficients, PMF curves, and morphological metrics (d_ABCOM, intersection angles); simulation trajectory details; convergence checks; parameter scan tables

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a timely and important question, namely how small-molecule cosolutes can modulate the morphology of multicomponent biomolecular condensates. The authors propose a conceptually appealing framework based on effective two-chain interactions, quantified via second virial coefficients, to rationalize and predict morphological transitions. The simulation methodology is appropriate and the systematic parameter sweeps are commendable. However, the central claim that the b22' ratio serves as a predictive criterion for morphology is undermined by acknowledged discrepancies between two-chain predictions and multichain simulations, which the authors attribute to stoichiometry effects without a fully quantitative resolution. The manuscript would be strengthened by a more rigorous treatment of these discrepancies, additional validation of the coarse-grained model against experimental observables, and clearer presentation of quantitative results. The work is likely to interest researchers in biomolecular condensates, soft matter, and computational biophysics, but the current evidence base is insufficient to fully establish the proposed framework as a general predictive tool.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying biomolecular condensates, particularly those interested in multiphase organization and its regulation by small molecules or metabolites; computational soft matter scientists working on coarse-grained models of phase separation; experimentalists seeking design rules for controlling condensate morphology in vitro or in cells. The framework connecting additive interactions to effective chain interactions could inform both mechanistic understanding and engineering of condensate-based materials.
- **Major strengths** 1) The question is timely and of broad significance, given the growing recognition that condensate internal organization is functionally important. 2) The systematic parameter sweeps across interaction strengths and stoichiometries are thorough and provide a rich phenomenology. 3) The use of second virial coefficients as a theoretical framework to connect microscopic interactions to mesoscale morphology is elegant and potentially transferable. 4) The extension to initially mixed condensates broadens the applicability of the findings. 5) The writing is generally clear and the logical flow from binary systems to additive-modulated systems is well structured.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Claim-evidence alignment
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the ratio b22AB'/b22BB' "generally serves as a useful metric for capturing the relative strength of A–B versus B–B chain interactions and for explaining the observed morphological transitions" and that when the ratio exceeds one, core–shell structures form, while when it falls below one, dewetting occurs.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Morphological Transitions in Condensates Mediated by Interaction Strength with Small-Molecule Cosolutes"; Figures 2c and S3 (not visible)
  - **Concern** The manuscript acknowledges multiple discrepancies between the b22' ratio predictions and the actual condensate morphologies, particularly at high λAC or λBC values and at high partition ratios. These discrepancies are attributed to stoichiometry effects, but the authors do not provide a quantitative criterion that incorporates stoichiometry into the predictive framework. As presented, the b22' ratio is not a reliable predictor across the full parameter space explored, which weakens the central claim of the paper. The authors state that "a few notable discrepancies were identified" and that "even in regimes where the effective interaction strengths determined from our two-chain calculations predict dewetting, the condensates exhibit a core–shell morphology instead." This is a significant limitation that is not fully resolved.
  - **Why it matters** The central intellectual contribution of the paper is the claim that effective two-chain interactions, as captured by b22', can predict and rationalize multicomponent condensate morphology. If this criterion fails in a substantial region of parameter space, the framework is incomplete. The authors need to either refine the criterion to include stoichiometry explicitly, or clearly delineate the boundaries of validity of the b22' ratio approach.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a quantitative analysis that incorporates stoichiometry (e.g., a modified criterion that includes the number of C particles per chain, or a phase diagram in the (λAC, λBC, concentration) space showing where the b22' ratio prediction holds and where it fails). Alternatively, demonstrate that the discrepancies are confined to a narrow parameter range and provide a physical explanation for why the criterion fails there.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Methodological validation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that their coarse-grained model captures "the essential physics" of multicomponent condensate morphology and that the framework "can also be extended to macromolecular regulators, such as RNAs and proteins."
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods section "Molecular Model and Simulation Methodology"; Conclusions
  - **Concern** The coarse-grained model uses a single-bead representation for both polymer chains and small molecules, with interaction parameters (λ) that are not mapped to any specific chemical system. While the authors cite prior work using similar models, there is no validation of the model against experimental data for any specific condensate system. The claim that the framework can be extended to macromolecular regulators is speculative, as the model does not account for multivalency, sequence specificity, or the structural complexity of real proteins and RNAs. The authors acknowledge that macromolecules "often exhibit multivalency" but do not address how this would alter the proposed framework.
  - **Why it matters** For the framework to be useful beyond a generic phenomenological description, the authors need to demonstrate that the model captures quantitative or at least semi-quantitative features of real systems. Without any experimental comparison or mapping to specific molecular interactions, the predictive power of the framework for biological contexts remains unclear.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a mapping of the coarse-grained parameters to a specific experimental system (e.g., a known protein–RNA condensate) and show that the model reproduces observed morphologies or phase behavior. Alternatively, clearly state the limitations of the model and temper the claims of extensibility to macromolecular regulators.

  - **Claim pointer** The authors describe "morphological transitions between, e.g., core–shell and dewetted structures" and quantify these via d_ABCOM and intersection angles.

While d_ABCOM and intersection angles are mentioned, no numerical values are provided in the text. The reader cannot assess the magnitude of the observed effects, the statistical significance of the differences between conditions, or the robustness of the morphological classifications. The claims of morphological transitions need to be supported by clear numerical evidence, including distributions of d_ABCOM, intersection angles, and their uncertainties. Without this, the reader cannot evaluate the strength of the conclusions.
  - **Resolution test** Provide quantitative values for d_ABCOM and intersection angles for representative conditions, with error bars, and clearly state the criteria used to classify morphologies. Include these in the main text or in a clearly referenced table.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 1) The predictive power of the b22' ratio is not quantitatively established across the full parameter space; the acknowledged discrepancies need to be resolved or explicitly bounded. 2) The model is not validated against any experimental system, limiting the biological relevance of the claims. 3) Quantitative results (d_ABCOM, intersection angles, b22' values) are not presented in the text, making it impossible to assess the magnitude and significance of the reported effects.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The work is original in its systematic exploration of additive-modulated morphology in multicomponent condensates and its attempt to provide a quantitative framework based on second virial coefficients. However, the conceptual idea that relative interaction strengths determine multiphase morphology is not entirely new, and the authors build on prior work. Scientific importance: The question of how small molecules regulate condensate morphology is of significant current interest, and a predictive framework would be valuable. However, the incomplete validation and unresolved discrepancies limit the immediate impact. Interdisciplinary readership: The topic bridges soft matter physics, biophysics, and cell biology, and the writing is accessible to a broad audience. Technical soundness: The simulation methodology is appropriate, but the lack of quantitative results and the unresolved discrepancies between two-chain and multichain predictions undermine the technical robustness of the conclusions. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well-written and the logic is clear, but the reliance on qualitative descriptions of morphology and the absence of quantitative summaries make it harder for nonspecialists to evaluate the strength of the evidence.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript addresses an important question and presents a potentially useful framework, but the central predictive claim is not fully established from the provided evidence. The authors need to provide quantitative results, resolve or bound the discrepancies between two-chain predictions and multichain simulations, and ideally validate the model against experimental data.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the b22' ratio "generally serves as a useful metric" for predicting morphology is not fully supported, as the authors acknowledge multiple discrepancies that are not quantitatively resolved.
- The claim that the framework "can also be extended to macromolecular regulators, such as RNAs and proteins" is speculative and not supported by any evidence in the manuscript.
- The claim that "morphological transitions" are observed is based on qualitative visual inspection; without quantitative metrics, this claim cannot be fully evaluated.
- The statement that "stoichiometry and interaction strength jointly determine the condensate morphology" is plausible but not quantitatively demonstrated; the relative contributions of each factor are not separated.
