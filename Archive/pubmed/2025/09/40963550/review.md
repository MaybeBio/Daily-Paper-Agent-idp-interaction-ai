## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text, including abstract, introduction, results and discussion, conclusions, methods, and supplementary figure descriptions.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological rigor, validity of claims relative to presented evidence, and alignment with Nature-style criteria. Editorial and stylistic issues are noted only where they affect scientific clarity.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use Martini 3 coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations to show that RGG proteins phase separate on neutral POPC membranes, that scaling protein–protein interactions by a factor of 0.975 reproduces experimentally observed thermal expansion, that increasing negatively charged lipid (POPS) fractions enhances membrane wetting when protein–protein interactions are unscaled, but that with scaled interactions, high POPS fractions oppose condensation and dissolve pre-formed condensates. Experimental fluorescence microscopy on suspended planar membranes confirms that adding 20 mol% DOPS reduces protein partitioning between condensed and dilute phases. The authors conclude that the balance between protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions determines whether membranes promote or oppose condensation.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures 1–6 (described in text), supplementary figures S1 and S2 (described in text), supplementary movies S1–S9 (referenced), methods section, and data availability statement.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Actual figure images, supplementary figure images, simulation parameter files, force field modification details, experimental phase diagram data, and full trajectory analysis details were not provided. Only textual descriptions of figures and methods are available for assessment.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and timely question: how the balance between protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions governs condensation of intrinsically disordered proteins on membrane surfaces. The combination of coarse-grained simulations with experimental validation is commendable and the central finding, that charged lipids can either promote or oppose condensation depending on interaction strengths, is potentially significant. However, several technical aspects require clarification and strengthening before the conclusions can be fully accepted. The calibration procedure for the scaling factor α is described but the quantitative comparison to experimental data is not fully detailed. The relationship between the simulation timescales and experimental timescales, the sensitivity of results to the tethering protocol, and the statistical robustness of the reported differences need more thorough treatment. The experimental validation is limited to a single condition (20 mol% DOPS) and would benefit from a broader comparison across the range of conditions explored in simulations.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying biomolecular condensation, phase separation, membrane biophysics, and the molecular mechanisms of membrane-associated processes such as endocytosis, T-cell signaling, and synaptic organization. The work is also relevant to the broader community interested in coarse-grained simulation methodology and the development of calibrated force fields for intrinsically disordered proteins.
- **Major strengths** 1) The study addresses a biologically important and mechanistically under-explored question. 2) The combination of simulation and experiment is powerful and provides complementary evidence. 3) The systematic exploration of protein–protein interaction scaling and lipid charge fractions is thorough. 4) The observation that charged lipids can dissolve pre-formed condensates is novel and potentially important. 5) The work is clearly written and the logic is easy to follow.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness of calibration
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that a scaling factor of α = 0.975 provides the best match to experimental phase behavior, based on comparison of condensed-phase density profiles and partitioning between phases.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "Scaling protein–protein interactions to calibrate the simulation against experimental data"; Figure 2; Figure S1
  - **Concern** The calibration procedure is described qualitatively. The authors state that at α = 0.975, the condensed-phase density decreased with increasing temperature in agreement with experimental data, and that α = 0.98 gave a "nearly vertical" boundary while α = 0.9725 produced a "nearly melted" state. However, no quantitative comparison between simulated and experimental density values, slopes, or transition temperatures is provided. The experimental data are described as "rough estimates" with "significant noise," but the criteria for selecting 0.975 over other values are not defined in a reproducible way. It is unclear whether the comparison is based on visual inspection, statistical metrics, or some other criterion.
  - **Why it matters** The entire subsequent analysis depends on this calibration. If the scaling factor is not rigorously justified, all conclusions about the balance between protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions are built on an uncertain foundation. The choice of α = 0.975 appears to be made from a small set of discrete values (0.9725, 0.975, 0.98), and it is unclear whether intermediate values or a broader range were tested.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a quantitative comparison between simulated and experimental phase diagrams, including numerical values for condensed-phase density as a function of temperature for each α value tested. Define a clear metric for agreement (e.g., root-mean-square deviation, overlap of confidence intervals) and show that α = 0.975 is optimal by this metric. Report the uncertainty in the experimental measurements and propagate this into the calibration.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Statistical robustness of simulation results
  - **Claim pointer** The authors report differences in condensate morphology, protein–lipid contacts, and lipid diffusion across conditions (0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5 PS fractions; α = 1 vs. α = 0.975; tethered vs. untethered).
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, sections "Introducing charged lipids increased condensate wetting of the bilayer", "The addition of charged lipids opposed condensation on the membrane when protein–protein interactions were scaled", "Charged lipids keep protein condensates on the membrane in the absence of a tethering constraint"; Figures 3, 4, 5; Figure S2
  - **Concern** The manuscript reports that only two independent simulations were performed for the initial coarsening study (duplicated coordinates with new velocities). For the subsequent condition studies, it is unclear how many independent replicates were run. The reported differences in Ravg, contact numbers, and diffusion coefficients are presented as mean values with standard deviations, but the number of replicates, the method for calculating standard deviations (e.g., across time windows vs. across independent simulations), and whether differences are statistically significant are not stated. For example, the claim that 0.1 PS produces a 7% increase in Ravg and 0.3 PS a 9% increase may or may not be statistically meaningful given the reported variability.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusions depend on quantitative differences between conditions. Without information on replicate numbers and statistical testing, it is impossible to assess whether the observed trends are robust or could arise from stochastic variation in a single or few trajectories.
  - **Resolution test** Report the number of independent simulations for each condition. Perform and report appropriate statistical tests (e.g., t-tests, ANOVA, or non-parametric equivalents) for all quantitative comparisons. Distinguish between uncertainty from time-averaging within a single trajectory and uncertainty from independent replicates. Provide confidence intervals for key metrics such as Ravg, contact numbers, and diffusion coefficients.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Generalizability of experimental validation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "Condensates investigated using an in vitro model demonstrated similar behavior, where partitioning between the condensed and dilute phases was reduced in the presence of charged lipids."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "In experiments, adding charged lipids to the membrane reduces the density of RGG proteins in the condensed phase"; Figure 6
  - **Concern** The experimental validation is limited to a single condition: 20 mol% DOPS versus 0 mol% DOPS, at a single protein concentration and temperature. The simulations explore a range of PS fractions (0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) and two scaling conditions. The experimental data confirm the direction of the effect (reduced partitioning with charged lipids) but do not test the predicted non-monotonic or threshold behavior that might be expected from the simulations. Additionally, the experimental system uses DOPS while simulations use POPS; while chemically similar, the difference in acyl chain composition could affect interactions.
  - **Why it matters** The claim of "similar behavior" between simulation and experiment is only weakly supported by a single comparison point. A more thorough experimental exploration across multiple DOPS fractions would strengthen the claim that the simulations capture the underlying physics.
  - **Resolution test** Perform additional experimental measurements at intermediate DOPS fractions (e.g., 5%, 10%, 30%) to map the partitioning behavior as a function of charged lipid content. If this is not feasible, clearly state the limitation and temper the claim of "similar behavior" to "consistent with the direction of the simulated effect at the single condition tested."
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Interpretation of tethering effects
  - **Claim pointer** The authors conclude that "removing the tether between proteins and membranes could strengthen phase separation" based on simulations where untethered condensates at 0.3 PS had more proteins in the primary condensate and fewer in the dilute phase compared to tethered conditions.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "Charged lipids keep protein condensates on the membrane in the absence of a tethering constraint"; Figure 5
  - **Concern** The tethering protocol used in the main simulations (constant force between the protein N-terminus and the nearest lipid headgroup) is a strong constraint that may not accurately represent physiological membrane binding. The comparison between tethered and untethered conditions is interesting, but the interpretation that tethering "promotes disassembly" is potentially confounded by the fact that the untethered condensate can also detach from the membrane entirely. The authors note that at 0 and 0.1 PS, the untethered condensate expanded in 3D and lost membrane contact, but the implications of this for the interpretation of the 0.3 and 0.5 PS conditions are not fully discussed.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusion that tethering promotes disassembly has implications for understanding how membrane anchoring regulates condensation in cells. If the effect is an artifact of the specific tethering protocol, the biological interpretation would be different.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed analysis of the untethered systems, including the fraction of proteins that remain membrane-bound versus those that desorb. Discuss how the choice of tethering strength and geometry affects the results. Consider performing simulations with a range of tethering strengths to map the transition from membrane-promoted to membrane-opposed condensation.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity of methods
  - **Affected element** Simulation methods, tethering description
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods, "Tethering" subsection
  - **Issue** The description of the tethering force is brief. The force constant, the choice of pulling coordinate, and how the "nearest lipid headgroup" is determined are not specified in the main text.
  - **Required correction** Provide the force constant value, the functional form of the pulling potential, and the frequency of updating the nearest lipid headgroup reference. Include this information in the main text or refer explicitly to the SI files where it is provided.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Statistical reporting
  - **Affected element** Diffusion coefficient measurements
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "Introducing charged lipids increased condensate wetting of the bilayer"; Figure 3D
  - **Issue** The diffusion coefficients are reported as averages with standard deviations, but it is unclear whether the standard deviation represents variation between time windows or between independent simulations. The number of windows and the window length are not stated in the main text.
  - **Required correction** Clarify the source of the standard deviation and report the number of windows and their duration. If possible, also report the number of independent simulations used for the diffusion analysis.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Experimental details
  - **Affected element** Experimental phase diagram
  - **Evidence pointer** Results and discussion, section "In experiments, adding charged lipids to the membrane reduces the density of RGG proteins in the condensed phase"; Figure 6
  - **Issue** The experimental section describes the measurement of partition coefficients but does not provide details on how the phase diagram was constructed, how many independent experiments were performed, or how the fluorescence intensity was calibrated to protein concentration.
  - **Required correction** Provide details on the number of experimental replicates, the calibration procedure for converting fluorescence intensity to protein concentration, and the criteria for defining the phase boundary.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Figure accessibility
  - **Affected element** All figures
  - **Evidence pointer** Figures 1–6
  - **Issue** The figure descriptions in the text are clear, but the actual figure images were not available for review. It is not possible to assess the quality of the data presentation, the clarity of the labels, or the appropriateness of the color schemes.
  - **Required correction** Ensure that all figures are self-contained, with clear labels, legends, and annotations. Consider providing a schematic summary figure for the proposed mechanism.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** R1-M1 (calibration rigor) and R1-M2 (statistical robustness) are the primary technical issues that must be resolved. The calibration of α is the foundation of the study, and the lack of quantitative comparison to experimental data undermines confidence in all subsequent conclusions. The statistical analysis of simulation results is insufficient to support the quantitative claims. R1-M3 (experimental validation breadth) and R1-M4 (tethering interpretation) are important but less critical; they should be addressed to strengthen the generalizability and interpretation of the findings.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality** The work is original in its systematic exploration of the balance between protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions in membrane-associated condensation. The finding that charged lipids can both promote and oppose condensation depending on interaction strengths is a novel contribution. However, the conceptual framework of competition between protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions is not entirely new, and the originality lies primarily in the systematic simulation study.
  - **Scientific importance** The question of how membranes regulate condensation is of high importance given the prevalence of membrane-associated condensates in cellular processes. The work has the potential to influence thinking about how membrane composition and charge regulate condensate formation and dissolution. The demonstration that charged lipids can dissolve pre-formed condensates is particularly significant.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The work bridges biophysics, cell biology, and computational chemistry. The findings are relevant to researchers studying phase separation, membrane biology, and signaling. The writing is accessible to a broad scientific audience, although some familiarity with coarse-grained simulation methods is assumed.
  - **Technical soundness** The technical soundness is currently not fully established due to the concerns raised above regarding calibration rigor and statistical robustness. The simulation methodology is appropriate for the questions asked, and the use of Martini 3 is reasonable. The experimental validation is a strength but is limited in scope.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well-written and the logic is clear. The abstract and introduction provide sufficient context. The results section is organized logically. However, some sections, particularly the calibration description, could be clearer for nonspecialists. The conclusions are appropriately cautious in some places but overstate the strength of the experimental validation.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The central question is important, the approach is appropriate, and the findings are potentially significant. However, the calibration procedure must be made more rigorous and reproducible, and the statistical analysis of simulation results must be strengthened. The experimental validation should be expanded or its limitations clearly acknowledged. If these issues are addressed, the manuscript would make a valuable contribution to the field.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that α = 0.975 is the "most appropriate scaling factor" is not quantitatively supported by the presented evidence.
- The claim that "the addition of charged lipids opposed condensation on the membrane when protein–protein interactions were scaled" is based on a limited number of simulations without reported statistical testing.
- The claim that "charged lipids can sustain protein–membrane interactions without a tethering force" is based on simulations where the untethered condensate at high PS fractions remains membrane-associated, but the long-term stability of this state is not demonstrated.
- The claim that "the tethering acted as a promoter of disassembly" is an interpretation that requires more careful consideration of alternative explanations.
- The claim that experimental results "mirror" or show "similar behavior" to simulations is based on a single experimental condition and is therefore not fully supported.
- The statement that "the bilayer can work against the formation of protein condensates when protein–lipid interactions compete with protein–protein interactions" is presented as a general conclusion but is only demonstrated for a specific protein (RGG) and a limited range of conditions.
