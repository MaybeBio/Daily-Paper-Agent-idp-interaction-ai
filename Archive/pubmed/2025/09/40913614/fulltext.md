# ssDNA and ssRNA Promote Phase Condensation of SAMHD1


## Abstract
  SAMHD1 (SAM domain and HD domain-containing protein 1) is a deoxynucleoside triphosphate triphosphohydrolase (dNTPase) with functions in viral restriction, R-loop resolution, DNA repair, telomere maintenance, ssRNA homeostasis, and regulation of self-nucleic acids. As a dNTPase, SAMHD1 functions as an allosterically activated tetramer, where binding of GTP to the A1 activator site of each monomer initiates dNTP-dependent tetramerization. cEM structures reveal that the nucleic-acid-related functions of SAMHD1 involve binding of guanine residues to the A1 site, leading to oligomeric forms that appear as beads-on-a-string on single-stranded RNA and DNA. SAMHD1’s cellular activities and known protein interactions involve liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), although there are no reports that SAMHD1 itself exhibits phase separation properties. The protein phase separation prediction algorithm MolPhase indicated an overall phase separation probability score of 0.65 and suggested that the amino terminal SAM domain and the disordered carboxyl terminus (CT) may promote phase separation. Although no phase separation behavior was observed in physiological buffer, in the presence of 9% PEG 2000 and ssDNA or ssRNA, SAMHD1 condensed into liquid-like droplets. These droplets were disrupted by deletion of the SAM or CT domains, showed fusion behavior, and were rapidly disrupted by the addition of A1 site ligands GTP, dGTP, and small-molecule inhibitors. We also observed that SAMHD1-ssDNA condensates within the nuclei of human cells in microinjection experiments, supporting a biological relevance for such complexes. LLPS by SAMHD1 could serve a regulatory role in cells and provide a new therapeutic target for the treatment of cancer and viral infections.


## INTRODUCTION
  The human enzyme sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain and HD domain-containing protein 1 (SAMHD1) is a guanosine-5′-triphosphate (GTP)-activated deoxynucleoside triphosphate triphosphohydrolase (dNTPase) capable of hydrolyzing all four canonical dNTPs to the deoxynucleoside and triphosphate and plays an important role in nucleotide homeostasis.1,2 The complicated activation mechanism of SAMHD1 involves binding of GTP to a guanine nucleotide-specific activator site (A1) located on each monomer, followed by binding of either dATP, dTTP, or dGTP to the second activator site (A2) and catalytic site to drive formation of the dNTPase-active tetramer.3,4

  In addition, SAMHD1 has non-dNTPase activities that involve binding of single-stranded (ss) DNA or RNA to the dimer–dimer interface of the tetramer.5,6 As established in recent cryo-electron microscopy and biochemical studies, the interaction with single-stranded nucleic acids (ssNAs) involves binding of guanine nucleotides to the A1 site.7 These structures show that single-stranded RNA and DNA sequences containing multiple guanine residues induce dimeric and tetrameric forms of SAMHD1 that are distinct from the nucleotide bound oligomers (Figure 1A,B). With long sequences of ssRNA capable of accommodating multiple oligomeric complexes, the structures resemble beads-on-a-string (Figure 1C). ssNA binding has the potential to downregulate SAMHD1 dNTPase activity in cells because the nucleic acid (NA) complexes abolish this activity.5 In addition, S phase specific phosphorylation of SAMHD1 at Thr592 (pSAMHD1) has been shown to facilitate NA binding through destabilization of the dNTP-induced tetramer, allowing rapid access of ssDNA to the dimer interface as compared to the very stable unphosphorylated form of the enzyme.8 Since pSAMHD1 is implicated in promoting restart of stalled replication forks, one biological function of phosphorylation may be to promote rapid binding to ssNAs present at stalled forks.9 Additional roles for SAMHD1 that involve ssNA binding have been reported in the context of double-strand break (DSB) repair,10–13 class switch recombination,13 R-loop formation at transcription–replication conflict regions,14 telomere maintenance,15 and RNA homeostasis.16 In addition to ssNA binding, some work has suggested that SAMHD1 has 3′ to 5′ exonuclease activity against ssDNA and ssRNA.16–19 However, this nuclease activity has been disputed in work from several laboratories, and the new structures with ssRNA do not show any NA density in the active site of SAMHD1.5–7,5–7,20–22

  Interestingly, most, if not all, of the cellular processes that include SAMHD1 have been associated with phase-separated (PS) protein-nucleic acid condensates. Examples include DSB repair,23–25 R-loops,26,27 telomeres,28 and regulation and segregation of ssRNA and dsRNA in cells.16 In addition, the ubiquitous SAM domain has been implicated in DNA-dependent PS condensates in the context of polycomb group proteins.29 Despite “guilt by association,” there have been no reports of SAMHD1 itself possessing phase separation properties, either in vitro or in cellulo. In this regard, we used the MolPhase advanced prediction tool to investigate whether the SAMHD1 structure and sequence might be conducive to phase separation (Figure 1D).30 This analysis suggested that the amino terminal SAM domain and the carboxyl terminus (CT) were intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) that could serve as scaffolds for phase separation. In addition, the SAM domain possessed strong potential for pi–pi interactions, and the CT contained clusters of cationic residues that could promote electrostatic interactions with NAs. The presence of such interaction motifs within IDRs is strongly associated with phase separation of proteins and protein-NA complexes.31–33

  In this study, we report that SAMHD1 forms liquid–liquid PS condensates that are greatly facilitated by the presence of ssNAs or macromolecular crowding. These condensates can be disrupted by the addition of GTP, dGTP, or small-molecule inhibitors that target the A1 site. The SAMHD1 condensates with ssNAs have fluid-like properties and require the SAM domain and CT for optimal formation. We characterize the properties of these condensates in vitro and show that similar condensates can form in the presence of concentrated protein extracts and by the microinjection of SAMHD1 into a human cell line. The finding suggests a new mechanism to regulate the dNTPase and NA binding functions of SAMHD1 in cells.


## METHODS AND MATERIALS

### Chemicals.
  GTP was obtained from ThermoFisher Scientific. 2′-deoxyguanosine-5′-[α-thio]-triphosphate (dGTPαS) was obtained from TriLink Biotechnologies. Uranyl formate was obtained from Electron Microscopy Sciences.


### DNA and RNA Oligonucleotides.
  All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). DNA oligonucleotides greater than 40 bases and all RNA oligonucleotides were HPLC purified. All oligonucleotides were dissolved to 100 μM concentration in nuclease-free water. When the oligonucleotide contained a fluorescein label, it was attached to the 5′ end of the sequence. Sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S1.


### Isolation of Total Cellular RNA from Hap1 Cells.
  Wild-type HAP1 cells were cultured from fresh freezer stocks in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% tetracycline-free FBS (Neuromics) for 1 week. Cells were allowed to reach 70% confluency in a T-75 flask (~107 cells total) and were released from the flask using 0.25% tryspin. The cells were spun down at 1200 g for 8 min, and the supernatant was decanted from the pellet. The pellet was lysed using RLT buffer (Qiagen), and RNA was isolated using a QiaWave RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The final RNA was eluted in DNAase/RNAase-free water, and the concentration was estimated using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer and the relationship 1 A260 absorbance unit = 40/ug/mL RNA. The RNA was stored at −80 °C.


### Expression and Purification of SAMHD1 and Its Domain Variants.
  Human SAMHD1 and its C-terminal deletion variant (Δ600–626), N-terminal deletion variant (Δ112), N-terminal S6-tagged variant (S6-SAMHD1), and phospho-Thr592 form (pSAMHD1) were all produced and purified as previously described.3,8 All were harbored in a pET19b plasmid and expressed in chemically competent BL21(DE3) E. coli cells as a PreScission protease cleavable 10×-His fusion constructs. For protein expression, overnight starter cultures were grown in 2×YT supplemented with carbenicillin (50 μg/L) and subsequently inoculated 1:100 (v/v) in 2×YT media. Cultures were grown in a Harbinger LEX-48 parallel bioreactor system at 37 °C until an OD600 of 0.7 was reached. The cells were then cold-shocked for 30 min in an ice bath, induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (ThermoFisher), and incubated for 20 h at 20 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (10,000 g) and stored at −80 °C.

  Cell pellets were thawed and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES—pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TCEP, 25 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol) with one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce), 1 mg of DNase I (Roche), 1 mg of RNase A (Alfa-Aesar), and 5 mg of lysozyme (Amresco) per 50 mL of buffer. The resuspension was passed two times through a LM10 microfluidizer (Microfluidics) and centrifuged at 40,000 g to produce a clarified lysate. The lysate was loaded onto a charged, pre-equilibrated 10 mL nickel column (HisPur Ni-NTA resin from ThermoFisher). The loaded column was given stringent, incremental washes with 30, 40, and 50 mM imidazole. After the UV trace returned to baseline, SAMHD1 was eluted from the column using 300 mM imidazole.

  SAMHD1 and its variants were dialyzed against 4 L of general buffer (50 mM HEPES—pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol) overnight with 1 mg of GST-tagged PreScission protease added to remove the imidazole and 10×-His tag. The following day, the protein solution was gently stirred at 4 °C with 2 mL of glutathione–agarose resin and then centrifuged to pellet the resin, and GST-PreScission protease was removed. The solution was concentrated to ~10 mg/mL, then diluted 16-fold in S-column binding buffer (50 mM HEPES—pH 7.5, 20 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol), and applied to a 10 mL GE HiTrap SP sepharose fast-flow column (two 5 mL cartridges in tandem) at 0.5 mL/min. Protein was eluted using an 80 mL gradient from 20 to 500 mM KCl. Then, the isolated proteins were concentrated to ~10 mg/mL and injected (45 mgs/injection) onto a Cytiva Superdex 200 pg HiLoad 26/600 size exclusion column to remove aggregates. The resulting protein was concentrated to 8–9 mg/mL and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen in 30 μL aliquots. SAMHD1 concentration was calculated based on its extinction coefficient at 280 nm (76500 M−1 cm−1).


### SFp Protein Purification and Expression.
  Using commercial gene synthesis (Genescript), the coding region for 4′-phosphopantetheinyl transferase (SFp) from B. subtilis was inserted in the pET29B expression vector and transformed into chemically competent BL21(DE3) (Agilent) cells. Diluted portions of the transformation culture were plated on agar plates containing 50 μg/mL to isolate colonies that were purified as previously described.34 An isolated colony was selected and added to 2×YT supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and grown overnight. The starter culture was used to inoculate 500 mL of 2×YT media. The culture was incubated at 37 °C until an OD of 0.7 was reached, and the culture temperature was quickly lowered on ice for 20 min. Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (ThermoFisher) was then added, and incubation was continued at 20 °C for 6 h. Cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min and stored at −80 °C. Cell pellets were thawed and resuspended in lysis buffer containing 5 mM imidazole, 0.5 M NaCl, and 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.9 supplemented one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce), 1 mg of DNase I (Roche), 1 mg of RNase A (Alfa-Aesar), and 5 mg of lysozyme (Amresco) per 50 mL of buffer. The resuspension was passed two times through an LM10 microfluidizer (Microfluidics) and centrifuged at 40,000 g to produce a clarified lysate. The lysate was loaded onto a charged, pre-equilibrated 10 mL nickel column (HisPur Ni-NTA resin, ThermoFisher). The loaded column was washed sequentially with 30, 40, and 50 mM imidazole. After the UV trace returned to the baseline, SFp was eluted from the column using 300 mM imidazole. The SFp fraction was dialyzed overnight against 4 L of buffer (0.5 M NaCl, and 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.9). The SFp isolate was concentrated to 200 μM and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen in 30 μL aliquots. The protein concentration was calculated based on its extinction coefficient at 280 nm (28,880 M−1 cm−1).

  The stoichiometry of labeling (moles of dye/mol of SAMHD1) fell in the range of 0.6–1 over four labeling experiments. Immediately after labeling, the activity of SAMHD1AF633 was measured using the malachite green assay (see below) and compared with unlabeled SAMHD1 that was treated identically but omitting the dye ester. A five percent (mol/mol) tracer amount of SAMHD1AF633 was spiked into a solution of unlabeled SAMHD1 for fluorescence microscopy imaging with ssDNA57FAM using the conditions described below.


### N-Terminal Labeling of SAMHD1 with Cy5-CoA (Cy5SAMHD1).
  SAMHD1 was modified with a 12 amino acid N-terminal tag with the sequence SSPH-(MGDSLSWLLRLLN)GK (S6-Tag).34 The enzyme SFp was overexpressed and purified as described above34 and was used to catalyze the transfer of a CoA-conjugated Cy5 fluorophore to the protein. The labeling reaction consisted of 5 μM SAMHD1, 0.1 μM SFp, and 5 μM Cy5-CoA in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM TCEP, and 10 mM MgCl2. The reaction proceeded at room temperature for 30 min. The reaction products were loaded onto a desalting column (Zeba, 0.5 mL) and spun at 17,000 g for 2 min at 4 °C to remove the unincorporated dye. The products were resolved by SDS-PAGE using a 4–12% bis-tris polyacrylamide gel (Invitrogen) to evaluate labeling using fluorescence imaging (Typhoon 5 Variable Mode Imager) (Supplemental Figure S1). The labeled protein formed PS condensates with DNA that were visually similar to those of unlabeled SAMHD1, although somewhat smaller in size on average. The labeled protein was stored at −80 °C and gave similar experimental results for about 2 weeks, after which signs of aggregation were observed. The labeled Cy5SAMHD1 protein was utilized for colocalization and stoichiometric studies by adding 5% of labeled protein to a solution of unlabeled SAMHD1. Fluorescence microscopy imaging was performed as described below.


### Fluorescence Microscopy of In Vitro Phase Separation.
  Purified SAMHD1 was mixed with either ssDNA57FAM or ssRNA57FAM in the buffer conditions of 150 mM potassium acetate, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM TCEP pH 7.1, and 9% PEG2000 (w/v%) in a 96-well plate. As described above, when SAMHD1AF633 or Cy5SAMHD1 was used, it was mixed with unlabeled protein at a molar ratio of 1/20. The mixtures of protein and DNA were allowed to sit at room temperature for 20 min in the dark, after which 10 μL was spotted on a 18-well untreated chamber slide (Ibidi). Alternative slide surfaces were compared, including Pll-g-PEG-incubated slides, aqua regia-washed glass slides, and untreated glass slides. Although droplets were observed on all surfaces, the droplets appeared smaller and sometimes irregular compared to the Ibidi chamber slides, which was used for all subsequent experiments. Condensates were allowed to settle on the slide for 40 min in the dark at room temperature. Images (eight per condition) were acquired using a 60× oil objective in brightfield, green fluorescence, or far-red fluorescence channels on a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E with a Photometrics Evolve 512 EMCCD camera. In controls, we explored temperature effects on the settling and formation of condensates. Ambient temperature was found to be most convenient (21–23 °C); at temperatures below 20 °C. The rate of droplet formation was inconveniently slow. Liquid-like droplets also readily formed at a physiological temperature of 37 °C and were visually similar to those observed at ambient temperature (Supplemental Figure S2).


### Screening of Crowding Agents for Induced Phase Separation of SAMHD1.
  Unlabeled SAMHD1 or SAMHD1A633 with or without ssDNAFAM were mixed in a buffer consisting of 150 mM potassium acetate, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM TCEP pH 7.1, and concentrations of PEG2K in the range 0 to 9% (w/v) in a 96-well plate. The solutions were allowed to sit at room temperature for 20 min in the dark followed by spotting 10 μL on a Ibidi untreated 18-well chamber slide. The mixture was allowed to settle for 40 min in the dark at room temperature. Images (eight per condition) were then acquired using a 60× oil objective in brightfield, green fluorescence, or far-red fluorescence channels on a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E with a Photometrics Evolve 512 EMCCD camera. Each image was analyzed utilizing a custom ImageJ macro to count all droplets larger than 1 μm2 based on the fluorescence intensity of the thresholded image. Areas (μm2) and droplet counts were then recorded for each PEG2K condition. Although other PEG macromolecules induced similar ssDNA-dependent droplets, PEG2K was selected because it consistently induced ssNA-dependent droplets with SAMHD1 at a low w/v percentage. In contrast, PEG 300 and 600 showed no droplet formation, while PEG 3350, 4000, and 8000 induced protein precipitation rather than phase separation at higher percentages. Ficoll 70 and 400 (15 w/v %) and BSA (150 mg/mL) did not result in the ssDNA-dependent phase separation of SAMHD1.

  Concentrated E. coli cell protein extracts were also tested for their ability to promote phase separation. Extracts were prepared from BL21(DE3) cells beginning with small overnight starter cultures grown in 2×YT media supplemented with carbenicillin (50 μg/L). Twenty milliliters of the starter culture was inoculated into 4 L of fresh 2×YT media containing the antibiotic and incubated at 37 °C for 14 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (10,000 g), and the pellet was stored at − 80 °C. The pellet (30 g) was processed by thawing on ice and resuspending in 100 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES—pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TCEP) containing one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce), 1 mg DNase I (Roche), 1 mg RNase A (Alfa-Aesar), and 5 mg lysozyme (Amresco) per 50 mL of buffer. The resuspension was passed two times through a LM10 microfluidizer (Microfluidics) and centrifuged at 40,000 g to produce a clarified lysate. The clarified lysate was then dialyzed against the same buffer to remove small molecules and nucleotides and then subsequently dialyzed in 1× physiological buffer (150 mM potassium acetate, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM TCEP). The resulting lysate was then spun at 40,000 g for 30 min to remove any precipitated protein. The protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay (Bio-Rad) and BSA standard. The lysate was concentrated to 385 mg/mL and centrifuged again at 40,000 g for 20 min to remove any aggregates that formed during the concentration step. The final lysate was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and the protein concentration was redetermined before using the extract in phase separation experiments. Phase separation experiments using the extract were performed in the presence and absence of 10 μM ssDNA57FAM, 200 mg/mL total protein extract, and 10 μM Cy5SAMHD1. The final buffer consisted of 150 mM potassium acetate, 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM TCEP.


### Disruption of SAMHD1-ssNA Condensates by Nucleotides and Small-Molecule Inhibitors.
  Fluorescence microscopy imaging and light scattering methods were used to study the formation and disruption of PS condensates by added nucleotides. In the imaging approach, 10 μM SAMHD1 and ssDNA57FAM or ssRNA57FAM were mixed according to the fluorescence microscopy protocol described above. After a 20 min droplet formation time, 13.5 μL of the solutions were mixed with 1.5 μL of 250 μM or 2.5 mM dGTP, GTP, and dATP, or 1.5 μL of 100 μM or 1 mM stocks of the inhibitors 5a and 8.35,36 Ten μL of condensate/nucleotide mixtures was spotted in the wells of an 18-well uncoated chamber slide and allowed to settle for 40 min. Images were acquired using a 60× oil objective in brightfield and green fluorescence channels on a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E with a Photometrics Evolve 512 EMCCD camera.

  To explore the kinetics of droplet formation and nucleotide disruption, a light scattering approach was employed. Buffer components identical to those used in the fluorescence microscopy protocol were added to the wells of a 96-well V-bottom plate (Griener). Nine μL of 55.3 μM SAMHD1 was then added to give a final concentration of 10 μM, followed by the addition of 5 μL of 100 μM ssDNA57 to initiate phase separation (final volume of 50 μL). Upon the addition of ssDNA57, the plate was immediately moved to a Biotek Neo2 plate reader, and light scattering was monitored at a wavelength of 330 nm for 10 min. After the initial condensate formation period, 5 μL was removed from the sample well and discarded, and 5 μL of either 2.5 mM GTP or 250 μM dGTP was added to initiate droplet disruption, and the decrease in light scattering at 330 nM was followed for 20 min.


### In Vitro Transcription of RNA.
  RNA was transcribed from the T7 promoter of the control template included in the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA transcription kit (NEB) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Trace labeling was performed by including 0.25 equiv of fluorescein-UTP along with unlabeled UTP. The resulting RNA was purified using a Monarch RNA cleanup kit (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C.


### SAMHD1 dGTPase Activity.
  To test whether the dNTPase activity of SAMHD1 was retained upon exiting PS condensates, a malachite green colorimetric activity assay was utilized using the same buffer conditions used in the fluorescence imaging experiments. Phase separation was initiated at an ambient temperature using 10 μM SAMHD1 and 10 μM ssDNA57 and allowed to proceed for 20 min. The condensate solution was then diluted 1:20 (0.5 μM [SAMHD1] final) into the same buffer containing 200 μM dGTP substrate. Initial rates of product formation were obtained from plots of absorbance versus time as previously described.37


### Reversible Exchange Kinetics of ssDNA between Bulk Solution and Droplets.
  The rates of ssDNA57FAM entry and exit from droplets were measured using real-time fluorescence imaging. Phase separation was first initiated at an ambient temperature and allowed to proceed for 20 min using 10 μM SAMHD1 and 5 μM ssDNA57FAM or 10 μM unlabeled ssDNA57 in a 50 μL volume. Thirty microliters of the condensate solution was spotted in a well of an Ibidi chamber slide and allowed to settle for 40 min. To initiate the exchange of labeled DNA out of individual droplets, unlabeled ssDNA57 (20 μM) was added to the labeled droplets. To measure the rate of labeled DNA entry into droplets, 1 μM ssDNA57FAM was added to unlabeled droplets. The rate of appearance or decay of droplet fluorescence was followed using real-time imaging and the green fluorescence channel of the instrument. Controls for photobleaching were also performed in the same time frame as the experimental measurements.


### Microinjection of HAP1 Cells.
  HAP1 SAMHD1 knockout cells were used for microinjection studies to assess SAMHD1-ssDNA liquid phase separation in cellulo. HAP1 SAMHD1 knockout cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% tetracycline-free FBS following freeze–thaw from −80 °C stocks. Cells were plated at a concentration of 2000 cells/mL in Ibidi low μ-dishes with the manufacturer’s Ibitreat reagent and incubated overnight prior to microinjection using an Eppendorf FemtoJet with an integrated pump and Eppendorf Injectman 4. Femtotip I tips (Eppendorf) were used for injection and were backloaded with 10 μM ssDNA57FAM or Cy5SAMHD1 and injected using a compensation pressure of 25 hPa, an injection pressure of 250 hPa, and an injection time of 2.0 s. Following injection, cells were imaged in the green or far-red fluorescent channel using a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E with a Photometrics Evolve 512 EMCCD camera. For coinjection studies on condensate formation, a mixture of Cy5SAMHD1 and ssDNA57FAM (50 μM each) was used. A cellular concentration of 5 μM for each component was estimated based on the injection volume and approximate cell volume. The same injection parameters were used as described above for the independent DNA and Cy5SAMHD1 injections.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### SAMHD1 Shows Phase Separation Behavior in the Presence of ssDNA and ssRNA.
  Macromolecular crowding agents are often employed and, in many cases, required to observe phase separation of proteins in vitro.38 A common inert crowding agent is polyethylene glycol (PEG), and we explored various masses and concentrations of PEG in the range of 300 to 8 K (see Methods). We found that PEG2K promoted NA-dependent phase separation at concentrations of ≥9% (w/v) without inducing protein precipitation (Figure 2A, see Supplemental Figure S2 for similar images with ssRNA). In the initial screening, fluorescent droplets were observed with 10 μM SAMHD1 and 10 μM ssDNA57FAM (Figure 2A,B). In contrast, brightfield images of 10 μM SAMHD1 in the absence of DNA showed no droplets, and red fluorescence images of dye-labeled SAMHD1A633 alone showed very small fluorescent puncta (<1 μm2) that were attributed to low levels of aggregation of the dye-labeled protein (see Methods for further details on the limited stability of SAMHD1A633) (Figure 2A–C). The droplets that formed in the presence of 10 μM SAMHD1 and 10 μM ssDNA57FAM were significantly larger (>10 μm2 on average) (Figure 2C) and showed time-dependent fusion behavior (Supplemental Figure S3). In addition, we also observed ssDNA-dependent droplet formation at the higher temperature of 37 °C (Supplemental Figure S4). These initial findings suggested that SAMHD1 complexes with ssDNA and RNA can serve as a scaffold to facilitate the formation of liquid-like droplets.

  To establish that droplets contained both SAMHD1 and ssDNA, we obtained brightfield and dual fluorescence confocal microscopy images of droplets formed from ssDNA57FAM and chemically labeled SAMHD1A633 (Figure 2D) or enzymatically amino terminal labeled Cy5SAMHD1 (Figure 2E) (see Methods for labeling protocols). The green, red, and merged fluorescence images using both labeled proteins clearly showed that the droplets contained both ssDNA and protein, although some smaller droplets showed lower levels of green fluorescence. This variability could arise from subtle perturbations related to the labeled protein structure or stability. Given the limitations of using SAMHD1A633 and Cy5SAMHD1, we chose out of caution to perform most of the remaining studies using labeled NAs.

  In our initial screening of crowding agents, we observed that high percentages of large mass PEG polymers (e.g., PEG 8K) induced protein condensation even in the absence of ssDNA (Supplemental Figure S5), which was also observed using highly concentrated bacterial protein extracts (Supplemental Figure S6). This transition from NA-dependent liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) to a protein alone mechanism is consistent with the expected influence of high concentrations of crowding agents, where the otherwise weak molecular interactions of the phase separating species are increased due to the large excluded volume.38 These high concentrations of PEG8K may not be relevant to biology because they could exceed the average crowding levels found in human cells based on cellular osmolality measurements.38,39 Nevertheless, NAs greatly facilitate the phase separation of SAMHD1 under modest crowding conditions, and the known localization of SAMHD1 to sites of DNA and RNA transactions in cells suggests a role for NAs in facilitating phase separation of SAMHD1 in cells.

  We investigated the dNTPase activity of SAMHD1 in the presence of 9% PEG2K and its monomer subunit ethylene glycol (EG) to ascertain whether either cosolute was inhibitory (Supplemental Figure S7). Using the malachite green assay with 200 μM dGTP as the activator and substrate, we found that EG was profoundly inhibitory, but PEG2K enhanced the dNTPase activity by about 2-fold using these conditions. We note that since dGTP rapidly disassembles condensates (see studies below), these activity measurements reflect the SAMHD1 tetramer in the solution phase in the presence of the PEG crowder. The results suggest that the small EG monomer can access binding sites on the enzyme that are inhibitory to either dGTP binding or the structure of the enzyme, while PEG2K cannot. We attribute the modest increase in SAMHD1 dNTPase activity in the crowded PEG solution to the more favorable entropy of forming compact protein oligomers as compared to the monomer subunits which occupy a larger volume.40 Such effects are often observed with oligomeric enzymes in the presence of inert crowding agents such as PEG2K. The increase in activity in the presence of PEG2K also indicates that diffusion and binding of dGTP in condensates are rapid under these crowding conditions.


### NA Length Dependence of PS Condensates.
  Previous bulk solution studies of the length dependence of SAMHD1 binding to NAs have revealed similar length dependences for the binding affinities of ssDNA and ssRNA but different SAMHD1 binding stoichiometries.5–8 In these studies, ssDNA and ssRNA both showed increasing binding affinities as the NA length was increased in the range of 10–60 nucleotides,7,8 and further increases in the NA length did not result in further affinity increases. With respect to protein/NA stoichiometry, a single molecule of a ssDNA 32 mer can induce tetramerization of SAMHD1, whereas two molecules of a ssRNA 32 mer are required to induce tetramerization.7 Negative stain electron microscopy images indicate that long sequences of ssRNA were bound to both dimers and tetramers of SAMHD1. These results, along with cryo-electron microscopy structures, indicate that ssNA binding induces dimerization of SAMHD1, and that nearby NA-bound dimers then associate to form tetramers.7,8

  Given these previous findings, we asked whether phase separation of SAMHD1 complexes with 10, 32, 40, 57, and 90 mer FAM-labeled ssDNA correlated with previous affinity measurements and changes in oligomeric state observed in bulk solution (Figure 3A). Using 10 μM DNA concentrations in the presence of 10 μM SAMHD1, we observed no condensates using the 10 mer ssDNA, but as the ssDNA length was increased from 32 to 57 nt, the number of droplets and the area of the droplets increased (Figure 3B). Since ssNA lengths greater than about 22 nt are required to induce SAMHD1 dimerization and tetramerization,7 the inability of the 10 mer to form condensates suggests that ssNA-induced oligomerization is required for phase separation. When the ssDNA concentration was decreased to 1 μM (while maintaining [SAMHD1] at 10 μM), the droplet numbers and areas were reduced for the 32, 40, and 57 mer sequences. This result suggests that the protein occupancy or the ensemble of oligomeric states present was not optimal for droplet formation at this concentration.

  Unique behavior was noted for the longest DNA sequence, 90 nt (Figure 3A). When 10 μM concentration was employed, the droplet sizes and numbers decreased as compared to the 57 mer but formed robustly when the 90 mer concentration was reduced to 1 μM (Figure 3A). We attribute the unique concentration dependences of the 90 mer to the reduced density of SAMHD1 oligomers on the longer 90 mer DNA strand when the DNA is provided in excess (10 μM) compared to SAMHD1 (1 μM), which hinders the formation of PS droplets (i.e., the limiting amount of protein is spread out over more DNA binding sites). Consistent with this interpretation, when SAMHD1 (10 μM) was present in a 10-fold molar excess over the ssDNA (1 μM), the density of SAMHD1 dimers and/or tetramers was increased and thereby sufficient to nucleate droplet formation (Figure 3A). Thus, this behavior of the 90 mer provides further evidence that phase separation requires oligomerization of SAMHD1 molecules that are relatively close to the DNA chain. The phenomena of inhibited droplet formation by excess DNA is observed for all DNA molecules when high DNA concentrations are used.

  The ssRNA length dependence of condensate formation was also investigated by using ssRNA32FAM and ssRNA57FAM. The studies with ssRNA32FAM are especially relevant because the cryoEM structural studies were performed with this same RNA.7 As noted above, the structure with ssRNA32 shows that two RNA molecules are bound to a single SAMHD1 tetramer, while complexes with ssDNA32 show only one DNA per tetramer. Green fluorescence images of condensates containing ssRNA32FAM displayed droplets much larger than those observed for ssDNA32FAM at both 10 and 1 μM conditions (Figure 3C,D). In addition, the condensates with ssRNA32FAM showed unique wetting behavior, suggesting a highly fluid nature. For condensates using 10 μM ssRNA57FAM, there was a lesser distinction with ssDNA57FAM, but the area distribution for the 1 μM condensates was skewed to larger areas for ssRNA57FAM (compare Figure 3A,B with Figure 3C,D). The detailed mechanism for these differences is not known at this time, but these data suggest that DNA and RNA condensates could have different physical properties in cells that have the potential for differential regulation.


### SAMHD1 and ssDNA Concentration Dependences of Droplet Formation.
  Using ssDNA57FAM and Cy5SAMHD1 as labeled probes to monitor droplet formation, we explored how droplet formation depended on the molar ratio of DNA to protein. These experiments were intended to shed light on whether droplet formation correlated with dimeric or tetrameric oligomeric states of SAMHD1. In one experiment, fixed ssDNA57FAM (10 μM) and varying concentrations of unlabeled SAMHD1 in the range 0.3 to 10 μM were mixed, and the areas and numbers of droplets were measured using fluorescence imaging (Figure 4A). In a second experiment, fixed Cy5SAMHD1 (10 μM) and varying concentrations of unlabeled ssDNA57 were used (Figure 4B). With unlabeled SAMHD1 as the variable component, the number and average area of the droplets containing the labeled DNA diminished markedly as the SAMHD1 concentration was decreased (Figure 4C). In contrast, using unlabeled ssDNA57 as the variable component, both the numbers and areas of droplets containing labeled SAMHD1 were similar at most of the tested ssDNA concentrations (Figure 4D). However, at 0.3 and 0.6 μM DNA concentrations, there were no condensates with areas greater than ~12 μm2, suggesting that the DNA concentration had become limiting for the formation of the largest droplets.

  These results can be rationalized by the simple mechanism shown in Figure 4E. When the ssDNA concentration is high, condensate formation is limited by the protein levels and the requirement for protein oligomers. In contrast, when SAMHD1 is fixed at a high concentration relative to DNA strands, protein oligomers readily form on the available DNA strands and can be pulled into condensates, even at fairly low levels of DNA strands.


### SAMHD1 PS Condensates Are Disrupted by Guanine Nucleotide Triphosphates.
  We have previously shown that binding of A1 site ligands (GTP, dGTP, small-molecule inhibitors) can disrupt SAMHD1 NA complexes, but other nucleotide triphosphates that do not bind the A1 site show weak antagonism to ssNA binding.5–7 To test whether occupancy of the A1 site by guanine nucleotides in ssNA is important for droplet formation, we formed droplets with ssDNA57FAM and ssRNA57FAM, added either dGTP, dATP, or GTP, and then collected fluorescence images to evaluate the impact of these nucleotides on droplet numbers and sizes (Figure 5A–D). For droplets formed with ssDNA57FAM (Figure 5A,B), the A1 site ligands dGTP and GTP shifted the distribution to much smaller droplet sizes even at the lowest concentration (2.5 μM), while dATP had no effect until a nucleotide concentration of 25 μM was used. In this series, dGTP had the most potent effect because it is a substrate that can also occupy A2 sites and promote formation of the stable nucleotide bound tetramer.7,8 Surprisingly, for droplets formed with ssRNA57FAM (Figure 5C,D), all three nucleotides showed similar abilities to disrupt the droplets and shift the distribution to much smaller droplet sizes. This result, in the context of PS condensates, differs from the strict guanine nucleotide specificity required for disruption of RNA complexes that we previously reported in the absence of condensation.7 The detailed mechanistic basis for this difference is not currently known but could arise from the unique fluidity or other physical characteristics of the RNA condensates (see above) and/or increases in the effective concentrations of the nucleotides within RNA condensates that obscure the expected guanine specificity. This observation suggests that RNA and DNA condensates may be differentially regulated by nucleotides in the cells.

  Finally, we recently reported the design of two inhibitors that are specific for the A1 site of SAMHD1 and inhibit its dNTPase activity and ssNA binding (compounds 5a and 8; Figure 5E,F).35,36 Both 5a and 8 completely disrupted droplets formed with ssDNA57FAM at concentrations as low as 10 μM, further establishing that specific targeting of the A1 site can potently disrupt both activities of SAMHD1, as well as its ability to exist in PS condensates.

  To support the imaging studies, we also used light scattering to measure the rates of formation of condensates with ssDNA and their disruption with guanine nucleotides (Figure 6). In these studies, condensates were first formed by the addition of ssDNA57 to a solution of SAMHD1, and an optical density (OD) increase at 330 nm was followed over time. After the OD reached its maximal level (~5 min), either dGTP (25 μM) or GTP (250 μM) was added, and the OD decreases were followed until a stable baseline level was reached (these concentrations of guanine nucleotides are in the range found in human cell lines).41 A control of SAMHD1 alone (which does not form condensates) was used to estimate the baseline OD for the condensate-free solutions (red data in Figure 6). For both dGTP and GTP at their respective concentrations, the OD values decreased by 51 and 45% in the 20 s time required for addition and mixing of the nucleotides and approached the OD330 value of free SAMHD1 after 15 to 20 min. These data establish the rapid accessibility of the A1 sites within the condensates by ligands, which is consistent with the expected characteristics of liquid condensates.38


### Exchange of ssDNA between Bulk Solution and SAMHD1 Condensates.
  We investigated whether droplets labeled with ssDNA57FAM could be invaded by unlabeled DNA added to bulk solution (Figure 7A). After time was allowed for droplets containing ssDNA57FAM to form and settle on the slide surface, unlabeled DNA (20 μM) was added, and real-time fluorescence imaging was used to monitor the time-dependent loss of droplet fluorescence. As early as 15 s after adding the unlabeled DNA, decreases in the condensate fluorescence intensity were detectable, which were largely complete by 60 s. The comparatively rapid time scale of these changes strongly suggests that the droplets are liquid-like and that NAs can freely exchange between bulk solution and condensates. These decreases in fluorescence intensity cannot be attributed to photobleaching because labeled condensates alone showed no changes over the same time frame (Supplemental Figure S8). A related experiment employing 10 μM ssDNA57FAM as the bulk solution component provided further mechanistic information about the mechanism of ssNA exchange between condensates and bulk solution (Figure 7B). In a time frame less than 30 s, labeled DNA was observed to penetrate larger droplets and form a ring structure around the droplet perimeter, while smaller droplets showed more extensive invasion of the labeled DNA. These data suggested an invasion mechanism involving rapid penetration of the droplet perimeter followed by slower diffusion of the invading DNA over the remaining droplet volume in a time scale of several minutes. In addition, the observation that the droplet sizes stayed constant as labeled DNA invaded suggests that exchange of labeled and unlabeled DNA occurs rather than increasing the DNA content of the droplet. Such behavior is consistent with a defined molar ratio of the protein and NA within condensates.


### Human Cell Studies.
  To move beyond the in vitro explorations of SAMHD1 phase separation, we employed Cy5SAMHD1 and ssDNA57FAM in cell microinjection experiments using the HAP1 SAMHD1 knockout human cell line.36 In control experiments, ssDNA57FAM and Cy5SAMHD1 were injected separately to ascertain the cellular image features associated with the injection of each individual component (Figure 8A,B). The ssDNA57FAM images (n = 10) were characterized by numerous, intensely fluorescent nuclear puncta and weak diffuse green fluorescence in the cytoplasm of the cell (Figure 8A). In contrast, the Cy5SAMHD1 images (n = 10) showed diffuse red fluorescence throughout the cell and numerous cytoplasmic puncta of varying sizes, but nuclei showed few or no red puncta (Figure 8B). With this information, we then combined Cy5SAMHD1 and ssDNA57FAM and coinjected the mixture into cells to look for colocalization of red and green fluorescence (Figure 8C). Multiple costained nuclear puncta varying in size from 1 to 2 μm were observed in approximately 50% of the nuclei of costained cells that were injected and imaged (n = 20). We did not observe any costaining of cytoplasmic puncta in these experiments. The average size of the costained puncta was about half the area of the average in vitro condensate using similar protein and DNA concentrations, and their morphology was similar to the liquid droplets observed in the in vitro experiments.

  The microinjection results showing costained puncta of Cy5SAMHD1 and ssDNA57FAM in cell nuclei supports the in vitro findings that SAMHD1 can form PS condensates in the presence of ssDNA. However, other aspects of the cellular findings, such as the puncta observed for DNA and SAMHD1 alone, are difficult to interpret unambiguously. We cannot rule out that cellular solutes (proteins, NAs, polyamines, and small-molecule osmolytes) not present in the in vitro experiments influence the behavior of the protein or DNA components and promote the formation of the fluorescent puncta. Further insights into the cellular aspects of SAMHD1 phase separation await the development of new methods for fluorescent labeling of SAMHD1 in cellulo without affecting its phase separation behavior or enzymatic activity.


### NA-Dependent LLPS of SAMHD1 Requires SAM and C-Terminal Domains.
  Given that the MolPhase phase separation prediction algorithm suggested that the amino terminal SAM domain and the disordered C-terminal (CT) domains might be involved in phase separation of SAMHD1 (Figure 1), we explored the phase separation behavior of the isolated SAM domain, the Δ112 amino terminal deletion mutant, the Δ583–626 CT deletion mutant, and the phosphothreonine 592 site-specifically modified form of the enzyme (pSAMHD1) (Figure 9).8 All three forms have been shown to retain near wild-type dNTPase activity.7,8 However, the CT deletion mutant and T592 phosphorylated form have increased tetramer dynamics, which promotes invasion of ssDNA into the A1 site as well as the ssDNA binding surface that is otherwise occluded in the dNTPase tetramer.

  These constructs were tested at concentrations of 10 μM (Figure 9A) and 1 μM (Figure 9B) in the presence of 9% PEG2K. The SAM domain itself showed no phase separation or aggregation at either concentration. In contrast, the Δ112 and Δ583–626 deletion mutants exhibited marked changes in the morphology of the condensates that were formed. Both showed much smaller condensates than full-length SAMHD1, and these condensates had characteristics of solids rather than the liquid-like droplets that were characteristic of the native protein.42,43 In addition, the condensates that formed with Δ112 appeared to be elongated aggregates. The phosphorylated pSAMHD1 formed liquid-like droplets that were similar in size and morphology to wild-type SAMHD1.

  The experimental results are consistent with the MolPhase predictions in that the SAM and CT domains were found to be critical for the formation of liquid condensates. However, the MolPhase predictions do not reveal the requirement for ssNA. We speculate that the binding energy for ssNA serves to bring multiple SAMHD1 dimers and tetramers in spatial proximity, where the flexible SAM domains can then interact with one another. This mechanism, which would be enhanced in crowded environment,3940, suggests that the intermolecular interactions of the SAM domain are intrinsically weak, and that binding to the flexible ssNA chain decreases the entropic barrier for forming these interactions. Evidence that the CT domain interacts with ssNA was previously reported using a mass spectroscopy crosslinking method.5 Both the T592 phosphorylated form and the delta 583–626 deletion mutant have been shown to promote destabilization of the dNTPase tetramer and allow access of ssNA to the A1 and interface binding sites.8 However, since delta 583–626 lacks the cationic and aromatic residues in the tail that could contribute to ssNA binding, the formation of liquid condensates may be negated.

  Finally, a point mutation in SAMHD1 that selectively disrupts NA-induced oligomerization would be expected to ablate condensate formation, given our current model. However, we do not have the information required to select such a mutation with high confidence because the binding mode of ssDNA is not known at high resolution, and the electrostatic binding surface for ssRNA characterized in cEM and mutational studies is extended across a large interface where a single point mutation is not likely to induce an observable effect. We view our deletion of the SAM protein–protein interaction domain and its negative impact on condensate formation as the closest experimental finding that relates to the mechanism of oligomerization-dependent phase separation.


## CONCLUSIONS
  Although in vitro studies can never mimic the cellular environment perfectly, such studies play a valuable role in understanding the mechanism because they allow the facile testing of specific hypotheses concerning mutational effects, regulatory factors, and physical properties of condensates that are challenging or impossible to characterize in cellular studies. In addition, the identification and characterization of mutational hot spots that impact phase separation can be invaluable for designing cellular experiments and interpreting the results using genomically expressed mutant proteins.

  Cellular experiments have their own limitations. Most notably, the need to label the target protein with a fluorescent probe (typically a fusion construct with a fluorescent reporter protein) can impact the phase separation behavior of a target protein in unpredictable ways.44 With SAMHD1, we observed that both chemical and N-terminal labeling can reduce the stability of the protein and affect its phase separation properties. This was true even with our very conservative addition of a short N-terminal peptide that allowed the site-specific attachment of a biotin-conjugated fluorophore using SFp. At this time, the goal of studying phase separation of SAMHD1 in human cells using in cellulo expressed SAMHD1 fusion proteins is contraindicated by our in vitro findings.

  Our studies point to several features of SAMHD1 phase separation that have implications for its biological functions. First, phase separation is greatly facilitated by ssNAs (at least under most conditions), which could help direct the protein to sites of DNA replication, repair, or RNA processing and is consistent with numerous previous studies establishing that such processes involve PS components (vide supra). Second, there is potential for guanine nucleotides to regulate the phase separation behavior of SAMHD1 in a rapid and reversible manner. Since NA processing in cells is often compartmentalized into PS condensates, dGTP and GTP would have to be present at a high enough local concentration at these PS foci to promote release of SAMHD1 from condensates. One can speculate that high concentrations of dGTP (and other dNTPs) at moving replication forks could promote the release of SAMHD1 from condensates and allow it to act as a dNTPase, promoting balanced levels of each dNTP important for faithful DNA replication.45–47 Alternatively, stalled forks with stretches of ssDNA could promote the formation of condensates that then initiate repair events, leading to fork progression. Finally, our findings that both the SAM and CTD domains of SAMHD1 are required for the formation of PS condensates indicate the potential for unexplored additional functions of these domains in cells. These questions, and many others, await the development of additional approaches for interrogating the SAMHD1 phase separation in cells.
