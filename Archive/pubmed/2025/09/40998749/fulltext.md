# Statistical Physics-Based Approaches to Model the Function and Complexation of Disordered Proteins


## Abstract
  Functional classification of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDP) is a challenge due to their low sequence homology and lack of stable tertiary structure. We embrace this challenge to classify a model system of two IDPs—NCBD and CID—that have coevolved and for which both ancestral and extant sequences are available, along with quantitative binding data. One of these sequences, NCBD, exhibits partial secondary structure, while the other (CID) remains highly disordered and is highly charged. We classify these sequences using statistical physics-derived sequence-dependent interaction maps that predict distance maps (ensemble average distances between arbitrary residue pairs). We also use sequence-specific dynamic profiles for further comparison. Our findings show that CID proteins can be classified into two major groups due to two distinct types of patterns in their electrostatic interaction maps. Classification of CIDs using nonelectrostatic patterning yields diverging predictions, illustrating the importance of accurately modeling long-range electrostatic interactions. Conversely, the classification of NCBD sequences generally reaches a consensus when physics-based noncharge patterning metrics are applied, along with the dynamical profiles. Furthermore, we used these sequence-dependent metrics and dynamical profiles to quantitatively model the binding affinities between the two IDPs. Surprisingly, we find that multiple physics-based sequence metrics quantitatively recapitulate the binding affinities between CID and NCBD variants, linking sequence composition and patterning to their emergent function. This integrated framework provides a generalizable strategy for classifying IDPs and predicting complexation behavior, offering new avenues for probing sequence–function relationships in disordered protein systems.


## INTRODUCTION
  Functionally similar proteins typically have similar native structures. This structure–function relationship has greatly enhanced our understanding of the functions of folded proteins. However, intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) lack a well-defined folded structure and thus do not fit this paradigm. Additionally, functionally similar IDPs can have a low degree of sequence similarity, unlike their folded counterparts. The absence of well-defined structure, combined with rapid sequence divergence1,2 presents challenges to the functional classification of IDPs. We need new methodologies3 to functionally classify IDPs.

  Despite wide sequence dissimilarities and disordered ensembles, IDPs can exhibit certain conformational similarities, for example, the average radius of the chain and its dependence on the protein length. Stated differently, similar features of conformation can be realized from different sequences. This has led to the hypothesis that some conformational features of IDP sequences, rather than the sequences themselves, have been conserved for function.4,5 Consistent with this, biophysical properties have been linked to function.3 For example, the composition of charge and other specific amino acids, and measures of their arrangement2,6–11 can broadly classify functionally similar IDPs. These sequence-based statistical properties have been shown to correlate with features of conformation, such as chain dimension.12–14 A separate line of investigation also evolved to analytically compute conformational observables directly from a sequence-dependent coarse-grained Hamiltonian. This statistical mechanics approach has led to the discovery of several sequence-dependent mathematical relations that quantify interactions, both electrostatic and nonelectrostatic, and subsequently derive conformational features within this formalism.15–20 For example, the sequence charge decoration (SCD) metric is a measure of the electrostatic interaction and can be used to directly compute the ensemble average end-to-end distance within a closed-form analytical relation.15 Simulation21,22 and experiments23 have shown that sequence-dependent chain dimensions strongly correlate with SCD. Although originally derived to describe single-chain conformation, SCD has also been useful in describing phase separation propensity.22,24–27 A generalized version of SCD is used to compute the distance between any two residues, not just the end-to-end.19 These detailed metrics, written as decoration matrices, provide a blueprint for sequences and have been useful in the functional classification of highly charged IDPs.28

  In this paper, we further extend the Hamiltonian-centric statistical mechanics approach to functionally classify a broader set of IDP sequences than previously studied. In doing so, we also investigate a relatively unexplored corner of the IDP sequence space that is typically overlooked. We consider sequences that a) are low in charge, b) are available for both extant and ancestral sequences, c) coevolved with another IDP, and d) have modest sequence conservation (see Tables S1 and S2) and secondary structure with the possibility of being marginal IDP.23 We focus on two specific protein families, NCBD and CID, which coevolved together and whose ancestral sequences have been phylogenetically resurrected and biophysically characterized to a great extent.29 NCBD is a Nuclear Coactivator Binding Domain of the transcriptional coactivator protein CREBBP and its paralogue p300.30 NCBD lacks stable tertiary structure and has AIUPred disorder scores31,32 between 0.22 and 0.61, with an average of 0.3 for all the paralogues and homologues considered. The molten globule-like nature of NCBD30,33 also raises the possibility that NCBD may be a marginal IDP. Marginal IDPs can exhibit properties of both folded and disordered proteins23,34 simultaneously and lie near the Uversky line.35,36 CREBBP-Interacting Domain (also known as CID) of nuclear coactivator (NCOA) proteins is highly disordered. CID has AIUPred disorder scores ranging from 0.38 to 0.77, with an average of 0.62 for the sequences considered in this work. CID is present in three mammalian nuclear coactivator paralogues—NCOA1, NCOA2, and NCOA3—and appeared much later in evolution than NCBD. Nuclear receptors respond to ligands such as hormones and bind to nuclear coactivators, which, in turn, recruit other coactivators like CREBBP/p-300 by complexation between CID and NCBD. Recruitment of CREBBP/p-300 onto nuclear coactivators facilitates chromatin remodeling and ultimately transcription.33 Ancient sequences of CID and NCBD have been reconstructed and together with the extant sequences (Figure 1) provide a model system to rigorously test functional classification algorithms.

  In contrast to the relatively nascent field of functional analysis of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), the functional classification of folded proteins is well established, largely due to the structural similarity shared among functionally related proteins. However, beyond static structures, structure-encoded dynamics have provided additional insight into how protein function evolves and is modulated by mutations. In this context, models that quantify dynamics, such as the residue-specific Dynamic Flexibility Index (DFI)37–39 have proven especially informative. Motivated by the success of this approach in folded proteins, we investigated whether similar dynamic function relationships exist in IDPs. We find that statistical physics-based metrics and dynamic measures can often produce consistent functional classifications for IDPs, particularly those with a low net charge. While there are major similarities, there are also some deviations between different approaches, particularly for CID, a highly charged IDP. The deviations between these metrics reflect (i) the rapid divergence of IDP sequences, (ii) the importance of appropriately modeling long-range electrostatics, and (iii) the possibility that different metrics may capture features responsible for different functions in multifunctional IDPs.

  Furthermore, we build new statistical physics-based tools to compare relative binding affinities between two IDPs. Despite recent advances40 in modeling interaction between IDPs, quantitative prediction of the binding affinities of two arbitrary IDP sequences remains a challenge due to several competing factors: chain entropy, translational entropy, solvation effects, counterion release, sequence-specific long-range electrostatics, and short-range interactions.41–44 We envision that these new methodologies, based on physics-based interaction matrices and dynamical features, will add to the emerging toolsets to advance IDP classification and IDP complexation, an unmet challenge in the field. We also anticipate that these predictions and differences between models will motivate new experiments for functional classification.


## METHODS
  We utilized various physics-derived sequence-dependent metrics to classify NCBD and CID sequences. These metrics broadly quantify interactions that determine the ensemble average distances between residue pairs, resulting in interaction maps. We hypothesize that the similarity (or dissimilarity) in these interaction maps between two intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) determines their conformational similarity (or dissimilarity) and their tendency to interact with other partners, ultimately impacting their function (see Figure 2).

  First, we utilized the Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix (SCDM), which computes the electrostatic contribution to the ensemble average distance between two amino acids, yielding an electrostatic interaction map showing regions of attraction and repulsion (similar to Figure 2 left panel, see Section 3 for actual maps). However, SCDM specifically captures electrostatic patterning and is not sensitive to noncharge interactions. To overcome this limitation, we next employed the Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Matrix (SHDM), which accounts for sequence effects of noncharge amino acids, important for different NCBD sequences that can have identical charge patterning. Thus, SHDM produces maps similar to SCDM but originating from sequence-dependent nonelectrostatic interactions. SHDM maps—unlike SCDM maps—are difficult to analytically calculate directly from sequence. We extracted SHDM from the ensemble average distances between residues from all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. However, MD simulations can be time-consuming and may not always be feasible. Therefore, we sought another measure of the sequence effect due to noncharge residues by considering the Sequence Hydropathy Decoration decomposition (SHDd). SHDd captures binary patterning of different classes of amino acids and can be determined without MD simulation. The interaction matrices (SCDM, SHDM, SHDd) were flattened to represent each protein as a vector, which was used to create a protein matrix. This high-dimensional protein matrix was projected to a lower dimensional space with PCA. Similarity (or dissimilarity) between proteins was determined as distances in this reduced PC space, which was subsequently used to classify proteins (see Figure 2). In addition to these interaction maps, we applied the Dynamic Flexibility Index (DFI) profile. DFI quantifies the response at a particular residue due to perturbations elsewhere. DFI has been shown to be relevant in the functional classification of folded proteins but has yet to be used in IDPs. After classifying proteins based on their similarity (or dissimilarity) using statistical physics-based metrics, we built models to quantitatively describe binding data between different combinations of NCBD and CID sequences. The details of these methods are described below.


### Computing Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix.
  Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix SCDMij computes electrostatic interactions between any two amino acids, i and j, and can be further used to compute the ensemble average distance ⟨Rij2⟩=⟨Ri-Rj2⟩ between i, j, where Ri and Rj are position vectors of some representative atoms of residues i and j. Specifically, SCDM is defined as (see refs 15, 18, and 19 for more details):  (1) SCDMi,j=1(i-j)∑m=ji∑n=1j-1qmqn(m-j)2(m-n)3/2+∑m=j+1i∑n=jm-1qmqn(m-n)1/2+∑m=i+1N∑n=1j-1qmqn(i-j)2(m-n)3/2+∑m=i+1N∑n=jiqmqn(i-n)2(m-n)3/2  with qi=-1 for amino acids D, E; qi=1 for amino acids R, K; and zero otherwise. Thus, SCDM gives an electrostatic patterning map (similar to the ones shown in Figure 2; see Section 3 for more details). All SCDM values were binarized to create a binary Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix (bSCDM) that captures the patterns of electrostatic maps depicting regions of attraction and repulsion rather than their actual magnitude. These maps have been useful in clustering functionally similar IDPs with high charge content.28 The bSCDM maps were created by the following assignment scheme: if the SCDM value is less than zero, a value of “−1” is assigned to that entry; if the SCDM value is greater than zero, a value of “+1” is assigned to that entry, and zero otherwise.


### Computing Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Matrix.
  Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Matrix (SCDMij) is similar to SCDMij, except that it characterizes nonelectrostatic interactions used to calculate Rij2. As a result, SHDM yields maps of nonelectrostatic interactions. SHDM is defined as:  (2) SHDMi,j=1(i-j)∑m=ji∑n=1j-1ωm,n(m-j)2(m-n)5/2+∑m=j+1i∑n=jm-1ωm,n(m-n)2(m-n)5/2+∑m=i+1N∑n=jiωm,n(i-n)2(m-n)5/2+∑m=i+1N∑n=1j-1ωm,n(i-j)2(m-n)5/2

  The exponent of 5/2 in the denominator (of all the terms) arises due to short-range (modeled by delta function) excluded volume interaction.15 The exponent becomes 3/2 (as seen in eq 1 for SCDM) for a long-range electrostatic interaction. This matrix along with SCDM can be used to predict Rij2 using the equation below (see ref 18 for details).  (3) xij3/21-1/xij=32π3/2SHDMijxij+32π3ω3Tij|i-j|xij5/2+lbl23πSCDMij  where xij=Rij2/|i-j|l2 is a dimensionless chain expansion term, with l=5.5Å, lb=7.2Å at room temperature, ω3 is a 3-body repulsive term, and Tij is defined in the earlier work.18 Computing SHDMij requires determining amino acid pair (m,n)-specific interaction parameters, ωm,n (in eq 2), which are not accurately known. However, the formalism can be inverted to extract SHDMij if the ensemble average distance between two amino acids i,j are known from experiment or simulation. Briefly, from all-atom explicit solvent simulation (see Sections S.2 and S.3 for details of MD simulation), we compute the ensemble average of distance, Rij2=⟨Ri-Rj2⟩, where Ri and Rj are the positions of the Cα carbons of residue i and residue j, respectively. From this, we subsequently extract xij=Rij2/|i-j|l2 and insert in eq 3 to infer SHDMij. This is possible since other terms (SCDMij and Tij) in eq 3 are known from the sequence. We note that this is a special feature of the theory that can be utilized to extract these interaction matrices from experiments or simulations (coarse-grained or all-atom). We have used a similar strategy in a recent work, where end-to-end distance from simulation was used to extract specific elements of the matrix.45


### Computing Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Decomposition (SHDd).
  As noted above, computing SHDM requires knowledge of Rij2 values from simulation or experiment. Here, we seek coarse-grained sequence patterning metrics of nonelectrostatic interactions that can be directly computed from the sequence without requiring simulation. For this purpose, we consider the Sequence Hydropathy Decoration (SHD) metric, derived as the contribution of nonelectrostatic interaction to the ensemble average end-to-end distance. Specifically, SHD is defined as:  (4) SHD=∑m=1N∑n=1m-1ωm,n(m-n)-1/2  where m, n are labels of different amino acids in the chain backbone.15 This metric was first termed SHD by Zheng et al.46 Converting the summation over different amino acid types, SHD can be rewritten as:  (5) SHD=∑i=1M∑j=iMωi,jAij;Aij=∑m=1N∑n=1m-1δi,mδj,n(m-n)-1/2  where i and j are different types of amino acids (or alphabets), and M is the number of different types of residues. The value of M could range between 1 (if all amino acids are assumed to be identical) and 20 (when all 20 amino acids are considered distinct). For the purpose presented here, we reduce the residue set to eight (M=8) with the following grouping: polar (S, T, N, Q, C, H), hydrophobic (I, L, M, V), acidic (E, D), basic (R, K), aromatic (F, W, Y), as well as an alanine, proline, and glycine group. These groupings create an 8 × 8 matrix with 36 unique elements and have been extensively used in efforts to construct statistical patterning metrics by the Pappu group.47 Aij is a specific decomposition of the SHD metric when the summation (in m,n residues in the chain sequence) is restricted between two specific types of residues i,j imposed by the delta function constraint. Consequently, Aijs yield a binary patterning metric for residue types i and j, where i and j can be dissimilar or identical residue types. A normalized version of this decomposition is then constructed as:  (6) SHDdi,j=Ai,j/∑i,jAi,j

  Thus, SHDd produces physics-based binary patterning metrics that are also related to Ree2 and do not require knowledge of the actual interaction parameters (ωm,n) between different types of residues. For an objective comparison between sequences, it is useful to normalize these values further with respect to a reference set and compute a z-score, as has been done in previous work.3,47 We choose the reference set to be the collection of all IDPs in human also known as human IDRome5 and compute the z-score as:  (7) SHDdzij=SHDdij-SHDdijSHDdij2-SHDdij2  where ⟨.⟩ denotes the average over the IDRome. In short, the SHDdz matrix yields binary patterning of two different (or the same) groups of amino acids in the background of the entire human IDRome and has been used for functional analysis in this paper. To intuitively understand the meaning of this metric, let us consider two sequences with the same composition (of two different amino acid types A and B) but with different patterning: sequence 1 (ABABAB) has A’s and B’s well mixed, while sequence 2 (AAABBB) has A’s and B’s completely segregated. SHDd metric (between A and B) will be higher for sequence 1 compared to sequence 2. On the other hand, SHDd metric (between A and A) will be higher for sequence 2 compared to sequence 1. In short, SHDd between the same amino acid types will be higher for sequences having a contiguous stretch of the same amino acid type, while SHDd between different amino acid types will be higher for sequences having the two amino acids well mixed.


### Calculating DFI.
  Dynamic Flexibility Index (DFI) quantifies the dynamic stability of a given position. Namely, DFI measures how much a given position responds to perturbations applied at all other positions in the protein, thereby capturing its sensitivity to global structural dynamics. Positions with high DFI values are more flexible, indicating greater responsiveness to distal perturbations, while positions with low DFI values are more rigid, reflecting structural resilience and transferring the perturbation to the rest of the protein. Because DFI integrates the effects of long-range dynamic interactions, it provides valuable insights into the functional roles of residues, particularly those involved in allosteric regulation, signal propagation, or structural stabilization.48,49

  The method for obtaining DFIs can be found in previous work.39,50–52 In brief, DFI is based on the perturbation response scanning (PRS) method,37,38,53 in which the C-alpha atom of each residue in the protein is modeled as a node in an Elastic Network Model (ENM). Within the elastic network, a small perturbation in the form of an external random force (i.e., Brownian kick) is sequentially applied to each node in the network, and the perturbation response of all nodes is recorded according to linear response theory, as shown in eq 8.

  Here, H denotes the Hessian matrix, which can be computed directly from the atomic coordinates. F is the applied force, and ΔR is the residue response displacement vector due to the applied force. When the Dynamic Flexibility Index (DFI) is calculated using the Elastic Network Model (ENM), H is derived from the second derivative of the harmonic potential with respect to Cartesian coordinates. However, since ENM is a coarse-grained model, DFI can be made more accurate and sensitive to mutations by using covariance matrices obtained from all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In this approach, the Hessian value is approximated as the inverse of the positional covariance matrix.

  To compute the DFI, isotropic perturbations are applied one by one to each residue (j), and the resulting fluctuation responses are recorded at all other positions (i). Repeating this perturbation process for every residue in the structure-encoded network yields the full DFI profile for the protein as:  (9) DFIi=∑j=1NΔRji∑i=1N∑j=1NΔRji

  In this profile, the numerator represents the total fluctuation response of residue (i) when all other positions (j) are perturbed, while the denominator normalizes these values against the overall flexibility of the protein. Our methodology adheres to a recognized protocol for assessing protein dynamics convergence using DFI, which has been documented in several studies.54 We utilized 50 ns intervals, with a 5 ns overlap, for computing covariance matrices throughout the final 1.5 or 2.0 μs (whichever had a higher covariance overlap and hence self-consistency;55 see Figure S1 for more details) of a 2.5-μs-long simulation, ensuring that the derived DFI profiles are consistent across different time windows. We also computed DFI profiles using 25, 75, and 100 ns-sized windows, illustrating the robust nature of this approach (see Figures S2 and S3).


### Building Joint Patterning Matrices to Model Complexation.
  To investigate the sequence-based features of NCBD and CID proteins and their role in complexation, the eight NCBD proteins and six CID proteins investigated by ref 29 were subjected to additional analysis. These proteins were analyzed using a combinatorial approach in which all possible NCBD–CID pairings were generated to form the input protein pair matrix. The protein pair matrix was constructed by concatenating (joining) the flattened SHDM (or SHDdz) of each CID to the corresponding SHDM (or SHDdz) of its paired NCBD. This produced a n×p protein matrix, where each row ni represented a single NCBD–CID complex (e.g., NCBD1 concatenated with CID1). After constructing this joint patterning matrix (from SHDM or SHDdz), all NCBD–CID pairings (16 out of 48 pairs) lacking measured Kd values were removed from the data set before Principal Component Analysis was performed. The principal components (PCs) extracted from this analysis were then correlated with measured Kd values29 for the corresponding pair (see Figure S4 for more details).


### PCA-Based Classification.
  Patterning metrics arranged in SHDM, SCDM, SHDdz (or joint matrices discussed above), and DFI are high-dimensional and may require projection to lower-dimensional space to detect important signatures. For this purpose, we used principal component analysis (PCA, see S.4 in the SI for details of PCA). PCA-based DFI profiles have previously been used to compare the function of folded proteins, particularly to differentiate those with high binding affinity from those without.54,56,57 Similarly, PCA of the patterning matrix has been used in the functional classification of IDPs.28 See SI (section S.5) for further information on how PCs are used to compute the distance matrix and subsequently dendrograms.


### Modeling Binding Free Energy of Complexation between Two Disordered Sequences.
  To calculate the binding free energies of the two sequences, we created composite IDPs by covalently linking the two IDPs at their ends. For every NCBD–CID pair, we consider four possible combinations: (i) C-terminus end of sequence A linked to N-terminus end of sequence B, (ii) C-terminus end of sequence A linked with C-terminus end of sequence B, (iii) N-terminus end of sequence A linked with C-terminus end of sequence B, and (iv) N-terminus end of sequence A linked with N-terminus end of sequence B. Next we use eq 10 below derived by us earlier16,19 to compute the free energy of an IDP with an arbitrary sequence.   x is a measure of chain dimension, and the free energy is minimized with respect to x to determine the most likely conformation and free energy at this conformation. The electrostatic component of the free energy is determined from the sequence-dependent Q term, computed by using q=-1 for amino acid residues D, E; q=1 for amino acid residues R, K; and zero for everything else. The nonelectrostatic interaction parameter ω2 is sequence dependent, and its value is determined from a physics-based machine learning algorithm trained against coarse-grained simulation, with the three-body excluded volume parameter ω3=0.2 (see ref 45 for more details). For each composite sequence, the minimum free energy is computed for these different constructs, and the lowest value from these minima was chosen as the free energy of the complex.


## RESULTS

### Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix Reveals Distinct Electrostatic Patterns in the CID Family.
  Previous work has shown that binary SCDM (bSCDM; see Section 2) can be used to detect functionally similar IDP sequences with high charge content.28 Consequently, we reasoned that bSCDM may be useful to classify highly charged sequences, such as the CID family (average charge fraction of 30%). Two major patterns emerge from the bSCDM maps of CID proteins (see Figure 3). NCOA1, NCOA3, and 1R have similar maps, where the C-terminal region exhibits attractive electrostatic interactions (blue). On the other hand, the same region in 2R, Fish, and NCOA2 sequences exhibits repulsion (red). It is interesting to note that the bSCDM map of 1R is different from that of 2R and Fish, despite 1R having high sequence homology with Fish and 2R. An implication of this finding is that sequences with high similarity can still have distinct electrostatic patterning maps.

  While visual inspection of these maps is insightful, an unsupervised classification scheme is needed to detect these differences (in the maps) in an automated and quantitative manner. For this purpose, we projected bSCDM maps to lower dimensions using PCA, followed by clustering based on distances in the low-dimensional PC space (see Section 2 and Sections S.4 and S.5 for more details). We expect that dimensional reduction using PCA will select primary features of the maps, yielding a classification similar to the one detected visually but performed in an automated manner. This approach was successful in our earlier study of the unsupervised classification of Ste50 proteins.28 As expected, this automated classification scheme groups CID proteins (see Figure 4 top left panel) into two major clusters: C1 (with NCOA1, NCOA3, 1R) and C2 (containing NCOA2, 2R, Fish), in agreement with the two groups visually detected from the original SCDM maps.

  This classification is consistent with several observations. First, we note that Fish and 2R are close in the evolutionary timeline and have very high sequence homology, supporting their grouping together. Furthermore, 2R is the ancestral sequence for NCOA2. The predicted grouping of NCOA1 and NCOA3 in C1 suggests that these two proteins are more similar than NCOA2. While it is not possible to conclusively support the prediction in the absence of direct measurement of function, we notice several similarities between NCOA1 and NCOA3 compared to NCOA2. They have similar binding affinities with the NCBD domain of human CREBBP and human p300.29 Since binding to NCBD is a critical aspect of CID function, it is reasonable to expect that binding affinity can be an approximate measure of function. Moreover, NCOA1 and NCOA3 have similar response curves measuring gain in helicity upon the addition of a helix stabilizer.29 Finally, we note that the classification remains unchanged even if bSCDM maps are directly compared without PCA (see Figure S5). However, we retain PCA-based analysis, consistent with our earlier work28 and due to its relevance in modeling the binding of NCBD and CID sequences, as will be seen in Section 3.5.

  Next, we test bSCDM’s ability to group sequences in the NCBD families (see Figure 4 top right panel). The sequence from Dmel is clustered separately, consistent with its unusually weak affinity for CID proteins. This sequence is also the furthest from all of the other sequences in the evolutionary context. However, bSCDM fails to distinguish the sequences of Pmar, Fish, HsapCREBBP, 1R2R, and Drer. These sequences have identical charge patterning and differ only in the arrangement of noncharge amino acids, which are not detectable by bSCDM. We conclude that for sequences with low charge content, such as the NCBD family, classifying based on charge patterning alone is insufficient.


### Noncharge Patterning Is Needed to Classify NCBD Sequences.
  Realizing the inadequacy of bSCDM to classify several NCBD sequences, we used Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Matrix (SHDM) to detect noncharge patterning. At present, we cannot directly predict these matrices for an arbitrary sequence due to our inability to model amino acid pair-specific interaction parameters (ωm,n in eq 2). However, our theoretical formalism can extract these noncharge interaction matrices directly from simulations (all-atom or coarse-grained), as described in the Section 2. Using SHDM combined with PCA, we find NCBD sequences from DP and Dmel are separated from the rest of the homologues (see Figure 4 bottom right). This grouping is consistent with the binding affinity data. Both NCBD sequences (from DP and Dmel), compared to all the other NCBD sequences, have very different binding affinities with the CID domains of all three human paralogues. Notably, these two proteins are in a different lineage in the evolutionary tree. Furthermore, SHDM-based analysis detects differences between sequences that were previously invisible using bSCDM. Sequences from Pmar and 1R2R are grouped closely, consistent with the observation that Pmar and 1R2R directly originated from the same lineage (see Figure 1). A broader cluster is formed with five sequences—Pmar, Drer, 1R2R, Hsapp300, and Hsap CREBBP—which is also consistent with the phylogenetic tree in Figure 1. Moreover, Pmar, Hsap300, and 1R2R sequences have similar binding affinities with the CID domains of the human paralogues (see Table 1 in ref 29). However, we also notice a deviation from the expectation when considering the sequence from Fish. This sequence is expected to be close to 1R2R, Drer, Pmar, and HaspCREBBP, based on sequence homology (see Table S2) but is separated from this grouping. This difference could be due to several reasons. First, SHDM-based analysis is expected to yield functional similarity that may not fully correlate with sequence similarity, even among sequences (such as NCBD) with high similarity compared to typical IDPs. It is possible that SHDM captures subtle features such as protein dynamics that are only revealed by all-atom MD simulation and not by sequence alignment. It is also possible that SHDM only partially captures features related to function, and there are other latent variables responsible for function that are not fully accounted for by SHDM. For example, electrostatic interaction quantified by bSCDM, but neglected in SHDM, may be needed to detect more subtle differences. Finally, it is important to note that binding data for Fish with other proteins is unavailable. Consequently, it is not possible to compare Fish with other proteins purely based on the binding data. At present, we are unable to determine the reason for this difference.

  The classification described above again correlates with the visual inspection of SHDM maps (see Figure 5). We notice that Pmar, Drer, 1R2R, HsapCREBBP, and Hsap300 have similar maps with an uniformly attractive (blue) patch. This is distinct from the maps of Fish, DP, and Dmel, where small patches of highly attractive (dark blue) regions are embedded in regions of moderate attraction (light blue). The maps of Fish, DP, and Dmel are also different from each other, consistent with their own branches seen in the dendrogram generated with PCA-based classification. For example, Dmel has three islands of repulsive (red) and two islands of attractive (blue) regions compared to two islands of repulsive regions in Fish and only one small repulsive region in DP. Thus, we conclude PCA-based analysis of SHDM is able to select primary features of maps. Finally, we again notice that classification using SHDM with PCA compares well with that without PCA (see Figure S6).

  Next, we revisited the classification of CID protein sequences using SHDM only (see Figure 4 bottom left panel). We noticed that 1R and 2R sequences are now grouped together; however, NCOA2 is detected as a closer homologue to this cluster than Fish, which is at odds with the bSCDM-based clustering and evolutionary tree. This highlights that SHDM alone may not be sufficient to cluster highly charged IDPs. We thus conclude that charge-patterning-based metrics are useful for classifying sequences with high charge content, while SHDM (extracted from all-atom MD simulation) is more suitable for classifying sequences with low charge content and/or sequences having identical charge patterning.


### Classification with SHDdz Offers a Complementary Approach through Noncharge Patterning.
  While the above analysis shows the importance of SHDM, it requires knowledge of average distances from the MD simulation. However, MD simulation is computationally expensive and not always feasible, particularly for large protein sequences. We seek other measures of nonelectrostatic patterning metrics that can be directly computed from the sequence alone. For this purpose, we used SHDdz as detailed in the Section 2. This metric accounts for interactions between specific amino acid types that contribute to the calculation of the global dimension (Ree2; end-to-end distance). Using this classification scheme for NCBD, we notice HsapCREBBP, Drer, Pmar, Fish, and 1R2R form a cluster on their own (see Figure 6 right panel). Furthermore, Hsapp300, DP, and Dmel are outside of this cluster, with Hsap300 being the closest and Dmel being the furthest. As before, an intuitive understanding of this classification can be gained from the actual SHDdz maps (see Figure S7). We conclude that SHDdz-based classification is in broad agreement with SHDM-based analysis, as shown in Figure 4 (bottom right panel). For CID proteins, SHDdz-based classification (see Figure S8 for the actual maps) yields a grouping different from bSCDM (see Figure 4 top left panel). It groups Fish, 2R, and 1R sequences together, consistent with their high sequence homology (see Table S1). The human paralogues (NCOA1, NCOA2, and NCOA3) are classified on their own, indicating dissimilarity between the paralogues and their divergence from the ancestral sequences. This classification is also different from SHDM-based classification (in Figure 4 bottom left). To reiterate, at present, we do not have experiments to conclusively prove or disprove these differences seen in CID classification. It is also important to note that SHDdz does not capture details of long-range electrostatics, unlike bSCDM-based metrics.


### DFI Provides Broad Agreement with Other Physics-Based Classification Tools.
  It has been well accepted that protein dynamics play a critical role in dictating the function of folded domains,58–61 and the Dynamic Flexibility Index (DFI) has proven useful in the functional classification of folded proteins, particularly in the characterization of enzymatic activities,62 binding,56,57and mutational outcomes.63 However, DFI or other dynamics-based metrics have not been previously applied to classify intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs).

  Using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation data, we computed DFI profiles for each protein and subsequently performed classification using principal component analysis (PCA; see Section 2 and Supporting Information for more details). We found that DFI-based clustering grouped the 1R and 2R variants similarly for CID, but distinctly separated the Fish sequence—unlike the clustering results based on bSCDM analysis (compare Figure 7 with Figure 4). Interestingly, this approach identified NCOA2 and NCOA1 as being more similar to each other than to NCOA3. This grouping can be directly understood from their DFI profiles: NCOA3 exhibits two distinct basins of low DFI, indicating the presence of hinge-like regions, which are absent in the profiles of NCOA1 and NCOA2 (see Figure S2). These predictions and their differences from bSCDM-based classifications highlight the multifunctionality of highly disordered proteins. Such proteins may share similarity with respect to one functional mechanism but differ in others. Consequently, different classification schemes may capture distinct features encoded in the sequence that are responsible for specific sets of functions, thereby yielding different groupings.

  For NCBD, we observe strong agreement between DFI and SHDdz-based analysis. Figure S3 provides the DFI profile for these sequences individually. We find that HsapCREBBP, Drer, Pmar, Fish, and 1R2R are all grouped in a primary cluster, consistent with SHDdz-based grouping. Next, we notice Hsap300, DP, and Dmel sequences form their own branch, with Hsap300 and Dmel being the closest and furthest, respectively, from the main cluster. This grouping is very similar to SHDdz-based clustering (see Figure 6). To quantitatively test this similarity, we compared the distance between each pair of sequences using the two classification schemes, DFI and SHDdz (see Figure S9). We also notice that DFI groups proteins similarly to SHDM with the only exception being Fish (see Figures 4 and S10). Overall, we conclude that DFI-based analysis retains the main characteristics of SHDdz and some characteristics of SHDM-based analyses, indicating a fundamental commonality of these classification tools when considering sequences with moderate charge, such as NCBD.


### Biophysical Metrics Correlate with Binding Affinity between NCBD and CID Sequences.
  Previous sections highlight the importance of high-dimensional physics-inspired interaction matrices and vectors of flexibility profiles (DFI) to classify IDP sequences. However, the majority of these classifications could not be compared against quantitative data. Here, we provide a quantitative test of patterning metrics to model experimentally measured binding affinities between NCBD and CID sequences. Modeling the complexation of IDPs with other proteins—while maintaining their fuzzy mode—is also timely due to its functional relevance and the recent interest it has received.42–44,64–67 We reasoned that complexation between two proteins would be largely affected by their interaction matrices, especially the sequence hydropathy decoration matrix (SHDM) or decomposition (SHDdz) since one of the partner sequences (NCBD) is low in charge. Motivated by this, we consider the joint SHDM of the pairs of proteins by appending the two matrices, as described in the Section 2 (also see Figure S4). We find that the third principal component (PC3) of the combined matrix correlates reasonably well with the binding data for all pairs (see Figure 8 left panel).

  Next, we repeated the analysis using SHDdz and again found that the third PC of the joint SHDdz matrix correlates well with the binding data (see Figure 8 right panel). This correlation highlights the importance of statistical physics-derived metrics not only to cluster individual protein families but also to guide binding affinity between two sequences. We note that higher PCs (PC1 or PC2), however, do not yield any correlation. We find that higher PCs account for primary differences within CID sequences (see Figures S11 and S12) but not features of NCBD sequences. From the binding data, however, it is evident that primary variation in the binding constant arises from changes in NCBD sequences, followed by more subtle differences in the binding partners (CID sequences). We find that PC3 captures features of NCBD sequences (see Figure S13). Thus, we conclude that PC3 of the joint matrix captures features of NCBD and subtle features of CID in the context of NCBD, which are needed to describe complexation.

  Next, we built a model based on the interaction sites between two proteins. We used the server FINCHES68 to identify the most likely interaction sites between the two proteins, and subsequently DFI of these sites was averaged. Figure S14 (left panel) shows the average DFI of the interaction sites also has a modest correlation with the binding constant. To ensure such a correlation arises only by considering interaction sites, we performed a control study by considering interaction sites chosen at random, different from the ones detected using FINCHES. Consistent with our expectation, this control study does not correlate well with Kd (see Figure S14, right panel). However, we do not use the raw score of FINCHES, as they do not correlate well with the binding affinities (see Figure S15). This analysis shows that SHDM, SHDdz, and DFI-based analysis can correlate with the binding constant, albeit to different extents. This is consistent with our earlier observation that SHDM, SHDdz, and DFI yield similar classifications of NCBD sequences.

  Although the metrics discussed so far show a modest correlation with binding affinities, they are not direct measures of free energy. In a complementary approach to the previous methods, we provide an approximate but direct calculation of complexation free energy between two IDPs. We assume that the free energy of complexation can be approximated by the free energy of a composite IDP created by covalently linking the two sequences (one NCBD and one CID). Our theoretical formalism can be used to construct the free energy of this combined IDP as a function of sequence (see Section 2 for more details). We note that this model has two major approximations. First, it assumes that complexation free energy can be described by the free energy of a composite IDP generated by covalently linking two IDPs in four possible combinations. But there could be alternate modes of binding forming contacts between two chains elsewhere. Second, the free-energy expression uses a coarse-grained description for both the electrostatic and nonelectrostatic contributions (trained from coarse-grained simulation). We also neglect important processes such as counterion release, which is significant in complexation between highly but oppositely charged IDPs.42 However, we expect this to be a minor effect here since one of the binding partners (NCBD) is low in charge. Despite these assumptions, the approximate free energy of complexation correlates well with measured binding affinity (Kd) values (see Figure 9) with two notable outliers with high Kd. Complexes with high Kd values are the least likely to bind. Our approach to covalently link two sequences that are not likely to complex may artificially induce constraints, predicting a lower-than-expected free energy of binding, which explains the deviation noted for the two pairs. Nevertheless, based on the overall agreement between a physical quantity such as free energy, albeit approximate, and data, we conclude that relative binding affinities may be approximated by our approach when considering complexation between two IDPs that are expected to bind. To test the robustness of our approach, we also repeated the analysis by inserting a linker of 10 alanine residues (expected to minimally affect interaction with the two IDPs based on the CALVADOS hydropathy score69) between the two sequences. Adding linkers does not significantly change the correlation (see Figure S16). We envision our approach will add to the existing studies and models42,70 and will stimulate further experimental studies to measure and estimate binding affinities between IDP chains and their variants to further test these models.


## DISCUSSION
  Coarse-grained Hamiltonians can be used to analytically compute several conformational features as functions of sequence. Consequently, the formalism yields mathematical functions that are both measures of interactions and representations of a sequence. These sequence decoration metrics can be arranged in high-dimensional matrix called binary Sequence Charge Decoration Matrix (bSCDM), Sequence Hydropathy Decoration Matrix (SHDM), and the z-score Sequence Hydropathy Decoration decomposition (SHDdz). While bSCDM can be directly computed using Coulomb’s law within polymer physics principles, SHDM is difficult to compute since amino acid pair-specific interaction parameters are unknown. One advantage of our formalism is that it can be inverted to extract these matrix elements if conformational features, such as distance maps, are known. We leverage this feature to infer SHDM of a protein directly from simulated distance maps. If all-atom simulation is used, the extracted SHDM will yield interaction matrices arising from an atomistic-level description. Thus, our analytical framework offers a way to bridge simulation and theory to quantitatively build sequence-dependent interaction matrices. We also extracted dynamic flexibility index (DFI) profiles from an all-atom simulation. To further compute noncharge patterning in the absence of simulation, we used z-score Sequence Hydropathy Decoration decomposition (SHDdz) matrix. We used these rigorously derived patterning matrices and dynamic profiles to classify two families of IDP sequences: CID (highly charged) and NCBD (relatively less charged). The choice of these sequences was motivated by the extensive measurements and availability of several extant and ancient sequences based on earlier work.29 Binary SCDM (bSCDM) maps reveal two groups with distinct patterns: group 1 (with NCOA1, NCOA3, and 1R) has a clear region of attraction near the C-terminal region, which is absent in group 2 (with 2R, NCOA2, and Fish). While bSCDM-based classification agrees with the evolutionary timeline and binding data, it also differs from other classification schemes, indicating the importance of accurately modeling long-range electrostatics. However, bSCDM fails to detect dissimilarities between different NCBD sequences that are low in charge or have identical charge patterning. For these sequences, patterning metrics beyond electrostatics are needed. Visual inspection of these nonelectrostatic patterning maps of NCBD sequences reveals distinct features and similarities (or dissimilarities) between proteins, yielding a broad classification. These insights were quantitatively established by an unsupervised approach using PCA followed by clustering. SHDM, SHDdz, and DFI-based analyses show several similarities. Broad agreement between SHDM, SHDdz, and DFI suggests a deeper link among these approaches. One possibility is that the interaction matrix elements also dictate fluctuations that, in turn, relate to DFI profiles. Future work is needed to build a new analytical theory to derive measures of fluctuations and bridge physics-derived patterning metrics with DFI profiles.71

  Furthermore, joint decoration matrices constructed by combining patterning metrics (SHDdz, SHDM) of NCBD and CID proteins hold clues about binding affinities between different NCBD and CID sequences. Moreover, relative binding affinities can be reasonably modeled using a coarse-grained sequence-dependent free energy of a composite IDP chain constructed by covalently linking the two complexing IDPs. There are also some caveats to the present work. We assumed that charges are fully ionized in the calculation of bSCDM. However, recent work20,72–74 has shown charges may be only partially ionized, and the degree of ionization would depend on conformation and sequence patterning. Advanced theories are needed to account for such effects in the calculation of charge patterning. Our exhaustive analysis also shows differences between different analyses. These differences could reflect either the superiority of certain methods over others or the possibility that different classification schemes identify different functional features. To distinguish between the two possibilities, we need careful experiments of function using genetic manipulations, in vivo characterization of protein expression, and phenotypic measurements similar to previous studies in other model systems.2,7


## CONCLUSION
  We present a statistical mechanics-based approach to functionally classify intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs). Specifically, we use sequence-dependent interaction maps that relate to distance maps and are defined by analytical relations derived from a coarse-grained Hamiltonian. While some of these maps, such as electrostatic interaction, can be easily computed from the sequence, the nonelectrostatic map is extracted from simulation. Thus, in addition to analytically computing patterning metrics, our theory also provides a formalism to relate all-atom simulations to coarse-grained interaction maps, bridging theory and simulation. We also computed dynamic profiles that have been routinely used in the classification of folded proteins but not in IDPs. We hypothesize that these interaction maps and dynamical profiles provide a molecular blueprint, and their similarity (or dissimilarity) determines their ability to interact with other proteins and may serve as an approximate measure of their function. This hypothesis was tested on a model system of two IDPs, NCBD and CID, which have coevolved and vary in their charge and secondary structure content. Different classification schemes largely agree in their classification of the NCBD protein (low charge) and are consistent with biophysical measurements. However, for the highly charged protein such as CID, electrostatics patterning-based interaction reveals two distinct clusters that are not captured by nonelectrostatic patterning maps, highlighting the importance of correctly modeling long-range electrostatics. Our physics-based formalism also provides tools to model the complexation between the two IDPs, NCBD and CID sequences. We hope that such a systematic analysis of a novel system, which has not been previously addressed, will contribute to the emerging field of functional classification and complexation of IDPs.
