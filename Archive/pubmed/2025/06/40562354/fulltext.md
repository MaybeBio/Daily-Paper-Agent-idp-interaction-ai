# NMR in the age of modern biomedical research and drug discovery


## Abstract
  The success of artificial intelligence for structure prediction has led to forecasts of a reduced need for experimental structural biology. Here, we focus on nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and its various applications to biomedical research, including and extending beyond structural biology. As a structural tool, NMR is highly complementary to X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and uniquely suited for studies of intrinsically disordered and dynamic systems in real time. It produces spectral fingerprints of biomolecules at the atomic scale to provide information on the structure, interactions, and motions that occur in solution. We focus on the power of NMR to characterize moderately sized dynamic systems, identify and validate the binding of small molecule ligands to biomolecular targets, and its capacity to further develop and be empowered by artificial intelligence-based structural prediction software. Experimental structural biology begins with sample preparation, and we present recent advancements for overcoming hurdles associated with reconstitution of biological systems for NMR studies and ongoing challenges, including in the application of NMR for cell-based research. NMR is aptly suited to bench-to-bedside efforts for its ability to do metabolomic profiling and follow drug response. We present the capacity of NMR to delve into complexities that are the foundation of biological function and focus on its ongoing challenges and opportunities to apply NMR for the advancement of biomedical research.


## Introduction
  NMR is a highly versatile technology that is routinely used to characterize small molecules and large biomolecular complexes alike. To determine structures of larger biomolecular complexes by NMR, a range of sophisticated NMR techniques have been developed. These include multidimensional NMR experiments [1–3], methodologies for measuring paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE) [4, 5] and residual dipolar couplings (RDCs) approaches [6, 7], which collectively provide short-range and long-range distance information and orientation restraints. Complementing these experimental advances, several transformative computational tools were developed to process, analyze, visualize, and calculate or refine structures from NMR data. We provide a representative list of these software packages in Table 1 and apologize for any omissions. All except CYANA as well as additional NMR software and other computational packages are accessible at the NMRbox server [8], which was developed to share resources for biomolecular NMR.

  NMR is considered one of the three major technologies for solving 3-dimensional (3D) structures of biomolecules, along with X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. In contrast to these other technologies however, NMR experiments are performed in solution rather than a crystalline or frozen state, allowing observation of real-time reactions and dynamics. This capability enables unique biological insights on how dynamics can drive molecular function and signaling pathways.

  Notably, NMR also allows for metabolic studies, including of clinical samples, allowing characterization of different disease states and the possibility to follow treatment response. As a chemical biology tool, NMR can be used for drug design, at the initial stage of small molecule screening to later stages of drug optimization based on structural characterization of the drug-target complex. We discuss here these many applications of NMR, focusing on the opportunities and challenges that have arisen in the application of NMR to biomolecules and biomedical research. We start with sample preparation and extend to integration of artificial intelligence for data analyses and structural studies.


## Engineering Samples for NMR
  The first challenge for any structural biology project is sample production. In the case of protein NMR, the study of biomolecules generally requires isotope labeling with 15N, 13C, or 2H. Such labeling is routinely achieved by protein expression in E. coli with media containing 15N-NH4Cl and/or 13C-glucose as sole nitrogen and/or carbon sources and the replacement of H2O with 2H2O [25–27]. Some proteins or protein complexes are challenging to grow in E. coli, particularly with 2H2O-based media. Expression of mammalian proteins is optimized by replacing codons rare to bacteria with ones better suited for E. coli expression [28], and co-expressing binding partners or molecular chaperones has aided in the production of intact samples. Co-expression systems offer a distinct advantage for selective isotope-labeling of a protein within a multi-protein complex. By isotopically labeling only one component of a multi-component complex, the spectral complexity is reduced and methods can be implemented to enrich for interactions either within or between asymmetrically labeled components [29]. The LEGO NMR method was developed using protein expression plasmids with either T7 or araBAD promoters to allow for separate expression of proteins from the same E. coli strain by alternating between labeled and unlabeled media [30]. Building on this approach, a dual-promoter plasmid containing both T7 and araBAD promoters was introduced [31]. This dual-promoter plasmid is compatible with other pET-duet vectors for co-expression in E. coli [32], allowing expression of up to eight proteins in the same culture. This selective labeling approach was used to investigate the HIV-1 Vif (viral infectivity factor)-CBFβ (core binding factor β) complex, as Vif remains disordered and insoluble without CBFβ [33]. Vif is a crucial accessory protein for HIV-1, facilitating viral infection of human cells by degrading host restriction factors through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway [34]. Before this vector system, assigning NMR signals for Vif had been stymied by its insolubility. This approach was applied to express the structured and soluble CBFβ protein prior to expression of the intrinsically disordered and insoluble Vif protein, resulting in the formation of a soluble Vif-CBFβ heterodimer complex [31]. It also enabled selective isotope labeling of Vif within its complex with CBFβ by either uniformly or amino acid selectively labeling Vif with 13C, 15N, and 2H while CBFβ remained unlabeled (Figure 1).


### Production of NMR active proteins in insect cells.
  Some large mammalian proteins, such as G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), have proven refractory to E. coli expression and require eukaryotic cells, which provide complex machineries for protein folding or membrane insertion as well as post-translational modifications [35–37]. Expression via baculovirus infection of insect cells provides an alternative system to mammalian cell expression that is easier to scale up to produce the large amounts of sample necessary for biomolecular NMR. Production of isotopically active amino acid mixtures in yeast and algae has reduced the costs associated with uniform labeling of insect cell media since labeled amino acids do not need to be separately added. Specific amino acid labeling is still beneficial for probing methionine methyl groups for binding and interaction studies. This labeling is done by adding 13C-ε-methyl-methionine into methionine free media and supplementing with deuterated algal amino acids [36–38].


### Production of NMR active proteins in mammalian cells.
  Significant effort has been invested to produce 15N and/or 13C labeled proteins in mammalian cells, either for purification and extracellular studies or use in cell-based experiments to monitor proteins in their cellular context [39–41]. Such labeling in mammalian cells involves the incorporation of 15N and/or 13C labeled amino acids into the cell culture media. Toward developing a more affordable stable-isotope labeling system for mammalian cells, continuous efforts have been made to reduce costs by evaluating labeling efficiency, optimizing conditions, and using more economical precursor compounds for labeling α-nitrogen, sidechain carbon, and protons with 15N, 13C, and 2H, respectively [42, 43].


### Cell free expression of NMR protein samples.
  In cases where expression in cells has failed, cell-free expression systems have offered an alternative for sample production [44, 45]. Cell-free protein expression systems are particularly useful for producing proteins that are toxic to cells and membrane proteins. It can be used to produce proteins with unnatural amino acids, post-translational modifications, or fluorophores. Stereo-Array Isotope Labeling (SAIL) was designed for cell free expression and enables the production of proteins with deuterated amino acids that have only a single 1H attached to each carbon or nitrogen [44, 46]. Incorporating amino acids labeled in this manner into proteins enhances NMR spectral resolution, facilitates stereospecific assignment of chiral protons, and yields more accurate structures. It also allows for selective methyl labeling in a deuterium background – a great benefit for large proteins. A drawback of this approach however is the high cost. A cost-effective approach was designed that takes advantage of E. coli to produce crude amino acids labeled as desired for the cell free expression of a protein of choice [45].

  An alternative cell-free expression system to SAIL is the wheat-germ [47] cell free expression system (WGCFS, CellFree Sciences Co., Ltd., Kanagawa, Japan) available for production of unlabeled proteins or uniformly 15N-labeled or 15N,13C-labeled proteins. Recently, this system allowed for the expression of a cytotoxic member of the APOBEC3 (apolipoprotein B mRNA Editing Enzyme, catalytic polypeptide-like 3) family of cytidine deaminases, APOBEC3A, for biophysical studies [48]. APOBEC3A expression in E. coli is extremely difficult due to the mutational activity of the enzyme leading to hypermutation of the bacterial genome and subsequent cell death. The WGCFS system allows for separation of the transcription and translation steps of protein synthesis, thereby reducing the impact of APOBEC3A mutational activity on the protein production since mRNA synthesis is completed before the active enzyme is generated, which can otherwise mutate the plasmid DNA.

  Overall, NMR as a biological tool has co-developed with molecular biology and protein chemistry techniques that allow for greater spectral resolution, sensitivity, and selection of specific atoms. Enhancing approaches for selective labeling in mammalian cells could drive further application of NMR into cell biology.


### Select 13C-labeling of methyl groups and the TROSY effect.
  Biomolecular NMR is greatly benefitted by high field magnets, as increasing the external magnetic field strength increases the signal-to-noise and yields greater spectral dispersion. One disadvantage of high field magnets is an increase in chemical shift anisotropy (CSA), which contributes to signal decay. This effect is most prominent for nuclei with greater magnetic anisotropy, such as carbonyl carbons. Throughout its application to biomedical research, spectroscopists and manufacturers have had to triumph over physical limitations that are innate to NMR. A technical hurdle that can become a roadblock for applying NMR to large complexes is the resonance broadening that occurs as molecular size increases. This broadening occurs from a slowed rotational correlation time, which enhances dipole-dipole (DD) interactions between 1H atoms. DD and CSA effects enhance signal loss by inducing fluctuations in the local magnetic field experienced by the nuclear spins, causing loss of the coherent transverse magnetization that is induced and detected during an NMR experiment [49, 50]. 1H-1H DD relaxation can be reduced by partial or full deuteration, which can also be used to prevent spin diffusion (indirect DD interactions) in NOESY experiments and resolve spectral complexities in large complexes by asymmetric labeling of sample components.

  An ingenious approach to remove the DD and CSA effects was developed with Transverse Relaxation Optimized Spectroscopy (TROSY) experiments [51], which take advantage of intrinsic situations in which they cancel each other out. The TROSY was first developed for amide groups, which often serve as a fingerprint of a protein, as 1H, 15N correlation experiments have the capacity to report on each amino acid except for proline. The innovation of the TROSY was to accept a loss of 50% of the otherwise detected signal with the purpose of only recording the sharpest component, namely the population for which DD and CSA cancel each other [51]. This signal selection involves removal of typically applied decoupling schemes in the 1H and 15N dimensions to average the effects of scalar coupling, yielding one signal instead of four. The TROSY approach can also be applied in this manner to benefit the detection of aromatic 13C-1H correlation signals [52]. Because of their reliance on CSA effects to cancel out DD effects, these experiments have an intrinsic optimal field strength tuned to the CSA of ~1.2 GHz for amide groups [51] and ~800 MHz for aromatic groups [52].

  Amide and aromatic signals are not the most NMR sensitive chemical groups in a protein and amide groups are challenged by signal broadening from chemical exchange with bulk water, which is debilitating at neutral and high pH. The most NMR-sensitive chemical group in proteins are the methyl groups, which contribute three 1H atoms and as an extra bonus, tend to be in structured regions of proteins, yielding dispersion in both the 13C and 1H dimensions due to local interactions. Initially, the methyl groups seemed immune to TROSY-like effects as their CSA is too small – insufficient to cancel DD effects. However, an individual methyl group has multiple relaxation pathways, formed by the two spin states of 13C and eight of 1H, and cancellation of intra-methyl DD interactions can yield a TROSY effect [53]. By carefully considering the individual pathways at each step in an NMR pulse sequence, the longest-lived coherence pathways can be selected to generate spectra with higher sensitivity [53, 54]. The DD effects that benefit a methyl-TROSY experiment are independent of the external magnetic field strength, allowing spectra with greater sensitivity to be recorded by implementing this approach on any spectrometer. An elegant review on the methyl TROSY is available [55].

  In highly deuterated samples, the DD relaxation of methyl groups is dominated by their own 1H-1H and 1H-13C correlations, making these samples ideal for methyl TROSY experiments. Particularly beneficial is labeling select methyl groups with 13C bound to 1H and substituting all other 1H in the molecule with 2H [56], thereby reducing or eliminating external 1H-1H DD interactions. Optimized experiments have been developed for specific methyl isotopomers 13C1H3, 13C1H22H, and 13C1H2H2, that take into account the unique DD interactions associated with each labeling scheme [57]. Such innovative methods to preserve and assign NMR signals to individual atoms has enabled the study of supramolecular complexes, notably the 670 kDa 20S proteasome core particle from the archaebacterium Thermoplasma acidophilum [58].

  A practical application of selective 13C-labeling of methyl groups and TROSY spectroscopy is exemplified in the study of GPCRs [59–63], a superfamily of membrane proteins that are important drug targets due to their integral function in several pathologies. As such, 34% of FDA-approved drugs target GPCRs [64]. The structural flexibility and dynamic interactions associated with GPCRs pose a challenge to their study by X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. The ability of NMR to quantify molecular motions makes it an ideal complementary tool to evaluate the structural details of GPCR function, protein-protein, and protein-drug interactions. For solution NMR experiments, GPCRs have typically been studied in detergent micelles and lipid nanodiscs and the data have yielded drug binding sites and functionally important allosteric motions [60, 63].


## Underutilized application of NMR for cell-based research
  Particularly exciting is the prospect of doing in situ NMR experiments on proteins in living, cultured mammalian cells [41, 65]. Isotopically labeled proteins have been microinjected into frog oocytes [66, 67] or delivered into mammalian cells by pore-forming toxins [68], electroporation [69], or cell-penetrating peptides [70]. The structure of GB1 was solved in human cells [71] and α-synuclein observed to be disordered but more compact in non-neuronal and neuronal cells [69]. Moreover, NMR can be used to screen for small molecule binding to proteins in cells [70]. A new wave of advancements could be achieved by the availability of commercial NMR bioreactors to maintain cells in a living state for studies of proteins of interest under different stress conditions and/or time-dependent reactions, as reviewed recently [72]. For example, an advanced NMR bioreactor has been applied to acquire in-cell NMR data, facilitating the real-time monitoring of protein–ligand interactions and protein oxidation processes for durations of up to 72 hours [73, 74].

  The general incorporation of NMR into cellular assays would be highly beneficial to biological research. NMR experiments can readily evaluate the structural impact of amino acid substitutions, even in cases where aggregation [75] or loss of structure [76] is induced. The induction of such structural states stymies X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. This capability could benefit routine functional assays in cells whereby amino acid substitutions are used to validate sites of interaction. Such mutations are often assumed not to affect protein structure, which can lead to misinterpretation of results. Moreover, the application of NMR in this manner can provide insights into the impact of missense mutations associated with disease.


## NMR and the Protein Data Bank
  NMR spectroscopy can directly probe individual atoms to provide information regarding their structural and chemical context, motions, and the existence of transiently populated states. The uniqueness of the type of information measured by NMR makes it highly complementary to other structural and chemical biology tools. In contrast to X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM, NMR records measurements in solution and can provide information on biomolecules or regions within that undergo chemical exchange and/or are intrinsically disordered or dynamic. NMR is frequently judged harshly for contributing <10 % of the structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). But many structures solved by NMR are of biomolecules too small for cryo-EM and too dynamic for X-ray crystallography.

  Proteasomes are well-studied by cryo-EM, but many regions and interactions remain poorly defined due to technical limitations caused by dynamic properties. For example, hRpn10 has a compact N-terminal von Willebrand factor type A (VWA) domain that is observed by cryo-EM assembled into proteasomes [77] and its ubiquitin interaction motif 1 (UIM1) region is present in a recent ubiquitin-bound structures [78]; but, its ubiquitin interaction motif 2 (UIM2) [79] and RAZUL domain, which is used to bind E3 ligase E6AP [80], have yet to be observed by cryo-EM or crystallized. The structures of these dynamic regions and their various complexes were readily solved by NMR [79–82]. The RAZUL domain is intrinsically disordered but becomes helical to form a 4-helix bundle with E6AP (Figure 2a). This disorder-to-order transition is readily detected by concomitant NMR amide signal dispersion following E6AP addition (Figure 2b) [80] and was exploited to design a fluorescent assay to screen for small molecules inhibitors of the RAZUL:E6AP interaction [83]. Moreover, NMR was able to detect E6AP binding to RAZUL in the 2.5 MDa 26S proteasome [80]. By contrast, E6AP has yet to be observed at proteasomes by cryo-EM even when added in excess [84] due to its binding to the dynamic hRpn10 region [80].

  All proteasome substrate receptors required NMR to solve their structures with ubiquitin chains, most likely because they exchange between the ubiquitin moieties, stymying crystal formation [82, 85, 86]. NMR has also resolved a small domain in myosin VI that has isoform-dependent structural properties to promote differential binding to downstream signaling molecules, including ubiquitin chains and clathrin [87–89]. These examples demonstrate the power of NMR to contribute structural diversity to the PDB and in some cases, protein folds or complexes have not been solved by other methods. Even in cases where both NMR and crystal structures have been solved, the unique ability of NMR to also provide insights into motion has proven invaluable for a complete understanding of function. For example, NMR data probing the backbone amide dynamics of apo hRpn13 revealed that the β1-β2 hairpin and the loop between β6 and β7 exhibit high-frequency motions [90]. These dynamic regions enhance the structural adaptability of hRpn13, allowing it to accommodate diverse binding partners (Figure 2c). Notably, this flexibility facilitates interactions with its proteasome docking site provided by hRpn2 [91], small-molecule ligands, including XL5 [92] and XL44 [90], histone deacetylase HDAC8 [93], and disordered regions of ubiquitinated substrates while binding to their ubiquitin chains [94].


## NMR of systems in motion
  NMR is the leading method to study intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in biomolecules, which has been estimated to comprise greater than 50% of the human proteome [95]. IDPs and IDRs produce lower chemical shift dispersion in NMR spectra, causing signals to cluster together. This phenomenon is especially limiting for backbone amide groups, which historically have played a central role in the assignment of NMR signals to the protein polypeptide backbone [49]. NMR experiments traditionally center on 1H, which has the largest gyromagnetic ratio and in turn Zeeman polarization at the beginning of an experiment (compared to 13C or 15N), causing greatest sensitivity. Prolines lack an amide proton however and are over-represented in IDPs and IDRs. IDPs and/or regions with proline can be assigned by 3D HACAN [96] and CANCO [97] experiments. Particularly helpful for proline assignment is a suite of proline-specialized NMR experiments that do not require an amide proton. Such experiments include CDCA(NCO)CAHA, which correlates the 13Cα and 13Cδ of a proline residue to 1Hα and 13Cα of the preceding residue [98], and CONPro, (H)CBCACONPro, (H)CCCONPro, (H)CBCANCOPro, and (HCA)COCONPro, which were modified from earlier experiments to include band-selective 15N pulses that excite only proline residues [99]. This suite of experiments allows the ready assignment of sequential proline stretches. In cases where a proline neighbors a non-proline amino acid, 3D HNN and HN(C)N experiments, which correlate the amide proton (1H) and nitrogen (15N) of a given residue to the N- and C-terminal (HNN) or solely C-terminal (HN(C)N) 15N neighbor can also be used [100].

  The strong 1H dipolar interactions of large biomolecules cause conventional 1H-detected experiments to have poor signal-to-noise, causing direct detection of 13C, as was used in some of the experiments mentioned above, to also be of benefit to these systems [101–108]. Such experiments require probes that can detect 13C, however. These and all 3D experiments benefit substantially by nonuniform sampling [108, 109], allowing for greater resolution and signal-to-noise. Collectively, these approaches have been a gamechanger for study of IDPs and high molecular weight protein systems.

  IDPs/IDRs play critical roles in disease and therapeutic treatments, making this application of NMR of valuable biomedical importance. Poly-glutamine tracts in huntingtin act as drivers of Huntington’s disease and by using NMR, it was discovered that huntingtin forms an equilibrium of sparsely populated α-helices that are structurally influenced by flanking sequences [110]. NMR was also recently applied to study the intrinsically disordered linkers in chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) used for immunotherapy [111, 112]. These studies discovered local structure in the CD28 hinge used in CAR T-cells; that both CD28 and CD8α hinges undergo dynamic chemical exchange; and that these CAR linkers can dictate inter-cellular distances between CAR T-cells and tumor cells [111, 112].

  NMR is also powerful for investigating time-dependent processes, including the contribution of conformational dynamics and flexibility to enzyme kinetics [113]. It was applied to directly monitor the enzymatic kinetics for deoxycytidine deamination by APOBEC3 proteins [114–117], a mechanism used by human hosts to restrict HIV infection by mutating the viral genome. NMR was also used to study proteolysis of α-synuclein by endogenous proteases in SH-SY5Y cell extracts [118] and to reveal mechanisms of substrate recognition for proteolysis of the sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) [119]. A cell type-specific post-translational modification of hRpn13 was also discovered by using NMR [93]. Recently, NMR experiments aided in the discovery of a new class of molecular chaperones that recognize and prevent initial stages of protein misfolding by sensing transient breakage of hydrogen bonds [120]. Another innovation has been to use high pressure NMR to study transition states during enzymatic catalysis [121] and protein folding [122].


## Use of NMR to study metabolic pathways and drug response
  NMR is a powerful technique for real-time monitoring of metabolic processes in living cells and organelles. Metabolic conversion of 13C-labeled pyruvate, lactate, and alanine has been followed in mouse skeletal myoblast cells and their isolated mitochondria by NMR to provide real-time insights into intracellular pyruvate-lactate-alanine cycling [123, 124]. NMR spectroscopy is also a powerful tool for studying cancer cell metabolism, as it enables the identification and quantification of metabolites within complex biological mixtures based on their distinct chemical structures. Such NMR-based profiling can be applied to diverse sample types, including urine, serum, plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, cells, media, and tissue (Figure 3a). As such, it is one of the main analytic tools for metabolomics. Moreover, NMR can also be used to identify disease biomarkers and monitor treatment response by biofluid analyses [125, 126]. The workflow for NMR-based metabolomics is similar to that of LC-MS and includes study design, sample collection, metabolite extraction, separation of the polar fraction from lipids and proteins, as well as final drying of the sample and resuspension in NMR buffer. Spectra are then recorded and processed on the NMR samples, and the metabolites are identified and quantified as described below. Finally, statistical analyses are performed to identify metabolites that are differentially altered between the groups studied; for example, disease versus healthy or control versus drug-treated (Figure 3a).

  NMR-based metabolic profiling is routinely done by using 1D NOESY pulse sequences with presaturation for water suppression [127, 128]. The NMR spectra are recorded, processed, annotated for the assigned metabolites, and quantified into datasets using a bucketing approach. These datasets can be used in multivariate analysis, clustering the samples according to their metabolic status, induced by the treatment effect or disease state. This application of NMR is complementary to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics. Advanced software tools, such as Chenomix (https://www.chenomx.com/products/), further enhance NMR-based metabolomics by allowing precise spectral fitting and compound identification using curated reference libraries. By using Chenomix, one can obtain accurate concentrations of known metabolites from the NMR spectra. NMR-based metabolomics was able to identify D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2HG) as a novel biomarker of lower grade gliomas that contain a mutation in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1mut), and to monitor the effect of IDH1mut inhibitor (AGI5198) on D-2HG concentration in patient-derived cell lines [129] (Figure 3b).

  Isotope tracing combined with NMR spectroscopy provides a powerful approach for studying metabolic fluxes [130]. This technique enables tracking of the metabolic fate of a specific moiety within a metabolite, or the breakdown of a uniformly labeled substrate across different metabolic pathways. Given the low natural abundance of 13C (~1%), substrates must be isotopically labeled at specific positions to allow sensitive and specific detection. In the context of glioma metabolism, 13C-NMR is particularly useful for probing alternative metabolic routes, as it yields detailed positional isotopomer information. The use of labeled tracers ensures that only products derived from the labeled substrate are detected, reducing background signals and enhancing pathway resolution.

  13C-labeled tracers have been instrumental in elucidating metabolic pathways in glioma and other brain tumor models, particularly in tracing glycolysis and TCA cycle fluxes. Early studies using labeled pyruvate and glucose focused on lactate production, a hallmark of the Warburg effect, to understand glioma metabolism [131]. Techniques advanced to include dual-tracer and deuterium incorporation strategies revealed dynamic exchange between lactate and pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-mediated catalysis [132] as well as the role of alternative substrates, such as glutamine, in fueling the TCA cycle and contributing to NADPH-generating pathways like malic enzyme activity [133]. Furthermore, 13C-NMR enabled detection of glucose- and glutamine-derived carbon incorporation into fatty acids, demonstrating that a substantial portion of lipid synthesis is supported by these nutrients [133]. These approaches underscore the utility of NMR-based tracer studies for mapping tumor metabolic reprogramming.

  In conclusion, NMR metabolomics offers a reproducible and non-destructive approach for profiling tumor biochemistry, providing a direct snapshot of cellular function that is particularly valuable for assessing dynamic processes such as treatment response. Despite its limited sensitivity, which restricts detection to abundant metabolites and requires substantial changes in steady-state concentrations, the technique has proven useful in preclinical and clinical research. Applications include identifying cancer-associated metabolic dysregulation, monitoring therapeutic efficacy, and detecting early toxicity signals in drug development [130]. Notably, ex vivo NMR has facilitated the development of non-invasive MRS biomarkers, such as D-2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) for detecting IDH1 mutations in gliomas and supports integrated analyses by enabling tissue reuse for both spectral and histopathological evaluation [134, 135]. One drawback of the 1D proton NMR is that the number of metabolites that can be identified is still limited compared with LC-MS. However, the power of NMR metabolomics over LC-MS is in the identification of position by 13C tracing experiments in metabolites, and with that, the unambiguous metabolic pathway assignment by which the isotopes are incorporated.


## Synergy of NMR with x-ray crystallography and cryo-EM
  As it emerged as a structural biology technique in the 1980s [136], NMR proved highly complementary to X-ray crystallography by its capacity to study dynamic biomolecules in solution.

  For example, an NMR structure of the intrinsically dynamic transcription factor PUT3 appeared in the same journal issue as the more ordered DNA-bound crystal structure [137, 138], providing comparison of the pre-engaged and post-DNA binding structure. NMR can also be used as a tool to optimize systems for crystallography. For example, in pursuing the structure of the proteasome substrate receptor hRpn13, NMR spectra indicated the protein from S. cerevisiae to be less ordered than the murine and human orthologs, as spectra recorded on scRpn13 showed severe signal broadening of amide signals in a helix due to chemical exchange [139]. As expected scRpn13 was unable to be crystallized whereas mRpn13 [140] and hRpn13 [141] each crystallized. In addition, NMR data can provide insights for engineering protein constructs to promote crystallization, such as by identifying and removing disordered or dynamic segments.

  Since X-ray crystallography requires the trapping of a protein in a crystalline state, it can be incomplete in describing the landscape of conformations that can be adopted by a protein. Even ubiquitin, with its renowned stable structure, was found by NMR to exchange in solution between myriad conformations that are selected by various binding partners [142]. Similarly, NMR has been used to define exchange dynamics of ubiquitin chains [143], which are formed by isopeptide bonds between the C-terminal glycine of one ubiquitin moiety and a lysine or N-terminal end of another ubiquitin [144]. The varying ubiquitin chains have distinct receptors and in turn, cell signaling pathways [145]. X-ray crystallography provided high resolution structures of ubiquitin polymer conformations to reveal certain linkage types promote an extended chain configuration, such as K63-linked ubiquitin chains [146], whereas others can adopt compact structures wherein the ubiquitin moieties are packed against each other [147]. NMR experiments were able to observe K48-linked ubiquitin chains in both the closed and extended form by using the overall rotational diffusion tensor [148]. An additional semi-packed configuration was also observed by NMR in unbound K48-linked ubiquitin chains that is used to bind hRpn13 [85].

  With the invention of direct electron detectors and other innovations, cryo-EM has emerged in the last 1.5 decades as the method of choice to study the structures of large biological systems. Continued innovations in sample preparation aimed at preventing preferred particle orientation, denaturation, or complex dissociation during vitrification [149] as well as advancements in data processing applications [150, 151] have streamlined the determination of high-resolution cryo-EM structures. Cryo-EM is not stymied by the molecular weight hurdles of NMR, nor the need to form crystals, and compared to NMR, it is an easier to learn and more user-friendly technique. A limitation of cryo-EM however is that it is unable to resolve highly dynamics parts of complexes. Among the earliest successes of cryo-EM was the resolution of the 26S proteasome structure in 2012 [152, 153]; however, dynamic regions of the proteasome that could be resolved by NMR remain unobserved by cryo-EM due to intrinsic disorder or dynamics, such as the aforementioned C-terminal region of hRpn10 [80].

  Due to the limitations of cryo-EM with intrinsic disorder and dynamics, NMR has been a powerful tool to understand local structure at high resolution. For biomolecules such as RNA, integrating NMR-derived distance restraints and molecular dynamics provided higher resolution information for the HIV-1 RNA duplex, which was limited to 9 Å by only cryo-EM [154]. The integration of magic-angle spinning (MAS) solid-state and solution NMR with cryo-EM data maps resolved regions not visible in previous crystal or EM structures of TET2, an aminopeptidase from P. horikoshii [155]. Overall, NMR can be significantly complementary in providing information on dynamics that crystallography and cryo-EM cannot resolve.


## NMR and advances in artificial intelligence
  Despite the power of NMR to study time-dependent processes, motions, and the prospect of applying it in native environments, another serious challenge for NMR is attracting the next generation of scientists to the discipline. The application of NMR to large systems requires greater skill to analyze the data. Even for small systems, a working knowledge of chemical biology is required to dissect and interpret NMR data. Challenges to analyzing NMR spectra increase considerably with molecular size due to the increase in signals and greater likelihood of their overlap in spectral position. A strength of NMR is that it can detect multiple states in biological systems undergoing chemical exchange, but this too increases spectral complexity as multiple signals are recorded for each atomic configuration. Compared to NMR, X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM are substantially easier to learn.

  Global excitement for incorporation of artificial intelligence into the modern scientific discovery pipeline was exemplified by the awarding of the 2024 Nobel Prize in chemistry to David Baker, John Jumper, and Demis Hassabis for their pioneering work in protein structure prediction and computational design. A striking feature of AlphaFold [156] is its user-friendly interface, which significantly simplifies the presentation of biological structures and interfaces such structures with other information and databases. Analogous implementation of artificial intelligence can help analyze and/or visualize NMR data. The assignment of NMR signals to atoms in a protein is laborious, especially for large proteins where signal overlap and/or broadening adds challenge. Neural networks have been applied to aid in this process for protein assignment [14, 157–159] and for assignment of metabolites [160]. Isotope pattern recognition can also aid assignment of protein signals by supplying isotopically patterned pyruvate to genetically engineered bacteria during expression [161]. This approach was recently integrated with the application of neural networks and the CcpNnmr software [15] to rapidly assign backbone atoms from a single HNCA spectrum [162]. In addition to aiding in NMR analyses and increasing signal-to-noise ratio [163], these deep neural network (DNN)-based approaches can further be applied to the reconstruction of NMR spectra from non-uniform sampling data [164–166] and virtual decoupling [167, 168].

  More user-friendly visualization, analyses, and validation of NMR data could have a tremendous impact in transitioning it to a method widely incorporated into biological research. Progress has already been made towards this goal. For example, the machine learning-based method ATRINA is reported to assign NMR signals at 91% accuracy and solve structures to 1.44 Å resolution with only raw NMR spectra and the corresponding protein sequence inputted for a 100-protein test sample [157]. This capability is available through the online platform NMRtist [158].

  Structural prediction by artificial intelligence has become impressively reliable [156, 169, 170] and AlphaFold has been used to empower NMR [171], X-ray crystallography [172], cryo-EM [173], and structural analyses by cross-linking mass spectrometry [174, 175]. In all cases, limited experimental data is merged and refined by using predicted structures. In the case of NMR, an initial structure predicted by AlphaFold2-Multimer was refined by using intermolecular NOE data [171]. AlphaFold has struggled to predict structures of intrinsically disordered sequences [176], but IDPConformerGenerator has had greater success [111, 177].

  Invoking artificial intelligence to predict protein-protein interactions based on proteome coevolution has become increasingly reliable [178]. RoseTTAFold2-Lite integrated deep learning to identify novel human host-bacterial pathogen interactions, with a 50% accuracy rate [179]. NMR could validate such interactions and more importantly, serve as a reliable method to report false positives. Knowledge of incorrect predictions is useful for improving such algorithms.


## Use of NMR for molecular targeting
  NMR has long been appreciated for its ability to readily screen for ligand binding [180], making it a strong asset for drug discovery. For example, fragment-based methods using NMR have been employed to identify small molecules targeting a range of proteins, including FK506-binding protein (FKBP) [180], Bcl-2 family members Bcl-xL [181, 182] and Bcl-2 [183, 184], as well as KRAS [185, 186], as reviewed recently [187]. In screening from compound libraries, NMR is highly reliable compared to other methods, especially when using 2D heteronuclear NMR correlation experiments acquired on proteins with chemical shift assignments available. These experiments not only detect binding, but map atoms involved in the interaction and can readily eliminate false positives found by other methods [92]. In addition, 2D 1H, 15N HSQC experiments have been applied in the field of targeted protein degradation to assess whether a proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) can facilitate the formation of a ternary complex involving the protein of interest (POI), the PROTAC molecule, and an E3 ligase; while also providing valuable insights into the binding mode [188].

  Ligand-based NMR screening can also readily detect interactions, including Water-ligand observed via gradient spectroscopy (WaterLOGSY), T2 and T1ρ filter experiments, or saturation transfer difference (STD) experiments [189]. WaterLOGSY has been effective for fragment-based screening [190, 191], determining solvent-exposed protons on bound ligands [192, 193], and measuring ligand binding affinity [194]. STD NMR spectroscopy can further provide key features of weak protein-ligand binding, as observed with differential epitope mapping by STD NMR (DEEP-STD NMR) [195–198].

  Fluorine substituents are common in ligand libraries and can further empower NMR for drug discovery, as elegantly reviewed in [199] and demonstrated with the discovery of inhibitors for the dimeric SARS-CoV-2 protease 3CLp [200]. 19F is NMR active, comparable to 1H in sensitivity, present at 100% natural abundance, has wide chemical shift dispersion (~800 ppm), and can couple to 1H or 13C allowing for 2D correlation experiments [201]. Trifluoromethyl-containing ligands against the KRAS switch II pocket were used as competitive probes to observe interactions in both nucleotides states [202]. These measurements took advantage of the change in transverse relaxation of the probe that occurs following protein binding. Additionally, a 19F NMR competitive assay was developed to determine the positive or negative cooperativity of ternary complexes formed by PROTACs [203]. In addition to ligand-based fluorine monitoring, a target protein can be fluorinated, as was done to screen for ligands against Brd4 by using fluorinated tryptophan [204]. Fluorinated amino acids, including as fluorinated phenylalanine and fluorinated tyrosine, can also be incorporated into mammalians cells for in-cell NMR to observe ligand binding [205] and perform ligand-based competition for HSP90α [206]. A limitation for 19F NMR is accessibility to a probe that can tune to fluorine, but because the frequencies of 1H and 19F are similar, fluorine can be detected on the 1H channel.

  By acquiring intermolecular NOESY data for ligands and their targets, structures can be reliably solved by NMR without the need for crystals (Figure 2c, PDB 7KXI) [92] or to provide information not present in X-ray diffraction data (Figure 2c, PDB 8FTQ) [90]. To solve the structure of XL44 bound to hRpn13, NMR was integrated with X-ray crystallography (Figure 2c, PDB 8FTQ) [90]. A crystal of the complex was obtained that diffracted to 2.1 Å resolution but methyl groups at the binding interface were not observable in the diffraction map. Interactions involving these methyl groups were detected by NMR with intermolecular NOE interactions. Moreover, only one stereoisomer of the hRpn13-bound ligand was observed by X-ray diffraction whereas NOESY data indicated the presence of a second stereoisomer. This example illustrates the complementarity of NMR and crystallography in drug discovery.

  All structural methods have limitations. Often crystal structures are used without consideration of the diffraction data and/or knowledge of the resolution at and around a ligand binding site. Overall, motions are generally underrepresented in ligand:biomolecular complexes. NMR can provide such information, but the resulting complexity causes the broader scientific community to prefer crystal structures. Efforts to train artificial intelligence algorithms for molecular targeting however are likely to improve by inclusion of dynamics at the interaction site. Expanding this concept further, allosteric influences on binding site structure and motion could also be used to better design molecular targeting agents.


## Perspective
  In this review, we summarize the unique strengths and limitations of NMR for studies of large biomolecular systems, systems in motion, and metabolic processes. Although there has been advanced progress in the preparation of NMR samples and the application of NMR within the cellular environment, key challenges persist in studying large biomolecular systems by NMR due to inherent size limitations, spectra complexity, and low signal sensitivity. To overcome these challenges, integration of NMR with complementary techniques, such as cryo-EM, X-ray crystallography, mass spectrometry, high-throughput screening, biophysical assays, cellular assays and artificial intelligence-driven modeling, will offer powerful avenues to construct a more comprehensive picture of biomolecular architecture. The application of NMR extends beyond fundamental biology, as it can be used as a major tool for future innovations in drug discovery and to follow patient response to therapeutic treatments.
