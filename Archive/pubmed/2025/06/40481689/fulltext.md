# Bioinformatics‐Based Comparative Analysis of the Human Retina Proteome


## Introduction
  The human retina relies on a complex network of proteins, many of which exhibit intrinsic disorder and liquid‐liquid phase separation (LLPS), enabling dynamic interactions for retinal function. Disruptions in these properties, along with missense mutations, have been linked to retinal diseases. This study aims to characterize and compare retinal proteins categorized by their expression specificity and tissue distribution using bioinformatics tools to explore relationships between intrinsic protein disorder, phase separation potential, and mutation pathogenicity.


## Methods
  We analyzed retinal proteins classified by the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) into two major groups based on gene expression specificity (degree of unique retinal expression) and gene expression distribution (extent of expression across tissues). We analyzed nine retinal proteomes categorized by gene expression specificity and distribution. Intrinsic protein disorder was assessed using per‐residue and global disorder predictors from the Rapid Intrinsic Disorder Analysis Online (RIDAO) platform, LLPS potential was evaluated with ParSe v2, and missense mutation pathogenicity was predicted using AlphaMissense.


## Results
  Significant differences in per‐residue intrinsic protein disorder were found within the specificity and distribution subgroups (p < 0.0001). In addition, global disorder predictions from the RIDAO platform demonstrated non‐random distributions of protein species across the proteomes analyzed in both subgroups (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, proteins specifically elevated in the retina exhibited higher intrinsic disorder and greater phase separation propensity (ParSe v2, AUC up to 0.650), compared to those more broadly expressed. Lastly, AlphaMissense analysis showed significant variations in the average pathogenicity scores of missense mutations within subgroups (p < 0.0001).


## Conclusion
  Our results show that intrinsic disorder, LLPS, and mutational tendencies are not evenly distributed among retinal proteomes. Our study demonstrates a link between intrinsic disorder, LLPS potential, and pathogenic vulnerability among retinal proteins, underscoring the unique structural and functional landscape of retinal proteomes. Proteins with higher specificity to the retina exhibit greater disorder and phase separation potential, highlighting their potential role in dynamic cellular processes that support retinal function. Conversely, proteins widely distributed across multiple tissues tend to be more ordered, suggesting a need for structural stability in their broader functional roles.


## Introduction
  The human retina is a delicate structure located in the posterior segment of the eye. The retina is 0.5 mm thick and consists of photoreceptors and glial cells, which capture and convert light into neural signals that are sent to the brain, enabling vision [1, 2]. The retina consists of multiple layers including the nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell layer, inner plexiform layer, outer plexiform layer, outer nuclear layer, and the photoreceptor layer [3]. Each layer is made of a diverse set of cells that includes photoreceptor, bipolar, amacrine, horizontal, ganglion, and Muller cells [3]. Beyond its cellular components, many subcellular processes drive the behavior of these highly specialized cells.

  Retinal function is largely influenced by the structural nature of its proteins. Some proteins adopt well‐defined three‐dimensional structures, while others lack a fixed conformation and exist as dynamic ensembles. These dynamic proteins, known as intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDPRs), play fundamental roles in cellular signaling, molecular interactions, and adaptability [4, 5, 6, 7]. Unlike structured proteins, IDPs do not adopt a single stable shape, allowing them to engage in multiple biological processes [5, 6, 7]. Another unique feature of many IDPs and IDPRs is their capability to undergo liquid‐liquid phase separation (LLPS), a driving force for the formation of membrane‐less organelles [8, 9].

  LLPS controls many biochemical processes within the body, and its malfunction has been associated with disease states [10, 11, 12, 13]. In the retina, proteins undergoing phase separation may contribute to important biological functions, and dysregulation of LLPS contributes to retinal disease pathology [14, 15]. Genetic variations, especially missense mutations, can further disrupt protein function by altering structural stability, flexibility, or protein‐protein interaction [16] networks. For instance, missense mutations can destabilize protein folding by several kcal/mol, interfere with critical binding interfaces, or delocalize proteins from their proper cellular compartments, ultimately leading to disease‐associated phenotypes in retinal cells [17, 18, 19]. Understanding the relationship between protein disorder, LLPS, and retinal function is critical for uncovering molecular mechanisms that underlie both normal vision and disease. By studying the propensities of various retinal proteins towards these behaviors, we may gain insight into the molecular groundwork behind retinal function and pathology. This may open avenues for developing clinical approaches for diagnosis, prevention, and targeted therapies for retinal diseases.

  One significant effort to define the proteome of all human cells, tissues, and organs is the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), a Swedish‐based open‐access repository started in 2003 that has already contributed to several thousands of publications in human biology and disease [20]. Using both protein and mRNA levels, the HPA has defined gene expression profiles of over 15,000 genes in over 40 different normal human tissues, including the human retina [20, 21, 22]. The HPA defines two different groups within the retina transcriptome based on either the specificity or distribution of the genes.

  The HPA classifies gene expression patterns across 36 human tissues, allowing for systematic comparison of retinal gene expression relative to other organ systems [23]. In our study, this comparison is framed through two classification axes: specificity and distribution. Specificity refers to how unique the expression of certain genes is to the retina compared to other tissues and identifies genes with elevated expression specifically in the retina. Elevated expression is classified into three subcategories: Tissue enriched, where the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) level in the retina is at least four times higher than in any other tissues; Group enriched, where the average mRNA level in a group of 2–5 tissues is at least four times higher compared to any other tissue; and Tissue enhanced, where the mRNA level in the retina is at least four times higher than the average level in all other tissues. Distribution refers to the presence of genes across different tissues and identifies genes detected at a minimum expression threshold exclusively in the retina. Distribution visualizes how many genes have or do not have detectable levels (transcripts per million (TPM) ≥ 1) of transcribed mRNA molecules in the retina compared to other tissues. All genes elevated in the retina are categorized as follows: Detected in single, where they are detected in a single tissue; Detected in some, where they are detected in more than one but less than one‐third of tissues; Detected in many, where they are detected in at least a third but not all tissues; and detected in all, where they are detected in all tissues.

  The HPA divides the specificity group into five different subgroups and the distribution group into six different subgroups based on how specific the expression or presence of a gene is to the retina and other tissues in the body. For example, one subgroup within the specificity group contains genes only found in the retina, while another subgroup contains genes found everywhere within the body. These classifications provide a biologically meaningful framework to study how proteomic properties may vary with tissue specificity.

  We aim to investigate the relationship between gene expression, as grouped by specificity and distribution, in the retina and the propensity for intrinsic protein disorder, LLPS, and the pathogenicity of missense mutations. To accomplish this, we use a three‐pronged approach to compare the characteristics of subgroup proteomes within the specificity and distribution groupings. We hypothesize that gene expression patterns influence intrinsic protein disorder, LLPS propensity, and the pathogenicity of missense mutations across specificity and distribution subgroups. To formally assess this, we test the null hypothesis that no differences exist among the subgroups for these molecular characteristics.

  First, we utilized previously established methodology to compare the levels of intrinsic disorder among both the specificity or distribution transcriptome subgroups of the retina using two per‐residue disorder scoring systems: (1) Average Disorder Scores (ADS), which quantify the mean disorder across a protein sequence, and (2) Percentages of Predicted Disordered Residues (PPDR), which measure the proportion of amino acids predicted to be disordered [24, 25, 26]. To further classify protein disorder, we utilized a combination of two binary disorder predictors to create charge‐hydropathy (CH) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots (CH‐CDF analysis), which evaluate disorder based on net charge and hydrophobicity, distinguishing structured proteins from intrinsically disordered ones [27]. Next, using AlphaMissense, a tool created by Google's DeepMind that evaluates missense mutations within a protein and determines their potential functional impact, we evaluated missense mutations within each subgroup proteome and predicted the likelihood that mutations are pathogenic [28, 29]. Finally, we evaluated each of the subgroup proteomes for their corresponding likelihood of exhibiting protein phase separation. This was done using ParSe v2 (Partition Sequence), an online tool that analyzes the primary sequence of an inputted protein sequence to determine which regions are both intrinsically disordered and likely to exhibit protein phase separation behavior [30].


## Methods
  In this study, we examine differences in intrinsic disorder, pathogenicity of missense mutations, and likelihood to undergo protein phase separation among the specificity and distribution subgroup proteomes identified by the HPA. The rationale behind this study comes from our aim to understand both the molecular basis and protein dynamics of both retinal physiology and disease processes. We used data from a comprehensive proteomic database and a suite of bioinformatics tools to identify significant differences in our selected metrics for both. By integrating large‐scale data analysis with robust statistical methods, we hope our study provides valuable insights into the molecular behaviors that may contribute to retinal pathology.


### Identification of Proteomes
  We sourced our sets of proteins from the HPA entry on the retina (available at https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/tissue/retina; accessed on 4/23/2024). Within the HPA retina entry, the transcriptome is reported in two unique ways: by specificity and distribution. For the specificity analysis, genes were grouped based on their mRNA expression levels in the retina compared to other tissues. Categories included genes with at least four‐fold higher expression, specifically in the retina, genes with at least four‐fold higher average expression across a group of 2–5 tissues including the retina, and genes elevated in the retina compared to the average level in all other tissues. Additional categories identified genes expressed in the retina but with low tissue specificity and those not detected in any tissue. In the distribution analysis, the detection levels of mRNA molecules were categorized based on their presence across tissues. Genes were classified as detected only in the retina, detected in the retina and some other tissues, detected in many but less than one‐third of tissues, detected in many including the retina, and detected in all tissues. This classification helps in understanding the breadth of gene expression across different biological samples. In this study, mRNA expression levels were used as a surrogate for protein expression to define the input proteomes, which were then analyzed for structural and functional characteristics. Each proteome was considered its separate independent subgroup, and the individual proteins were considered an independent observation within each subgroup. After the gene names of each proteome were acquired from the HPA, the various proteomes were uploaded to the Universal Protein Resource (available at: https://www.uniprot.org/; accessed on 4/23/2024) and mapped to their entries using the Retrieve/ID mapping tool [31, 32]. Once the mapping was complete, the FASTA format files for the respective proteins were downloaded directly from UniProt. These FASTA files were the primary input for subsequent predictions and downstream analysis.


### Analysis of Intrinsic Protein Disorder in the Specificity and Distribution Subgroups

#### Prediction of Protein Disorder Using Commonly Used Predictors
  To quantify the degree of intrinsic disorder at the amino acid residue level within various proteomes that were subdivided based on either specificity or distribution, we utilized four Predictor of Natural Disordered Regions (PONDR): PONDR VSL2, PONDR VL3, PONDR VLXT, and PONDR FIT, in addition to IUPred Short and Long. These disorder predictors were accessed through the Rapid Intrinsic Disorder Analysis Online (RIDAO) platform (available at https://RIDAO.app, accessed on 4/24/24) [26]. Using the outputs from these predictors, we calculated ADS and PPDR for each protein. The ADS represented the average disorder for a protein, while PPDR measured the proportion of amino acids within a protein that have a predicted disorder above 0.5.

  Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the statistical significance of the difference in ADS and PPDR between the retinal proteomes analyzed that were part of either the specificity or the distribution grouping. Our statistical test of choice allowed us to determine if variations of intrinsic protein disorder are present between the intra‐specificity or intra‐distribution grouping of proteomes analyzed in this study.


#### Average Disorder Scores (ADS) Versus Percent of Predicted Disorder Residues (PPDR) Analysis
  In the next phase of our per‐residue analysis, we applied a technique that utilized the ADS and PPDR predictions measured by PONDR VSL2B from the RIDAO output [26, 33]. PONDR VSL2B is designed to evaluate protein disorder for each individual amino acid, and its efficacy has been validated in the Critical Assessment of Protein Intrinsic Disorder [34]. Using PONDR VSL2B outputs as the primary data, specific cut‐off points were defined to categorize each protein by its disorder status. Proteins were classified as highly ordered (PPDR < 10% and ADS < 0.15), moderately ordered ((10% ≤ PPDR < 30% and ADS < 0.5) or (PPDR < 10% and 0.15 ≤ ADS < 0.5)), moderately disordered (10% ≤ PPDR < 30% and 0.15 ≤ ADS < 0.5), and highly disordered (PPDR ≥ 30% and ADS ≥ 0.5). Highly ordered proteins are characterized by both PPDR and ADS being in the lowest ranges, which signifies strong structural stability with a minimal propensity for intrinsic protein disorder. Moderately ordered proteins straddle the line, with either PPDR or ADS falling into slightly higher ranges while not exceeding both thresholds concurrently, suggesting these proteins maintain a balance between structure and flexibility. Moderately disordered proteins display intermediate levels of both PPDR and ADS, indicative of greater structural flexibility and a notable potential for disorder, yet they do not reach the highest level of disorder observed in other categories. Highly disordered proteins are defined by high values in both PPDR and ADS, marking them as distinctly lacking structured elements and being predominantly disordered. This classification system attempts to measure all possible structural characteristics across a spectrum from highly ordered to highly disordered proteins, reflecting variations in their biological functions and stability. These thresholds align with those outlined in our prior studies, and these cut‐off values are consistent with the standards set in our previous publications [35, 36, 37]. One important note is that ADS and PPDR are not directly correlated. ADS provides a prediction of the overall disorder for each protein, while PPDR indicates the proportion of amino acids within a protein with a disorder score of 0.5 or more. PPDR scores surpassing 0.5 indicate an overall likelihood of a protein to be disordered. For detailed definitions of additional RIDAO output terms, including PER, RNK, dCDF, and dCH, please refer to the Supporting Information.

  Using these accepted thresholds, we graphically classify proteins based on the specific quadrant they occupy, which helps to determine their protein classification and their general tendency towards disorder. This approach allows for a refined analysis of protein populations of various levels of order and disorder. To statistically test the distribution of protein classes for each proteome in our study, we conducted a Chi‐squared (χ 2) analysis with the null hypothesis that there is no association between protein groups and their classifications. The χ 2 allowed us to examine whether the distribution of protein categories—highly ordered, moderately ordered, moderately disordered, and highly disordered—differs significantly from expected frequencies. The degrees of freedom (DOF) for the χ 2 test was calculated using the formula DOF = (r–1) × (c–1), where r represents the number of classification categories (4), and c represents the number of protein groups (five for the specificity subgrouping and six for the distribution subgrouping). The DOF is 12 for the specificity subgrouping and 15 for the distribution subgrouping. By determining the χ 2 statistic and corresponding p value, we can assess the likelihood that any observed differences in protein distribution across groups occurred by chance.


### CH‐CDF Plot Analysis
  To deepen our analysis of both the specificity and distribution proteomes, we employed two binary predictors of disorder: the CH and the CDF. These metrics evaluate the intrinsic disorder across the entire protein, instead of assessing disorder at the level of individual amino acid residues as done in previous steps with RIDAO outputs. Using these CH and CDF outputs, an intrinsic disorder of proteins was categorized in a binary manner (i.e., yes or no; disordered or ordered; unstructured or structured). The CH considers the net charge and hydrophobicity of each protein to determine its level of disorder. Compared to ordered proteins, disordered proteins typically exhibit lower hydropathy and higher net charge [27]. The CDF represents the cumulative frequency of disordered residues throughout the length of a given protein. In this study, we integrated both CH and CDF metrics to construct a CH‐CDF plot, where each of the nine distinct proteomes across the specificity and distribution groups was plotted.

  With this method, we could differentiate between structured and unstructured proteins based on their positions on the CH‐CDF plot. The Cartesian coordinate system of the CH‐CDF plot facilitates a qualitative classification and detailed analysis of each protein's structural characteristics within a two‐dimensional framework. Quadrant 1 (Q1, bottom right) encompasses proteins likely to be structured, characterized by a negative CH score and a positive CDF score, indicating order. Quadrant 2 (Q2, bottom left) includes proteins that are either molten globular or hybrid, with negative scores for both CH and CDF. Proteins in Quadrant 2 are likely compact without a distinct 3D structure or containing noticeable levels of ordered and disordered residues. Quadrant 3 (Q3, top left) consists of highly disordered proteins with positive CH scores and negative CDF values. Finally, Quadrant 4 (Q4, top right) represents proteins predicted to be disordered based on the CH plot but ordered based on the CDF plot [27, 38].

  To statistically assess the distribution of these classified proteins within each of the five specificity subgroup proteomes and the six distribution subgroup proteomes, we conducted χ 2 tests. The χ 2 test was utilized to determine whether the distribution of protein categories across the quadrants differed significantly from what would be expected under the null hypothesis of no association between protein groups and their classifications. For the specificity subgrouping, involving five protein subgroups, and the distribution subgrouping, which included six groups, the DOF was calculated using the formula DOF = (r–1) × (c–1). Where 𝑟 represents the four quadrants (highly ordered, moderately ordered, moderately disordered, highly disordered) defined by the CH‐CDF analysis, and 𝑐 represents the number of protein groups in each subgrouping (five for specificity subgrouping and six for distribution subgrouping). The DOF was 12 for the specificity subgrouping and 15 for the distribution subgrouping. The rationale for conducting the χ 2 tests was similar to our prior analysis in Section 2.2.


### Assessing Pathogenicity of Missense Mutations Within Each Proteome
  AlphaMissense, a tool developed by Google's DeepMind, predicts how amino acid substitutions affect protein stability in the human proteome. These predictions are based on several datasets aligned to different versions of the human genome and transcript annotations, as per the GENCODE releases V27 for hg19 and V32 for hg38. The database includes predictions for both canonical and non‐canonical transcript isoforms, ensuring a broad coverage of the human transcriptome. For our analysis, the primary dataset utilized was the AlphaMissense Amino Acid Substitutions dataset, which consists of predictions for all feasible single amino acid substitutions across approximately 20,000 UniProt canonical isoforms, representing over 216 million protein variants. This dataset does not contain genomic coordinates, focusing solely on the protein‐centric implications of amino acid changes. The program leverages its database of 216 million missense variant predictions to determine whether a given amino acid substitution is likely to be either pathogenic or benign [29]. AlphaMissense assigns each mutation a pathogenicity score from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating likely pathogenicity and 0 indicating a likely benign mutation [29]. Scores ranging from 0.56 to 1.00 are classified as pathogenic, indicating a higher likelihood of disease association; scores from 0.34 to 0.56 are deemed ambiguous, reflecting a less definitive predictive value; and scores from 0.04 to 0.34 are considered benign, suggesting a lower probability of disease relevance [29]. This classification helps in assessing the potential impact of genetic variants on protein function and disease outcomes. Utilizing these thresholds, we classified all potential amino acid changes within the proteomes that fell within each of the specificity and distribution transcriptomes subgroups as pathogenic, ambiguous, or benign. This allowed us to determine if there were differences in the frequency of pathogenic mutations between the specificity and distribution transcriptomes.

  We calculated the average pathogenicity score for each subgroup proteome within both the specificity and distribution groups to provide an overview of the susceptibility of each proteome to pathogenic missense mutations. The proportion of each subgroup proteome that was benign, pathogenic, or ambiguous was also calculated to shed light on the distribution of missense pathogenicity within the specificity and distribution groups. Once again, we utilized the χ 2 test to analyze the distribution of variant classifications across our two subgroups: specificity and distribution. The DOF for the χ 2 statistic was calculated using the formula (𝑟–1) × (𝑐–1), where 𝑟 denotes the number of groups analyzed and 𝑐 denotes the number of classification categories (3; benign, ambiguous, pathogenic). For the specificity subgrouping, with five groups, there were eight DOF. For distribution subgrouping, which involved six groups, the DOF was 10. This approach allows for a detailed assessment of the variability in variant classifications across the different groups, facilitating a deeper understanding of the distribution patterns within the genetic data analyzed. ANOVA analysis was then performed to determine if there were significant differences in the average pathogenicity scores for each proteome analyzed in the two subgroups. Additionally, we utilized Tukey's HSD as a post‐hoc analysis to evaluate the significance in pairwise differences between subgroup proteomes within the two groupings for our proteomes (i.e., specificity and distribution).


### Evaluating Likelihood to Undergo Liquid‐Liquid Phase Separation (LLPS) Within Each Proteome
  In order to analyze the likelihood of proteins in both the specificity and distribution groups to undergo liquid‐liquid phase separation (LLPS, also called protein phase separation), we utilized an online tool called ParSe v2 (Partition Sequence version 2; available at https://stevewhitten.github.io/Parse_v2_FASTA/; accessed on 4/26/2024), which identifies phase separating intrinsically disordered regions (PS IDRs) within a given sequence [30]. This predictor relies on the established correlation between the hydrodynamic size of proteins and their likelihood to drive phase separation, utilizing the human proteome as a reference [39, 40, 41]. The ParSe algorithm is capable of scanning tens of thousands of sequences from a single computer with equal or better accuracy than other published phase separation predictors [42].

  The five subgroups within the specificity group and the six subgroups within the distribution group, as described above, were each inputted individually into the ParSe v2 algorithm in FASTA format. The ParSe v2 outputs were used to generate graphs for each of the specificity and distribution subgroup proteomes, as well as a corresponding AUC value. An AUC > 0.5 indicated a greater propensity of a proteome to undergo protein phase separation when compared to the human reference proteome [23].


## Results

### Identification of Proteomes
  For the specificity group, five subgroups of genes were available for analysis on the HPA. A total of 785 genes were elevated in the retina (EIR), 4601 genes were elevated in other tissues but expressed in the retina (EIOTBEIR), 7734 genes had low tissue specificity but were expressed in retina (LTSBEIR), 6004 genes were not detected in the retina (NDIR), and 1038 genes were not detected in any tissue (NDIAT, Figure 1A). See methods Section 2.1 for details on how proteomes were identified.

  For the distribution group, there were six subgroups available for the analysis. Fifty‐seven genes were only detected in the retina (ODIR), 903 genes were detected in some tissues including the retina (DISIR), 3261 genes were detected in many tissues including the retina (DIMIR), 8899 genes were detected in all (DIA), 6004 genes were not detected in the retina (NDIR), and 1038 genes were not detected in any tissue (NDIAT, Figure 1B). This gave us 11 total subgroups. Although NDIR and NDIAT in both the specificity and distribution groups were composed of identical genes, we included both transcriptomes within both the specificity and distribution analyses. Therefore, out of the 11 total subgroups, only nine unique subgroup transcriptomes were analyzed in this study.

  From the original gene data from the HPA in the specificity group, we identified 725 proteins in the EIR proteome, 4662 proteins in the EIOTBEIR proteome, 7800 proteins in the LTSBEIR proteome, 5767 proteins in the NDIR proteome, and 868 proteins in the NDIAT proteome.

  From the HPA data for the distribution group, we identified 46 proteins in the ODIR proteome, 861 proteins in the DISIR proteome, 3246 in the DIMIR proteome, 9020 proteins in the DIA proteome, and once again, 5767 proteins in the NDIR proteome, and 868 proteins in the NDIAT proteome. These proteomes, as well as this nomenclature and associated abbreviations, were used for all subsequent analysis.


### Analysis of Intrinsic Disorder in the Specificity and Distribution Subgroups

#### Prediction of Protein Disorder Using Commonly Used Predictors
  We then measured and contrasted the disorder tendencies at the level of amino acid residues within the subgroup proteomes of both the specificity and distribution groups. The specificity subgroup proteomes analyzed for the intrinsic disorder at the residue level were EIR, EIOTBEIR, LTSBEIR, NDIR, and NDIAT, while the distribution subgroup proteomes analyzed were ODIR, DISIR, DIMIR, DIA, NDIR, and NDIAT. The proteins in each population were considered one‐to‐one for this portion of our analysis; see methods Section 2.2.1 for details on the protein disorder measurements and metrics used below.

  Using the RIDAO tool, we acquired measures of intrinsic protein disorder for the specificity subgroups using two separate scoring metrics, PPDR and ADS (Table 1). When the PONDR VSL2B predictor was used to analyze the specificity subgroups, the PPDR for EIR, EIOTBEIR, LTSBEIR, NDIR, and NDIAT fluctuated between 30.36% and 44.95%. Simultaneously, the ADS spanned from 0.361 to 0.491. The ANOVA test produced F‐statistics of 66.32 and 109.09 with p values < 0.0001 for both the PPDR and ADS metrics from PONDR VSL2B, respectively, signifying a statistically significant variation among the specificity subgroups. ANOVA performed on the output of the five other disorder predictors (PONDR VL3, PONDR VLXT, PONDR FIT, IUPred Short, and IUPred Long) showed statistically significant variations among subgroups as well. ANOVA with PONDR VL3 showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 67.87 for PPDR and 60.98 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. PONDR VLXT showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 107.81 for PPDR and 144.98 for ADS, with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. PONDR FIT showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 21.39 for PPDR and 42.77 for ADS, with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. IUPred Short showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 44.45 for PPDR and 173.29 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. IUPred Long showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 54.34 for PPDR and 169.78 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics (Table S1).

  A similar analysis was performed for the distribution subgroups. When the PONDR VSL2B predictor was used, PPDR for ODIR, DISIR, DIMIR, DIA, and NDIR/NDIAT (both of which are also found in the specificity subgroups) fluctuated between 30.36% and 44.79%. The ADS scores ranged from 0.361 to 0.487. The ANOVA test on the PONDR VSL2B outputs had F‐statistics of 48.18 and 82.35 with p values < 0.0001 for PPDR and ADS, respectively. This also denotes a statistically significant variation in disorder among the distribution subgroups. ANOVA with PONDR VL3 showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 45.94 for PPDR and 40.51 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. PONDR VLXT showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 82.99 for PPDR and 112.74 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. PONDR FIT showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 14.07 for PPDR and 30.48 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. IUPred Short showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 31.77 for PPDR and 133.68 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics. IUPred Long showed statistically significant F‐statistics of 39.04 for PPDR and 131.55 for ADS with p values < 0.0001 for both metrics (Table S2).

  The ANOVA tests were followed by Tukey's HSD analysis to examine the specific differences in disorder found between each pair of subgroup proteomes within both the specificity and distribution groups using the PONDR VSL2B output (Tables S3 and S4). The comparisons between pairs in the specificity group showed that 7 of the 10 predictors suggested rejecting the null hypothesis, suggesting that the differences between pairs are significant. Similarly, the comparisons between pairs in the distribution group showed that 6 of the 10 predictors suggest rejecting the null hypothesis, also indicating significant differences between specific groups analyzed in the specificity and distribution group. This implies that the significant variances detected by our ANOVA analysis can be explained by significant pairwise differences between subgroup proteomes.


#### Average Disorder Scores (ADS) Versus Percent of Predicted Disorder Residues (PPDR) Analysis
  Continuing with our focus on the PONDR VSL2B PPDR and ADS outputs, we looked at the distribution of disordered proteins in both the specificity and distribution proteomes as highly ordered (PPDR < 10% and ADS < 0.15), moderately ordered ((10% ≤ PPDR < 30% and ADS < 0.5) or (PPDR < 10% and 0.15 ≤ ADS < 0.5)), moderately disordered (10% ≤ PPDR < 30% and 0.15 ≤ ADS < 0.5), and highly disordered (PPDR ≥ 30% and ADS ≥ 0.5). See methods Section 2.3.1 for details on the protein disorder measurements and metrics used below. For the specificity group (Figure 2A), LTSBEIR had the greatest proportion of highly disordered proteins, with 42.8% (3328) of the proteome. Of EIR, 37.0% (267) of the proteome was highly disordered. Of EIOBEIR, 36.9% (1717) of the proteome was highly disordered. NDIR and NDIAT had 38.5% (2211) and 23.4% (202) of their proteomes classified as highly disordered, respectively.

  In the distribution group (Figure 3A), DIMIR had the greatest proportion of highly disordered proteins with 41.2% (1335). Of the DIA proteome, 40.8% (3667) of proteins were highly disordered. Of the DISIR proteome, 34.7% (298) of proteins were considered highly disordered. Of the ODIR proteome, only 21.7% (10) of proteins were considered highly disordered. Identical to the specificity group, NDIR and NDIAT had 38.5% (2211) and 23.4% (202) of their proteomes classified as highly disordered, respectively. Additionally, ODIR and NDIAT had the greatest proportion of highly ordered proteins with 2.2% (1) and 6.0% (52) of proteins, respectively.

  Statistical analysis on the quadrant distributions using χ 2 analysis revealed significant variations in the categorization of proteins across subgroup proteomes within both the specificity (χ 2 value of 389.43, p value < 0.0001) and distribution proteomes (χ 2 value of 2106.38, p value < 0.0001). The statistically significant outcome in χ 2 analysis for both the specificity and distribution groups denotes a non‐random distribution of protein disorder among the subgroup proteomes within the two groups.

DIMIR had 1860 proteins (57.4%) in Q1, 889 proteins (27.48%) in Q2, 345 proteins (10.68%) in Q3, and 146 proteins (4.5%) in Q4. DIA had 5127 proteins (57.0%) in Q1, 2282 proteins (25.48%) in Q2, 1288 proteins (14.3%) in Q3, and 300 proteins (3.3%) in Q4.

  To evaluate the distribution of proteins across different quadrants in the CH‐CDF plot, a χ 2 analysis was performed. For the specificity group proteins, there was a significant variance from the expected distribution across the four quadrants (χ 2 value of 389.43, p value < 0.0001). There was similar significance in the analysis done on the distribution group proteomes (χ 2 value of 286.59, p value < 0.0001, DOF of 15). The significant differences indicated by this χ 2 analysis show that the distribution across the four quadrants in both the specificity and distribution proteomes is not due to random variation. This suggests that the differences in structural disorder within each group are distinctly different than what would occur by chance.


### Assessing Pathogenicity of Missense Mutations Within Each Proteome
  AlphaMissense was used to evaluate the pathogenicity of every potential missense mutation within all nine subgroup proteomes. See methods Section 2.4 for details on AlphaMissense and the tool's role in the study. The average pathogenicity of each subgroup proteome was reported as a decimal ranging from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating likely pathogenicity and 0 indicating a likely benign mutation (Table 1) [29]. In the specificity group, EIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.540 (ambiguous), with 41% of its proteome labeled as benign, 45% labeled as pathogenic, and 14% as ambiguous. EIOTBEIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.539 (ambiguous), with 40% of its proteome labeled as benign, 46% labeled as pathogenic, and 14% as ambiguous (Figure 4A). LTSBEIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.562 (pathogenic), with 39% of its proteome labeled as benign, 48% labeled as pathogenic, and 13% labeled as ambiguous. NDIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.460 (ambiguous), with 48% considered benign, 36% considered pathogenic, and 16% considered ambiguous. NDIAT had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.340 (benign), with 53% considered benign, 27% considered pathogenic, and 19% considered ambiguous.

  In the distribution group, ODIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.496 (ambiguous), with 48% of its proteome considered benign, 37% considered pathogenic, and 15% considered ambiguous (Figure 4B). DISIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.519 (ambiguous), with 41% of its proteome considered benign, 45% considered pathogenic, and 14% considered ambiguous. DIMIR had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.520 (ambiguous), with 42% of its proteome considered benign, 44% considered pathogenic, and 14% considered ambiguous. DIA had an overall pathogenicity score of 0.568 (pathogenic), with 38% of its proteome considered benign, 49% considered pathogenic, and 13% considered ambiguous. Pathogenicity scores for NDIR and NDIAT were identical to the specificity group.

  ANOVA analysis found statistically significant differences among average pathogenicity scores within the specificity (F‐statistic of 1056.40, p value < 0.0001, Figure 4A) and distribution (F‐statistic of 942.65, p value < 0.0001, Figure 4B) subgroup proteomes. Tukey's HSD was then performed to examine the significance of differences between pairs of subgroup proteomes within the specificity and distribution groups. For the specificity group, 9 out of 10 predictors strongly suggested rejecting null hypotheses, indicating significant differences between pairs of subgroup proteomes. Likewise, comparisons between pairs in the distribution group showed that 11 of the 15 predictors strongly suggested rejecting the null hypothesis, also indicating significant differences between pairs of subgroup proteomes.

  Statistical analysis on the AlphaMissense data using χ 2 analysis revealed significant variations in the average pathogenicity scores across subgroup proteomes within both the specificity (χ 2 value of 2575987.01, p value < 0.0001) and distribution proteomes (χ 2 value of 2849224.93, p value < 0.0001). The statistically significant outcome in χ 2 analysis for both the specificity and distribution groups denotes a non‐random distribution of average pathogenicity scores among the subgroup proteomes within the two groups.


### Evaluating Likelihood to Undergo Protein Phase Separation Within Each Proteome
  ParSe v2 was utilized to identify phase separating intrinsically disordered regions (PS IDRs) in each of the five subgroups within the specificity group and the six subgroups within the distribution group to undergo protein phase separation and their corresponding likelihood to undergo protein phase separation. See methods Section 2.5 for details on ParSe v2 and the metrics used to assess each proteomes propensity of LLPS. PS IDRs were first characterized by the length and percent of each proteome that contains PS IDRs of at least a given length. These results were graphed for the five specificity (Figure 5A) and six distribution (Figure 5B) proteomes, along with the reference human proteome as described by Ibrahim et al. [30, 42].

  Within the specificity group, EIR was found to have the greatest portion of its proteome containing PS IDRs ≥ 50 residues in length, while NDIAT was found to have the smallest portion of its proteome containing PS IDRs ≥ 50 residues in length. In the distribution group, DIMIR was found to have the greatest portion of its proteome containing PS IDRs ≥ 50 residues in length, while NDIAT, once again, was found to have the smallest portion of its proteome containing PS IDRs ≥ 50 residues in length.

  Recall plots were generated using ParSe v2 outputs for the five specificity proteomes and six distribution proteomes, along with the reference human proteome. AUC > 0.5 indicated a greater propensity of a proteome to undergo protein phase separation when compared to the human reference proteome [23]. For the specificity group, EIR had the greatest AUC of 0.650, indicating an increased likelihood of undergoing protein phase separation (Figure 5B). EIOBEIR had an AUC of 0.606, LTSBEIR had an AUC of 0.608, and NDIR had an AUC of 0.586. These three proteomes were all more likely than the reference human proteome to undergo protein phase separation. Finally, NDIAT had an AUC of 0.372, suggesting that it was less likely to exhibit phase separation behavior.

  For the distribution subgroup proteomes, DIMIR had an AUC of 0.647, ODIR had an AUC of 0.580, DISIR had an AUC of 0.621, and DIA had an AUC of 0.596 (Figure 5D). These four proteomes were, therefore, more likely to exhibit phase separation behavior. NDIR and NDIAT had identical AUCs as those described in the specificity group.


## Discussion
  Our three‐pronged analyses with RIDAO, AlphaMissense, and ParSe v2 highlight the intersection between variations in protein disorder, the pathogenicity associated with missense mutations, and the propensity for LLPS, offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying retinal physiology as compared to other tissues. The novelty of our work lies in integrating these structural and functional analyses within the framework of gene expression specificity and distribution as defined by the HPA. To our knowledge, this conceptual approach of quantifying molecular features has not been previously applied to the retinal proteome. By analyzing proteins grouped according to these HPA‐defined subcategories, we provide a systems‐level view of how structural properties correlate with expression patterns unique to or shared with other tissues. This systems‐level view revealed that disorder, LLPS propensity, and pathogenicity are not randomly distributed but instead show meaningful variation that aligns with expression specificity and breadth, suggesting biologically encoded structural diversity.

  The proteins were analyzed by distinct subgroups based on their expression specificity and distribution subgroupings, which allowed us to further understand the molecular landscape of retinal proteins. Once again, specificity refers to how unique the expression of certain genes is to the retina compared to other tissues, identifying genes with elevated expression specifically in the retina. Distribution refers to the presence of genes across different tissues, identifying genes detected at a minimum expression threshold exclusively in the retina. Our findings reveal a relationship between gene expression in the retina and the structural and functional characteristics of its proteomes. The distinct subgroups based on expression specificity and distribution exhibit substantial structural diversity and functional adaptability as measured by the various structural biology utilized in our bioinformatics study. This underscores the retina's specialized biological roles and provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying retinal health and disease. The considerable variability among these subgroups highlights the complex interplay between gene expression patterns and protein functionality.

  Our initial PONDR analysis revealed significant variations in intrinsic disorder among both specificity and distribution subgroup proteomes. The significant variances detected by our ANOVA analysis may indicate that these differences are related to gene expression patterns. In addition, these findings suggest a diverse structural landscape within the proteomes analyzed in this study, where higher disorder levels in certain subgroups imply greater functional versatility and adaptability, crucial for various biological processes in the retina and other tissues such as signaling, regulation, and interaction with multiple partners [43, 44, 45]. For example, proteins like transcription factors and signaling molecules often require flexibility to bind various targets and mediate complex interactions [46]. A well‐studied case is the heat shock factor (HSF) family of transcription factors, which are enriched in intrinsically disordered regions that facilitate dynamic binding to DNA and co‐regulators during stress responses [46]. This flexibility likely plays a role in the rapid activation of heat shock proteins under proteotoxic conditions, underscoring the importance of intrinsic disorder in modulating regulatory protein function. The elevated disorder in specific subgroups could indicate a higher degree of functional versatility and adaptability, which may be essential for the dynamic environment of the retina. Moreover, understanding the intrinsic disorder profiles of these proteomes could provide insights into disease mechanisms, as disordered regions are often implicated in pathological conditions like neurodegenerative diseases and cancer [47, 48, 49]. For example, one study highlights that proteins such as tau, α‐synuclein, and transactive response (TAR) DNA‐binding protein 43, each of which harbors extensive intrinsically disordered regions, are central to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), respectively. These disordered regions promote aggregation and confer binding promiscuity, both of which contribute to pathological protein misfolding and cellular dysfunction in these neurodegenerative disorders [47].

  Our analysis of the ADS versus the PPDR in both the specificity and distribution proteomes reveals notable patterns of protein disorder across various subgroups. We identified the LTSBEIR and DIMIR groups as having the highest proportions of highly disordered proteins, with 64.5% and 63.8%, respectively. This elevated disorder is likely due to the functional demands placed on these proteomes. Both LTSBEIR and DIMIR are composed of proteins that are broadly expressed across multiple tissues, suggesting roles in fundamental regulatory and signaling pathways. Proteins in these categories may need to interact with a wide range of cellular partners and adapt to diverse physiological conditions, which are key features of IDPs [50]. IDPs facilitate rapid cellular responses and multipartner binding due to their conformational flexibility [51]. Thus, the high disorder in these subgroups likely reflects their enrichment in multifunctional proteins critical for system‐wide regulatory control. For example, the beta subunit of phosphodiesterase 6 (PDE6B), a protein involved in the visual signal processing pathway, has been found to have IDRs [15]. IDRs have also been identified in the Centrosomal Protein of 290 kDA (CEP290), a protein involved in ciliary function in retinal photoreceptors. Mutations in the CEP290 gene are linked to various ciliopathies, including Leber congenital amaurosis [15].

  The diversity of protein populations within each group underscores the complex nature of protein functionality in the retina and other tissues. The specificity and distribution subgroups exhibit significant differences in their protein disorder profiles, as confirmed by χ 2 analysis. These results suggest that the variations in disorder levels are not random and may be reflective of the distinct biological roles and environments of these proteins. For instance, the high proportion of disordered proteins in LTSBEIR may be linked to its functional requirements in the retina, which demands rapid and flexible responses to environmental changes.

  Our AlphaMissense analysis revealed that the LTSBEIR and DIA subgroups have the highest pathogenicity scores, indicating a higher likelihood that any occurring missense mutations will affect protein function in a pathogenic way. Specifically, LTSBEIR had a pathogenicity score of 0.562, with 48% of its proteome labeled as pathogenic, while DIA had a pathogenicity score of 0.568, with 49% of its proteome considered pathogenic. These findings align with the high levels of intrinsic disorder observed in these subgroups from our prior PONDR VSL2B analysis. The elevated disorder in these subgroups suggests that these proteins play crucial roles in cellular processes such as signaling, regulation, and interaction with multiple partners, which are essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to environmental changes. Our findings suggest that while these proteins are more structurally flexible, they are also more vulnerable to functional disruptions caused by mutations. This vulnerability underscores the significance of these disorder proteins in disease mechanisms, as their dysfunction can lead to pathological conditions. These findings are consistent with a previous study that found proteins implicated in inherited retinal disease that were associated with an over‐representation of missense mutations also had a high amount of intrinsic disorder [14]. Another study found that missense mutations in the Cone‐Rod Homeobox (CRX) gene cause autosomal dominant retinopathies [19], while another concluded that missense mutations in the pre‐mRNA processing factor 6 (PRPF6) gene causes retinitis pigmentosa [18]. In summary, the combination of high intrinsic disorder and high pathogenicity scores in the LTSBEIR and DIA subgroups suggest that these proteins play critical roles in cellular function and disease.

  Our analysis using ParSe v2 further identified significant differences in the propensity for protein phase separation across the specificity and distribution subgroups. EIR was found to have the greatest portion of its proteome containing phase separating intrinsically disordered regions (PS IDRs) of ≥ 50 residues, with an AUC of 0.650, indicating a high likelihood of undergoing protein phase separation. In contrast, NDIAT had the smallest portion of PS IDRs and the lowest AUC of 0.372, suggesting a lower likelihood of phase separation. Similarly, in the distribution group, DIMIR had the greatest portion of its proteome with PS IDRs ≥ 50 residues and an AUC of 0.647, while NDIAT again had the smallest portion of PS IDRs.

  These findings indicate that subgroups with a higher proportion of PS IDRs, such as EIR and DIMIR, have a greater propensity to undergo protein phase separation. This behavior is essential for cellular compartmentalization and stress adaptation, particularly through the formation of membrane‐less organelles such as stress granules and processing bodies [46, 52, 53]. IDPs play a central role in this process, where multivalent weak interactions drive the formation of dynamic biomolecular condensates. These condensates regulate key processes like ribonucleic acid (RNA) metabolism, proteostasis, and signaling—all crucial for retinal health [54]. LLPS has also been shown to have a role in innate and adaptive immune responses [55]. For instance, Cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS), a cytosolic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) sensor in the innate immune pathway, undergoes LLPS with viral DNA to form condensates that enhance cGAMP production and activate STING‐mediated signaling [55]. Similarly, in adaptive immunity, LLPS facilitates T‐cell receptor (TCR) signal transduction by promoting the formation of condensates containing signaling molecules like LAT, GRB2, and Sos1, while excluding phosphatases like CD45 that inhibit T‐cell activation [55]. These findings illustrate how phase separation orchestrates immune signaling with spatial precision.

  The differences in LLPS between subgroup proteomes may reflect the different immunogenic roles of retinal tissue when compared to the other bodily tissues. Additionally, it has been found that aberrant protein phase separation plays a role in pathological protein aggregates found in neurodegenerative diseases [56]. The higher propensity of the EIR subgroup proteome to undergo protein phase separation may hint at some role of protein disorder and phase separation in the pathogenesis of retinal disease. As these EIR genes are elevated specifically in the retina, dysfunction of protein phase separation may even increase the likelihood of pathogenic protein aggregation. This can implicate diseases such as age‐related macular degeneration, where protein misfolding processes may lead to a buildup of drusen and other extracellular deposits, leading to pathologic states [57]. The lower likelihood of phase separation in NDIAT suggests that these proteins may have more structured regions and different functional roles compared to those in subgroups with higher phase separation potential.

  The combination of high intrinsic disorder, high pathogenicity scores, and a strong propensity for phase separation in subgroups like LTSBEIR and DIA underscores the complex functional landscape of these proteomes. These proteins' susceptibility to mutations and their dynamic structural properties highlights their importance in disease mechanisms and their potential as therapeutic targets.

  Future research should focus on characterizing these pathogenic mutations and phase‐separating regions in greater detail to elucidate their specific roles and impacts on cellular functions and disease progression. By targeting these high‐disorder, high‐pathogenicity proteins, it may be possible to develop new treatments for retinal diseases and other conditions. However, it is also important to recognize that gene expression profiles may change between retinal disease and non‐disease states. Consequently, specific proteins that are associated with high levels of disorder and missense pathogenicity may differ depending on the health or disease state of the retinal proteome analyzed. As such, the next steps should also focus on comparing these metrics in both healthy and diseased retinal proteomes. This may help pinpoint specific proteins implicated in certain retinal diseases, such as age‐related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy. Identification of these proteins may bring us one step closer to developing new therapeutic modalities for common retinal conditions. However, this paper is intended to be an exploratory study looking to develop our understanding of retinal physiology and has merit through its investigation into the molecular interactions that influence the proper function of the human retina.


## Limitations
  A limitation of our study is the use of mRNA expression data as a proxy for protein abundance, which may not always reflect actual protein levels due to post‐transcriptional and translational regulation. As such, the proteomes defined by specificity and distribution subgroups represent transcript‐derived estimates rather than direct protein quantification. Despite this limitation, our analysis demonstrates that structural features such as intrinsic disorder, pathogenicity, and LLPS propensity differ systematically across proteins grouped by expression specificity and distribution in the retina. These HPA‐established groupings capture the uniqueness of gene expression in the retina and how broadly a gene is expressed across other tissues. The emergence of distinct molecular signatures in these subgroups suggests that tissue‐specific gene expression patterns may encode predictable molecular differences in protein network. These findings can be further validated in retinal or retinal‐like cell models not included in the HPA dataset, enabling independent assessment of whether the levels of intrinsic disorder and phase separation observed computationally are reproducible at the proteome level. Moreover, disease‐modeling systems can be studied to determine whether these structural features are altered in pathologic states, potentially linking protein‐level biophysics to broader disease mechanisms. This provides a meaningful path forward for translating our findings into experimental studies that explore how the retina's unique proteomic architecture may influence its physiological processes or susceptibility to disease.

  Our findings demonstrate that even under normal physiological conditions, retinal proteins stratified by specificity and distribution exhibit distinct molecular characteristics. These observations suggest that tissue‐specific expression patterns are reflected in measurable differences in protein structure and behavior, even prior to disease onset. This opens the door for future validation studies in cell‐based models. For example, intrinsic disorder predictions could be experimentally assessed in relevant retinal cell lines using proteomics approaches or biophysical assays. Moreover, comparing these structural features between healthy and disease‐modeled retinal systems could reveal how disorder and phase separation propensity shift in pathologic states. Such analyses would provide important mechanistic insight into how proteomic composition and structural plasticity contribute to retinal disease processes and could ultimately inform therapeutic targeting strategies.

  While our bioinformatics study provides valuable insights into the structural and functional diversity of retinal proteomes, it is not without limitations. First, our analyses are heavily dependent on the accuracy and completeness of online databases such as the HPA and the datasets utilized by tools like AlphaMissense, PONDR VSL2B, and ParSe v2. Any errors or biases in these data sources could potentially affect our results and interpretations. In addition, the predictive nature of bioinformatics tools means that our findings are based on computational models rather than experimental validation. Although tools like AlphaMissense and PONDR VSL2B provide robust predictions, they cannot fully capture the complexity of protein behavior in vivo. Experimental studies are necessary to confirm the pathogenicity of specific mutations and the propensity for protein phase separation observed in our study. Future directions should focus on identifying specific proteins involved in retinal disease, evaluating them for intrinsic disorder, missense mutations, and protein phase separation. Understanding the behavior of these proteins can lead to targeted therapeutic drug development for a unique retinal disease, rather than providing a broad overview of entire proteomes.

  Another key limitation of this study is the ambiguity in pathogenicity classification for some proteomes, as predictive models rely on available data and computational algorithms that may not fully capture the functional consequences of missense mutations. Additionally, while our bioinformatic approach is validated in the literature and has been successfully applied to other ocular tissues, there are numerous tools available for measuring intrinsic disorder. The tools used in this study were selected based on prior experience and their established use in similar analyses, but there may be other potentially more refined methods for assessing intrinsic disorder and phase separation. Future studies integrating alternative computational tools, functional assays, and patient‐derived data are essential to refine these findings and establish their clinical relevance.

  Additionally, our analysis of mutation pathogenicity was restricted to missense mutations due to the use of AlphaMissense, which is specifically designed to evaluate the functional impact of single amino acid substitutions. Other mutation types, such as frameshift, nonsense, and splice site mutations, were not analyzed in this study. Future studies utilizing broader variant interpretation tools could expand upon our findings by assessing additional classes of genetic variation relevant to retinal disease. Lastly, there was ambiguity in pathogenicity classification for some proteomes, which may not fully capture the functional consequences of missense mutations. AlphaMissense provides a continuous pathogenicity score between 0 and 1, but many proteomes in our analysis fall within an intermediate range, resulting in an “Ambiguous” classification. This reflects uncertainty about the cumulative pathogenic potential of these proteomes and limits definitive interpretation. This ambiguity may also reflect the wide range of missense mutations present within these subgroups, which likely include both benign and pathogenic variants, thereby diluting the overall signal and complicating interpretation at the group level.

  Our study focuses on the intrinsic disorder, pathogenicity, and phase separation within the context of retinal proteins, which may not fully represent the behavior of these proteins in other tissues or under different physiological conditions. The dynamic environment of the retina, with its unique functional requirements, might influence protein behavior differently than in other tissue types. Furthermore, the reliance on static datasets does not account for temporal changes in protein expression, post‐translational modifications, or interactions that could influence the functional properties of proteins. Additionally, the identification of IDRs and their roles in phase separation are based on specific length and compositional criteria. Variations in these criteria or the use of different computational tools could yield different results, highlighting the need for standardized approaches in bioinformatics analyses. Finally, the nature of our study being dependent on large‐scale data analysis means that we may overlook subtleties in individual protein behavior and interactions. Future research should aim to integrate experimental data, explore protein behavior in various physiological contexts, and develop standardized methodologies for bioinformatics analyses.

  In summary, our integrated analysis using RIDAO, AlphaMissense, and ParSe v2 has provided valuable insights into the structural and functional diversity of retinal proteomes. The elevated pathogenicity and phase separation propensity in specific subgroups point to potential areas for further research, particularly in understanding molecular mechanisms underpinning retinal physiology. The detailed insights from this study pave the way for future investigations into the roles of IDPs and their contributions to cellular processes and disease pathogenesis. By characterizing key differences in protein disorder, pathogenicity, and phase separation potential, our study provides a valuable framework for future research aimed at understanding these differences and advancing our knowledge of retinal biology.


## Conclusion
  Our study presents an integrated analysis of the structural properties, pathogenic potential, and phase separation tendencies of proteomes expressed in the retina and across other tissues. We reveal that protein intrinsic disorder is unevenly distributed among proteomic subgroups, with subgroups such as LTSBEIR and DIMIR exhibiting the highest levels of disorder. These variations appear to be shaped by gene expression patterns and functional demands, suggesting a structural basis for tissue specialization.

  Importantly, subgroups with greater intrinsic disorder were also found to have a higher propensity for phase separation and pathogenic missense mutations. This triad—disorder, LLPS potential, and mutational burden—points to a complex molecular architecture that may underlie both normal retinal function and susceptibility to disease. The association between gene expression specificity and structural flexibility offers new insight into how the retina maintains cellular compartmentalization and dynamic signaling while remaining vulnerable to misregulation and aggregation‐related pathologies.

  By linking transcriptomic classification with bioinformatic structure‐function predictions, our study lays the groundwork for a systems‐level understanding of retinal proteomics. These findings offer a foundation for future studies to explore disorder‐driven mechanisms in retinal degeneration and other disease contexts, potentially informing new biomarker discovery and therapeutic design.
