# The Glycine-Rich Region as a Flexible Molecular Glue Promoting hPrP106–145 Aggregation into β-Sheet Structures


## Abstract
  The abnormal aggregation of human prion protein (hPrP) into cross-β fibrillar amyloid deposits is associated with prion diseases such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and fatal familial insomnia. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the early stages of prion aggregation remain poorly understood. In this study, we employed multiple long-timescale atomistic discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) simulations to investigate the conformational dynamics of hPrP106–145, a critical fragment with intrinsic aggregation propensity and key involvement in infectivity. Our results revealed that the hPrP106–145 monomer primarily adopted a helical conformation in the alanine-rich region (residues 109–118), while the remaining sequence was largely unstructured, exhibiting dynamic β-sheet formation around residues 120AVV122, 128YVL130, and 138IIH140. Upon dimerization, β-sheet formation was significantly enhanced, particularly around 138IIH140, which displayed the highest β-sheet propensity and inter-peptide contact frequency, underscoring its pivotal role in aggregate stabilization. The glycine-rich region (residues 119–131) was found to facilitate aggregation by conferring structural flexibility due to glycine’s minimal steric hindrance. This flexibility allowed hydrophobic and aromatic residues to collapse dynamically, forming transient intra- and inter-peptide β-sheets. These interactions acted as a molecular glue, promoting aggregation while maintaining structural adaptability. Although β-sheet formation lowered potential energy, excessive β-sheet content resulted in significant entropic loss, highlighting a trade-off between stability and conformational entropy. Overall, this study provides molecular insights into the early nucleation events of hPrP106–145 aggregation, emphasizing the critical role of glycine-mediated flexibility. Our findings deepen the understanding of prion misfolding and offer a computational framework for exploring glycine-rich peptide phase separation in amyloid-related disorders.


## Introduction.
  The prion protein (PrP), encoded by the PRNP gene, is a cell surface glycoprotein expressed in various tissues, with its highest concentrations found in the central and peripheral nervous systems1, 2. A hallmark of prion diseases is the misfolding and aberrant accumulation of PrP into amyloid fibrils1, 3–5. These diseases include Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, kuru, fatal familial insomnia, and Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome in humans, as well as scrapie in sheep and bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle2, 4, 6. The functional monomeric form of PrP, termed PrPC, predominantly comprises α-helical structures (~42%) with minimal β-sheet content (~3%), as demonstrated by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy7–10. In contrast, the disease-associated misfolded form, PrPSc, shows a pronounced increase in β-sheet content (~43%) and a corresponding reduction in α-helical structures (~30%)9, 10. In prion diseases, PrPC undergoes a conformational transformation into the aggregation-prone PrPSc 10, 11. During prion replication, PrPSc fibrils act as templates, catalyzing the conversion of PrPC into additional PrPSc molecules12. This self-propagating cascade of misfolding and aggregation underpins the infectious nature of prions and their capacity to spread within the host organism.

  The PrP is initially synthesized as a 253-amino-acid precursor, which undergoes post-translational processing to produce a mature 209-amino-acid protein following the removal of N- and C-terminal signal peptides3, 4. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis reveals that monomeric PrPC comprises an unstructured N-terminal region and a structured globular C-terminal domain spanning residues 125–2287–9. This C-terminal domain features three α-helices (residues 144–156, 174–194, and 200–228) and a short β-sheet composed of residues 128–131 and 161–1647–9, 13. By contrast, atomic-resolution cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies of PrPSc fibrils, identified in highly infectious prion rod preparations, reveal a highly ordered, in-register β-sheet structure extending across PrP residues 94–22512, 14, 15. In these PrPSc fibril models (PDB: 7LNA14, 7QIG12, and 8A0015), the N-terminal residues 94–169 and C-terminal residues 170–225 of each PrP monomer form two asymmetric cross-β cores. The independent fibril models derived from each of the aforementioned core regions—PrP106–145 (PDB: 6UUR)16, PrP108–141 (PDB: 7RL4)17, PrP108–144 (PDB: 7YAT)18, and PrP170–229 (PDB: 6LNI)19—have been comprehensively characterized using cryo-EM analysis. These findings underscore that both the two fibrillar core domains of PrP are capable of independently assembling into well-ordered fibrillar PrPSc structures, highlighting their individual contributions to prion propagation and pathogenesis.

  Residues 34–113 of PrPC, although not directly involved in its native folding7–10, 20, play a pivotal role in prion formation21, 22. Deletion of residues 34–94 results in protease-resistant aggregates with a proteolytic profile identical to the wild-type protein, whereas deletion of residues 34–113 produces aggregates with a distinct profile, underscoring the critical importance of the 95–113 segment in PrP misfolding23. Additionally, the PrP106–126 region has been shown to promote the pathological aggregation of cellular PrP and enhance its neurotoxicity in prion diseases24, 25. Notably, while the amyloid core of non-infectious fibrils generated in vitro typically begins around residue 160, infectious prion fibrils formed in vivo span a broader region, encompassing residues 90–23026, 27. Moreover, the truncated PrP23–144 can independently form infectious fibrils and induce disease in animal models28. These findings suggest that the PrP106–145 region, located within the first of the two protofilament cores, plays a central role in the misfolding process and serves as a critical determinant in the formation of infectious prions. Although the structural dynamics and environmental factors, such as pH and mutations, influencing the PrPSc structured domain (e.g., residues 125–228) have been extensively investigated using computational simulations29–31, the self-assembly mechanism of PrP106–145, a crucial contributor to infectious fibril formation, remains poorly understood. Therefore, elucidating the atomistic conformational dynamics of PrP106–145 during the early stages of aggregation is crucial not only for uncovering the pathological aggregation mechanisms of PrP—particularly the pivotal role of PrP106–145 in prion infectivity—but also for informing therapeutic strategy development against prion diseases and advancing our broader understanding of amyloid aggregation in other pathogenic peptides.

  To investigate the aggregation mechanism of PrP106–145, we conducted multiple long time-scale discrete molecular dynamics (DMD)32, 33 simulations. Fifty independent 600 ns simulations showed that the PrP106–145 monomer primarily adopted a helical structure in the alanine-rich region (residues 109MKHMAGAAAA118) and remained largely unstructured elsewhere, with dynamic β-sheet formations centered on 120AVV122, 128YVL130, and 138IIH140. In contrast, fifty 1000 ns independent DMD simulations of the dimer revealed enhanced β-sheet formation and reduced unstructured conformations. Residues 138IIH140 exhibited the highest β-sheet propensity and interpeptide contact frequency, suggesting a key role in structural stability. The glycine-rich region (119GAVVGGLGGYVLG131) facilitated β-sheet formation through dynamic interactions of hydrophobic and aromatic residues, particularly 120AVV122 and 128YVL130, with themselves or 138IIH140. The comparable inter- and intra-peptide contacts in this glycine-rich region indicated low structural frustration. These findings suggested that the flexible glycine-rich region acts as a molecular glue, promoting β-sheet aggregation during early nucleation of PrP106–145. Overall, our findings not only contribute to a better understanding of the pathological aggregation mechanisms of PrP but also lay the groundwork for future studies aimed at identifying potential therapeutic targets to disrupt these early aggregation events.


## Materials and methods

### Molecular systems.
  The amino acid sequence of hPrP106–145 used in our simulations is shown in Figure 1a. The initial structure was derived from the monomeric hPrP90–231 (PDB: 5YJ5, Figure 1b), determined by NMR spectroscopy in solution34 and widely utilized for computational studies on hPrP conformational dynamics35, 36. The hPrP106–145 segment was selected for its ability to independently form cross-β protofilaments (PDB: 6UUR16, Figure 1c) and function as a separate protofilament core within full-length hPrP fibrils (PDB: 7LNA14, Figure 1d). While non-infectious prion fibrils typically initiate around residue 160, infectious fibrils span residues ~90–23026, 27. The ability of truncated hPrP23–144 to form infectious fibrils and induce disease highlights the critical role of residues 106–145 in PrP aggregation and pathological conversion.

  To investigate the folding and aggregation dynamics of hPrP106–145, we performed comprehensive all-atom DMD simulations for both monomeric and dimeric systems. For monomer simulations, fifty independent 600 ns DMD runs were conducted, each initialized with different velocities within a 6.5 nm cubic simulation box to ensure extensive conformational sampling. The central structures of the ten most populated monomeric conformations, collectively accounting for approximately 38.5% of the sampled states, were selected as initial structures for dimerization simulations. To examine the self-assembly dynamics of hPrP106–145, fifty independent 1000 ns DMD simulations were carried out for dimerization, starting from different initial coordinates and velocities. In each simulation, two hPrP106–145 peptides, randomly selected from the top ten monomeric conformations, were placed in a 7.5 nm cubic box with an initial intermolecular distance exceeding 1.5 nm to prevent immediate interactions.


### DMD Simulations.
  All simulations were conducted at 300 K using the all-atom DMD algorithm33, 37 with the Medusa force field38. While DMD and conventional MD adhere to the same physical principles, DMD differs by discretizing interatomic interaction potentials, leading to a collision-driven system where atomic velocities change upon energy step transitions39. As step sizes decrease, all-atom DMD approximates traditional MD. The Medusa force field accounts for both bonded interactions (covalent bonds, bond angles, and dihedrals) and nonbonded interactions (van der Waals forces, solvation, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatics), similar to conventional MD force fields38. Solvation energy was computed using the Lazaridis–Karplus effective energy function40, while hydrogen bonds were explicitly modeled via a reaction-like algorithm41. Electrostatic interactions between charged atoms were calculated using the Debye–Hückel approximation, with a Debye length of ~10 Å under physiological conditions. DMD software is available to academic researchers through Molecules In Action, LLC (www.moleculesinaction.com). The simulation units for mass, time, length, and energy were set to 1 Da, ~50 fs, 1 Å, and 1 kcal/mol, respectively.

  Our previous work has established the predictive power of DMD simulations coupled with the Medusa force field and the EEF1 implicit solvation model41–43. These studies successfully recapitulated native protein folding—capturing both secondary and tertiary structures41—and produced conformational ensembles that align well with single-molecule FRET measurements of multidomain proteins42, 43. In addition, our amyloid aggregation simulations on calcitonin peptides (hCT, sCT, phCT, DM-hCT, and TL-hCT)44–46 and amylin peptides (hIAPP, hIAPP(S20G), and rIAPP)47, 48 have accurately reproduced the experimentally observed variations in amyloidogenic propensity, further validating the utility of this approach. Notably, DMD-derived conformational ensembles of hCT monomers exhibit significant overlap with NMR data45. The accuracy of DMD with the Medusa force field has been rigorously benchmarked against standard MD simulations using force fields such as GROMOS96, OPLS-AA, AMBER99SB-ILDN, and CHARMM36m48, 49. Given its enhanced sampling efficiency and proven performance in studying protein folding and amyloid aggregation50, 51, we selected DMD with the Medusa force field to investigate the conformational dynamics of the hPrP106–145 monomer and dimer.


### Analysis Methods.
  Secondary structure characterization was performed using the DSSP (Dictionary of Secondary Structure of Proteins) algorithm52. Hydrogen bonds were identified based on an N⋯O distance cutoff of 3.5 Å and an N–H⋯O angle exceeding 150°53. Residue contacts were defined when non-adjacent side chain or main chain heavy atoms were within 0.65 nm of each other. To characterize the conformational diversity of hPrP106–145 monomers, we applied the Daura clustering algorithm54 with a backbone RMSD cutoff of 0.55 nm. The algorithm evaluated the pairwise backbone RMSD between structures and grouped those with RMSD values below the cutoff into the same cluster. Structures exceeding the threshold were assigned to separate clusters, enabling us to identify and categorize distinct conformational states within the ensemble. To further investigate conformational landscapes, a two-dimensional (2D) free energy surface, or potential mean force (PMF)55, was computed using the equation F(x, y) = −kBT ln P(x, y) + C, where F(x, y) denotes the relative free energy at given parameter values x and y, P(x, y) is the probability of observing a conformation with those parameter values. Here, kB is the Boltzmann constant and T is the simulation temperature. The constant C defines the zero-point of the energy scale and corresponds to the most populated state, i.e., the state with the highest P(x, y). As a result, free energy values are expressed relative to the global minimum. The local Cα density, ρ(r), analogous to a radial distribution function but without normalization to bulk density56, was determined relative to the geometric center of the peptide complex using the equation ρ(r) = Nr,r+dr/(4πr2dr). Here, Nr,r+dr denotes the number of atoms located within a radial shell spanning distances r to r + dr from the geometric center, which was defined based on the Cα positions of all residues.


## Results and discussion

### The hPrP106–145 monomer primarily adopted helices around the alanine-rich region, while the other regions remained mostly unstructured with transient β-sheets.
  The conformational dynamics of the hPrP106–145 monomer were explored through fifty independent 600 ns DMD simulations to ensure extensive sampling. Structural parameters, including the radius of gyration (Rg), the number of backbone hydrogen bonds and heavy atom contacts, along with the content of each secondary structure, were monitored over time to assess sampling efficiency and equilibrium (Figure S1a-c). The absence of persistent trends in these metrics, alongside sustained fluctuations, confirmed that the simulations avoided kinetic traps and achieved sufficient sampling. Additionally, the stable ensemble averages of these structural parameters suggested the system reached a reasonable equilibrium (Figure S1d). To minimize bias from initial structures, only the final 300 ns of each trajectory was used for conformational analysis.

  The folding dynamics of the hPrP106–145 monomer were characterized by tracking the time evolution of secondary structures for each residue (Figure 2a&b), revealing frequent conformational transitions. Residues 106–118 predominantly adopted helical structures, whereas the remaining residues largely displayed unstructured conformations, including random coils and bends, with occasional transient short β-sheets. Ensemble-averaged secondary structure analysis indicated that the hPrP106–145 monomer consisted of 56.7% unstructured conformations, 20.2% helices, and 14.4% β-sheets (Figure 2c). Residue-level secondary structure propensity analysis showed that helices were primarily formed by residues 109–118, with a propensity ranging from 55% to 82% (Figure 2d). In contrast, residues 106–108 and 119–145 exhibited a strong preference for unstructured conformations, predominantly exceeding 60%. Dynamic β-sheet formation was observed in three key regions: 120AVV122 (~27.7%), 128YVL130 (~48.3%), and 138IIH140 (~39.9%). Conformational clustering of the hPrP106–145 monomer using the Daura algorithm54 with a backbone RMSD cutoff of 0.55 nm identified the 12 most dominant structural states. The analysis revealed marked conformational heterogeneity: the alanine-rich region consistently adopted helical conformations, whereas the remaining segments were predominantly unstructured with sporadic short β-sheets. The low occupancy of individual clusters further underscored the intrinsically dynamic nature of the hPrP106–145 monomer (Figure 2e).

  The conformational conversion dynamics of the helix-rich region (residues 106–118) and the β-sheet-prone segment (residues 119–145) within hPrP106–145 monomer were analyzed across individual DMD trajectories (Figure 3a&b). The time evolution of the average helical content in residues 106–118 predominantly remained within a range of 40%–80%, with only rare instances of helix unfolding below 40% (Figure 3a). In contrast, β-sheet formation in residues 119–145 generally fluctuated between 0% and 30%, though transient β-sheet formations exceeding 40% were also observed (Figure 3b). Additionally, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis revealed that residues 109MKHMAGAAAA118, 119GAVVG122, 127GYVL130, and 136RPIIH140 exhibited lower fluctuations compared to the overall peptide average. The high structural flexibility of residues 119–145 can be attributed to the abundance of glycine residues, which, due to the lack of a side chain, hinder stable conformational formation. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that glycine-rich segments play a crucial role in driving liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a process characterized by frequent dynamic conformational transitions.

  The conformational free energy landscape of the hPrP106–145 monomer was analyzed by plotting the β-sheet ratio of residues 119–145 against the corresponding radius of gyration of whole hPrP106–145 peptide, using the final 300 ns of data from fifty independent DMD simulations (Figure 3d). The results revealed a broad free energy basin, with the β-sheet ratio in residues 119–145 varying between 0.0 and 0.5. Notably, β-sheet formation did not lead to a reduction in the monomer’s radius of gyration, likely due to the weak structural connectivity between residues 119–145 and 106–118. Although increased β-sheet content correlated with a reduction in unstructured conformations, leading to lower potential energy states, highly β-sheet-rich structures were associated with relatively high free energy (Figure S2). This suggests that β-sheet formation significantly reduces the conformational entropy of the hPrP106–145 monomer, making these states thermodynamically less favorable.

  The Lazaridis–Karplus implicit solvation model40, employed in our DMD simulations, estimated solvation free energy using empirically derived potentials calibrated against experimental measurements. Although this model did not explicitly represent individual water molecules, it effectively captured solvent-mediated thermodynamic contributions, including the entropic gains associated with the release of structured water molecules from solvation shells. Notably, our observation of dynamic and transient β-sheet formation within the glycine-rich region of the hPrP106–145 monomer was consistent with previous experimental and computational findings. For example, NMR studies of the hPrP monomer revealed that the glycine-rich segment was intrinsically disordered34, supporting its structural flexibility. Furthermore, similar transient β-sheet conformations in glycine-rich regions—such as the RGG domain of hnRNPA257 and the M2 domain of suckerin49—were reported in molecular dynamics simulations utilizing explicit solvent models. Collectively, these findings supported the validity of our observations despite the use of an implicit solvation model.


### The minimal steric hindrance of glycine facilitated the collapse of hydrophobic and aromatic residues within hPrP106–145 leading to the formation of short flexible β-sheets.
  To further uncover key interactions contributing to the monomer’s structural stability, residue-pairwise contact frequency analysis was performed (Figure 4). Consistent with the secondary structure analysis (Figure 3a), the alanine-rich N-terminal region (residues 108–120) exhibited a strong helical structure pattern along the diagonal (snapshot 1, Figure 4). This pattern aligns with previous findings, where alanine (A or ALA) was shown to favor helix formation58. Supporting this, poly-alanine sequences are well-documented to adopt helical structures, and the alanine-rich region preceding the polyQ tract in ataxin-7 similarly demonstrated stable helices59, 60, reinforcing this observation. Beyond the helical segment, the hPrP106–145 monomer also exhibited two distinct β-hairpin patterns, oriented perpendicular to the diagonal. The first involved residues 119GAVVG123 paired with 127GYVLG131 (snapshot 2, Figure 4), while the second formed between 127GYVLG131 and 137PIIHF141 (snapshot 3, Figure 4). Additionally, an intra-peptide parallel β-sheet interaction emerged between 120AVVG123 and 137PIIH140 (snapshot 4, Figure 4). The glycine-rich region spanning residues 119–145 lacked a stable, ordered conformation due to glycine’s absence of a side chain, leading to heightened structural flexibility and dynamic fluctuations. This low steric hindrance allowed nearby hydrophobic and aromatic residues to collapse into transient61, 62, short β-sheet structures. Such behavior mirrors findings from previous studies, which reported that glycine-rich regions — including those in FUS63 and HNRNPG64 — frequently display intrinsic disorder. Notably, these regions are critical in driving liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS)65, 66, facilitated by dynamic hydrophobic and aromatic interactions, supporting the observed behavior of the hPrP106–145 monomer.


### Dimerization of hPrP106–145 facilitated a conformational transition towards β-sheet structures while simultaneously reducing unstructured conformations.
  To gain deeper insights into the effects of aggregation on the conformational dynamics of hPrP106–145, dimerization was further examined through fifty independent 1000 ns DMD simulations. To mitigate potential biases from initial structures, each hPrP106–145 monomer in the dimerization simulations was randomly selected from the top 10 most populated central conformations using an in-house algorithm. The efficiency of conformational sampling and equilibrium was systematically evaluated by monitoring the time evolution of key structural parameters, including the radius of gyration, total number of hydrogen bonds and atomic contacts, and the probability distribution of secondary structure elements (Figure S3). All these parameters stabilized during the final 600 ns without significant fluctuations, confirming that the system had reached equilibrium. Consequently, the last 600 ns of simulation data were utilized for ensemble-averaged structural analysis of hPrP106–145 dimers.

  The conformational dynamics of hPrP106–145 dimerization were analyzed by tracking the time evolution of each residue’s secondary structure, along with intra- and inter-peptide hydrogen bonds and atomic contacts (Figure 5a&b). The alanine-rich region (residues 108–118) consistently maintained a stable helical conformation, aligning with observations from hPrP106–145 monomer simulations. In contrast to isolated monomers, which exhibited only transient and dynamic intra-peptide β-sheet formations, dimerization promoted the formation of both intra- and inter-peptide β-sheets, leading to enhanced stabilization of β-sheet structures, particularly around residues 137PIIH140. This stabilization was driven by the formation of inter-peptide hydrogen bonds and atomic contacts, facilitating the alignment and persistence of interpeptide β-sheets. Ensemble-averaged secondary structure analysis further confirmed that dimerization promoted β-sheet formation while reducing unstructured regions compared to monomers (Figure 5c). The average β-sheet propensity, encompassing both intra- and inter-peptide β-sheet structures, increased to approximately 20.1% in hPrP106–145 dimers—a notable rise from the 14.4% observed in monomers. Concurrently, unstructured conformations dropped to 51.5% in dimers, compared to 56.7% in monomers. Meanwhile, the average helix and turn content remained relatively unchanged, with differences of less than 1% between dimers and monomers. These findings suggested that dimerization facilitated a conformational shift in hPrP106–145, promoting β-sheet formation while suppressing unstructured states.

  The average secondary structure propensity for each residue was further analyzed (Figure 5d). Compared to the hPrP106–145 monomer, dimerization significantly enhanced the β-sheet propensity for residues 138IIH140, with an average increase of ~31.6%. Additionally, a modest increase in β-sheet formation was observed around residues 120AVV122 (~8.5%) and 128YVL130 (~8.6%). This increase in β-sheet content was accompanied by a decrease in the unstructured propensity in these regions. Due to differences in β-sheet organization between the monomer and dimer, the dominant β-sheet region in the monomer primarily formed intra-molecular β-sheets around 128YVL130, whereas in the dimer, it shifted to 138IIHF141, which engaged in both intra- and inter-molecular β-sheet formation. This shift reflected a structural reorganization upon dimerization rather than a direct transformation of the monomeric β-sheet. To investigate the potential mechanisms behind these dimerization-induced conformational changes, the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) for each residue was examined (Figure 5d). In the monomeric state, residues 120AVV122 and 128YVL130 exhibited a much smaller exposed surface area than 138IIH140 (Figure 5d). In contrast, in the dimer, the smallest SASA values were primarily found around 128YVL130 and 138IIH140. Analysis of the relative changes in SASA upon dimerization indicated that dimerization led to a significant decrease (~11.7%) in the exposed surface area of residues 138IIH140 (Figure 5e), supporting their involvement in inter-molecular β-sheet formation. In contrast, the reductions in surface area for residues 120AVV122 (~4.0%) and 128YVL130 (~4.1%) were relatively modest. The local Cα density distribution function, relative to the geometric center of the dimer, further suggested that residues from the three regions 120AVV122, 128YVL130, and 138IIHF141 were primarily buried within the dimer structure (Figure 5f). These findings indicated that dimerization induced the formation of buried β-sheet conformations, particularly in the 138IIHF141 region, stabilizing the inner layer of the hPrP106–145 dimer.


### Dimerization of hPrP106–145 lowered conformational free energy by forming low-potential-energy β-sheet structures, but high β-sheet content led to significantly entropic loss.
  To gain deeper insights into the conformational changes induced by hPrP106–145 dimerization, we analyzed the conformational free energy landscape as a function of total potential energy against the average proportions of unstructured, β-sheet (including both intra- and inter-chain β-sheet), and helical conformations (Figure 6a). hPrP106–145 dimers predominantly adopting unstructured conformations exhibited significantly high free energy due to their high potential energy (corresponding to state 1 in Figure 6a). Conformations within the energy basin were enriched in both β-sheet (10%–30%) and helix (10%–30%) structures (corresponding to states 2–7 in Figure 6a). Furthermore, within the energy basin, a reduction in unstructured conformations accompanied by an increase in β-sheet content led to lower potential energy states. However, hPrP106–145 dimer conformations with β-sheet content exceeding 35% exhibited markedly lower potential energy but significantly higher free energy, suggesting that excessive β-sheet formation resulted in substantial entropic loss47, 67 (corresponding to state 8 in Figure 6a). The secondary structure probability distribution of hPrP106–145 in its isolated monomeric and dimeric states (Figure 6b) further supported a conformational shift from unstructured states toward β-sheet formation, indicating that dimerization of hPrP106–145 promoted low-conformational-free-energy states by forming low-potential-energy β-sheet structures.

  Although highly populated helical conformations generally exhibited lower potential energy and conformational free energy compared to structures with lower helical content, the helical population of hPrP106–145 remained largely unchanged between its monomeric and dimeric states (Figure 6a&b). This observation suggested that dimerization did not significantly alter the helical propensity of hPrP106–145, potentially because the number of peptides in the simulation did not reach the critical nucleus size required for conformational conversion to β-sheet structures. For instance, our previous study on the aggregation of the helical fragment of hIAPP8-20 demonstrated that oligomers composed of fewer than six peptides predominantly adopted helical conformations, whereas helix-to-β-sheet conversion occurred when the number of peptides reached approximately six to eight68.

  Our analysis of hPrP106–145 folding and dimerization dynamics (Figures 2a–b and 5a–b) revealed that transient β-sheets in monomers, stabilized only by intra-peptide interactions, became more stable upon the formation of inter-peptide backbone hydrogen bonds—indicative of inter-chain β-sheet formation. Conformational states with high inter-chain contact but limited hydrogen bonding exhibited greater β-sheet fluctuations, whereas states with well-formed inter-peptide hydrogen bonds showed enhanced structural stability. These findings suggested that the presence of a second monomer facilitated a conformational shift toward β-sheet–rich structures through the formation of inter-peptide β-sheets. Consistently, the average β-sheet propensity—including both intra- and inter-molecular contributions—for each hPrP106–145 peptide was slightly higher in the dimeric state than in the monomeric state (Figure 6b).

  To further elucidate the role of residue-specific interactions in dimer stabilization, we analyzed the average number of intra- and inter-peptide contacts for each residue in hPrP106–145 monomers and dimers (Figure 6c). The helical region spanning residues 108–120 displayed a strong tendency to form intra-molecular contacts but exhibited minimal capability for inter-molecular interactions, suggesting that this region contributed little to the early nucleation of hPrP106–145 aggregation. In contrast, the dynamic β-sheet regions, particularly residues 120AVV122 and 128YVL130 within the glycine-rich region, exhibited comparable propensities for both intra- and inter-peptide contacts, involving both main-chain and side-chain atoms, indicating their role in interpeptide β-sheet formation. Notably, residues 138IIH140 displayed the strongest propensity for inter-peptide interactions, highlighting their critical role in maintaining the stability of hPrP106–145 dimers.


### The glycine-rich region acted as a flexible molecular glue, facilitating the aggregation of hPrP106–145 into β-sheet–rich structures.
  Residue-pairwise contact frequency analysis within the hPrP106–145 dimer was performed to elucidate the critical interactions driving its aggregation (Figure 7). The intra-peptide contact patterns in the dimer were similar to those observed in the isolated hPrP106–145 monomer (snapshots 1–4 in Figure 7) and included one helical motif (108NMKHMAGAAAAGA120), two β-hairpin motifs (127GYVLG131 vs. 119GAVVG123, and 127GYVLGSA133 vs. 135SRPIIHF141), and one intra-peptide parallel β-sheet (120AVVGGL125 vs. 136RPIIHF141). In contrast, the residue-pairwise inter-peptide contact frequency revealed diverse parallel and anti-parallel inter-chain β-sheet contact patterns, with the central β-sheet region primarily localized around residues 120AVV122, 128YVL130, and 138IIH140, each exhibiting distinct average contact frequencies (snapshots 6–12 in Figure 7). Notably, residue 138IIH140 showed the strongest propensity to form inter-peptide β-sheets, predominantly contributing to one antiparallel (135SRPIIHFGSD144 vs. 133AMSRPIIHFG142) and one parallel (135SRPIIHFGS143 vs. 135SRPIIHFGS143) β-sheet (snapshots 5–6 in Figure 7). Additionally, inter-peptide β-sheets involving interactions between 138IIH140 and residues 120AVV122 or 128YVL130 were observed, albeit with slightly weaker tendencies (snapshots 7–9 in Figure 7), including contacts such as 138IIHFGS143 vs. 126GGYVLG131, 135SRPII139 vs. 120AVVGG124, and 137PIIHFG142 vs. 126GGYVLG131. Moreover, hydrophobic or aromatic residues, in conjunction with glycine-rich segments, appeared to drive interactions among residues 120AVV122 and 128YVL130, either with themselves or with each other, resulting in weakly populated β-sheet formations (snapshots 10–12 in Figure 7), such as 126GGYVLGS132 vs. 124GLGGYVL130, 123GGLGGYV129 vs. 121VVGGLGG127, and 118AGAVV122 vs. 120AVVGG124.

  Comparing isolated hPrP106–145 monomers, dimerization significantly enhanced the stability of β-sheet formations around residue 138IIH140 through robust hydrophobic and aromatic inter-peptide contacts. Although residues within the glycine-rich region (120AVV122 and 128YVL130) also exhibited a strong propensity for inter-peptide interactions, the resulting β-sheet formations in this region remained highly dynamic—similar to those observed in the monomeric state—likely due to the inherent flexibility conferred by glycine’s minimal steric hindrance. Consequently, hydrophobic and aromatic residues in the glycine-rich region readily collapsed into transient intra- or inter-peptide β-sheets. Notably, the average number of inter-peptide contacts for residues 120AVV122 and 128YVL130 was comparable to their intra-peptide contacts, suggesting that the structural frustration in this region was relatively low. Moreover, interactions between residues 120AVV122 and 128YVL130 formed intra-peptide β-hairpins that were also involved in similar inter-peptide β-sheet contacts. Both regions demonstrated a strong tendency to self-associate and form inter-peptide anti-parallel β-sheets. Dynamic β-sheets integrating residues 120AVV122 or 128YVL130 with 138IIH140 were observed in both monomers and dimers. Ramachandran plot analysis of glycine residues (Figures S4 and S5) revealed broad φ/ψ distributions for G123, G124, G126, G127, and G131 in both monomers and dimers, reflecting their intrinsic flexibility. Upon dimerization, a slight increase in density was observed in the β-sheet–favored region (+135° < φ < +180°, −180° < ψ < −135°), indicating a shift toward β-structures69, 70. In contrast, G114 and G119 in the alanine-rich region favored helical angles (−89° < φ < −39°, −66° < ψ < −16°), consistent with secondary structure analysis. These findings suggest that the flexible glycine-rich region acted as a soft molecular glue, driving hPrP106–145 aggregation into β-sheet–rich structures during early nucleation via hydrophobic and aromatic interactions. This behavior mirrors observations in suckerin peptides, where glycine-rich regions similarly promoted β-sheet aggregation in vitro71 and in silico49, and aligns with studies showing that such regions can lead to locally condensed phases lacking stable structure, as seen in the LLPS of FUS63, 66, HNRNPG64, and TDP4372. Overall, our results indicate that the glycine-rich region plays a pivotal role in facilitating the aggregation of hPrP106–145 into β-sheet–rich assemblies.


## Conclusions
  The hPrP106–145 is essential for prion aggregation16–18 and infectivity26, 27, yet its atomistic conformational dynamics remain poorly understood. Here, we present the first systematic analysis of its monomeric and dimeric states using long-timescale DMD simulations. The hPrP106–145 monomer predominantly adopted a helical conformation in the alanine-rich region (residues 109–118), while the remaining sequence was largely unstructured, exhibiting dynamic β-sheet formations centered around residues 120AVV122, 128YVL130, and 138IIH140. Upon dimerization, β-sheet formation was significantly enhanced, particularly around 138IIH140, which displayed the highest β-sheet propensity and inter-peptide contact frequency, underscoring its crucial role in dimer stability. A key finding of this study was the role of the glycine-rich region (residues 119GAVVGGLGGYVLG131) in facilitating hPrP106–145 aggregation. The inherent flexibility conferred by glycine’s minimal steric hindrance allowed the surrounding hydrophobic and aromatic residues to dynamically collapse, forming transient intra- and inter-peptide β-sheets. These flexible interactions acted as a molecular glue, promoting aggregation while maintaining structural adaptability. Although β-sheet formation in this region lowered potential energy, excessive β-sheet content led to significant entropic loss, indicating a trade-off between stability and conformational entropy. Although the cross-β-sheet core of full-length hPrP fibrils involves residues 94–22512, 14, 15, the hPrP monomer predominantly adopts buried helical conformations in residues 144–228, with the glycine-rich segment (residues 119–131) remaining solvent-exposed and structurally dynamic34. We hypothesize that this flexible region acts as a soft molecular glue, facilitating early fibrillization of full-length hPrP. This hypothesis provides a theoretical explanation for why infectious fibrils span residues ~90–230, while non-infectious fibrils typically initiate near residue 160. Additionally, targeting the glycine-rich region with specific antibodies to inhibit aggregation may offer a promising strategy for combating PrP-related diseases. For example, previous studies have shown that targeting the glycine-rich domain of TDP-43 with antibodies prevents its aggregation in vitro and reduces neurofilament levels in vivo73. This mechanistic insight not only contributes to our understanding of the pathological aggregation of hPrP but also provides a foundation for computational studies on the phase separation behavior of glycine-rich amyloid peptides64.
