# Computational insights into the cross-talk between medin and Aβ: implications for age-related vascular risk factors in Alzheimer’s disease


## Abstract
  The aggregation of medin forming aortic medial amyloid is linked to arterial wall degeneration and cerebrovascular dysfunction. Elevated levels of arteriolar medin are correlated with an increased presence of vascular amyloid-β (Aβ) aggregates, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and vascular dementia. The cross-interaction between medin and Aβ results in the formation of heterologous fibrils through co-aggregation and cross-seeding processes both in vitro and in vivo. However, a comprehensive molecular understanding of the cross-interaction between medin and Aβ—two intrinsically disordered proteins—is critically lacking. Here, we employed atomistic discrete molecular dynamics simulations to systematically investigate the self-association, co-aggregation and also the phenomenon of cross-seeding between these two proteins. Our results demonstrated that both Aβ and medin were aggregation prone and their mixture tended to form β-sheet-rich hetero-aggregates. The formation of Aβ-medin hetero-aggregates did not hinder Aβ and medin from recruiting additional Aβ and medin peptides to grow into larger β-sheet-rich aggregates. The β-barrel oligomer intermediates observed in the self-aggregations of Aβ and medin were also present during their co-aggregation. In cross-seeding simulations, preformed Aβ fibrils could recruit isolated medin monomers to form elongated β-sheets. Overall, our comprehensive simulations suggested that the cross-interaction between Aβ and medin may contribute to their pathological aggregation, given the inherent amyloidogenic tendencies of both medin and Aβ. Targeting medin, therefore, could offer a novel therapeutic approach to preserving brain function during aging and AD by improving vascular health.


## INTRODUCTION
  The abnormal misfolding and aggregation of amyloid proteins, leading to the formation of insoluble fibril deposits within specific human organs or tissues, have garnered significant research attention due to their association with a broad spectrum of degenerative diseases [1, 2]. Currently, approximately 50 different proteins or peptides are known to assemble into amyloid fibrils associated with various human diseases [3], including the aggregation of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the presence of α-synuclein Lewy bodies in Parkinson’s disease (PD) and the localized deposition of medin in aortic medial amyloidosis (AMA) [3, 4]. While a specific amyloidosis disease is typically pathologically associated with one or two particular amyloid peptides or proteins [2, 3], it is not uncommon in clinical practice to observe the simultaneous presence of different amyloid disease–related peptides within the same tissues or organs [5, 6]. This co-occurrence can lead to the simultaneous onset of multiple amyloid diseases in the same individual (e.g. AD alongside PD [7] or AMA alongside AD [8]), consequently elevating the risk of heightened disease severity and progression. Age is the most significant independent risk factor for both AD and AMA, yet it is associated with distinct amyloid peptides in each condition. As the risk of AD rises with age-induced vascular risk factors, cerebral arteriole medin has been characterized as a novel risk factor or biomarker for AD and vascular dementia (VaD) [8–10]. A recent study uncovered that medin’s direct interaction with Aβ enhances their amyloid aggregation, suggesting a possible involvement in age- and AD-related cognitive decline, whether independently or in conjunction with brain vascular Aβ pathology [11, 12]. Therefore, thoroughly exploring how medin interacts with Aβ and impacts their pathological aggregation is crucial for understanding the co-occurrence of AMA and AD, providing valuable insights for future therapeutic development.

  Medin is a 50-amino-acid peptide derived from the protein milk fat globule EGF-like factor-8 (MFG-E8, also known as lactadherin) and is among the most prevalent human amyloids discovered to date [4, 10]. For example, medin amyloid aggregates are present in the vasculature of nearly all individuals over 50 years of age [13]. A growing body of evidence suggests that medin aggregation contributes to the age-related decline in vascular elasticity, with prefibrillar intermediates of medin potentially playing a role in sporadic thoracic aortic aneurysm pathogenesis [14, 15]. Deletion of the MFG-E8’s C2 domain, which contains medin, eliminated medin aggregates and restored age-related arterial dysfunction in a transgenic mouse model [15]. The characterization of medin aggregation species from monomers to oligomers and fibrils including their structures and conversion dynamics is still critically lacking, both experimentally and computationally. Previous circular dichroism (CD) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements indicated that medin monomers are primarily composed of random coil and β-sheet structures, with relatively weak helical conformations [16–18]. Medin oligomers, formed during the early stages of aggregation, display a β-sheet content higher than that of medin monomers but smaller than that of medin fibrils [17]. These oligomers have been linked to the pathogenesis of aortic aneurysm and VaD [17, 19]. For instance, it has been suggested that the formation of β-barrel intermediates, which induce membrane disruption and alter cellular homeostasis, may contribute to medin’s involvement in vascular pathology [19]. Aggregation assays with truncated medin peptides have identified the amyloid-prone region within the final 18–19 C-terminal amino acids [20].

  Aβ is an amyloidogenic peptide ranging from 39 to 43 residues, and it forms extracellular amyloid plaques, a hallmark in the brain of AD [2]. The length of Aβ significantly impacts its physical properties, with Aβ42 and Aβ43 being particularly amyloidogenic. Among them, Aβ40 and Aβ42 are the most prevalent species found in AD pathology [21]. Aβ monomers in solution primarily adopt random coil and partial β-sheet structures, as determined by previous nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), circular dichroism (CD) experiments and computational simulations [22–25]. Upon oligomerization, Aβ peptides undergo aggregation into β-sheet-rich oligomers and potentially toxic β-barrel intermediates [26, 27]. Reducing the population of β-sheet-rich oligomers through inhibiting or promoting Aβ fibrillization can effectively mitigate the cytotoxicity of Aβ, underscoring that soluble β-sheet-rich oligomers are the primary toxic entities [28]. Aβ fibrils exhibit polymorphism but share a common cross-β structure with in-registered parallel β-strands within each proto-filament, aligned perpendicular to the fibril axis and packed against each other, forming a cross-β core [1, 29, 30]. The experimentally characterized Aβ fibrils predominantly featured β-strands in the vicinity of residues 11–20 and 31–40 [29–31].

  The self-aggregation kinetics of medin and Aβ exhibit a shared sigmoidal curve in Thioflavin T (ThT) affinity assays. Medin at ~15 μM exhibits notably faster aggregation kinetics in vitro than Aβ40 at a higher concentration of ~20 μM [12]. Interestingly, when their mixture co-aggregated, the process occurs at a considerably accelerated rate compared to Aβ40 alone in vitro [12]. Comparatively higher levels of medin are detected in the brain parenchymal arteries of individuals with AD and VaD, in contrast to dementia-free control subjects [11]. In vivo, co-aggregation of medin and Aβ is also observed in the mouse model. Furthermore, preformed medin fibrils can act as seeds to accelerate Aβ aggregation both in vitro and in vivo [11, 12]. Hence, the interplay between medin and Aβ, involving co-aggregation and cross-seeding, is suggested as a potential pivotal factor contributing to the onset of AD and VaD. Medin emerges as a prospective therapeutic target for preventing vascular damage and cognitive decline associated with Aβ deposition in cerebral blood vessels [11, 12]. However, the molecular understanding of the inter-molecular interactions and the conformational dynamics governing the interplay between medin and Aβ is yet to be established, a crucial prerequisite for the design of targeted therapeutic strategies.

  To investigate the interplay between Aβ and medin in their amyloid aggregation, we computationally invested the folding of Aβ and medin monomers, their homo- and hetero-dimerization, along the formation of hetero-tetramers by two Aβ and two medin using atomistic discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) simulations, a rapid and accurate molecular dynamics method to study protein folding and aggregation [32–34]. Our findings indicated that both Aβ and medin monomers primarily adopted unstructured and β-sheet conformations, with medin’s β-sheet conformations showing greater stability than Aβ’s. Individual dimerization simulations for both two Aβ and two medin revealed that both Aβ and medin were aggregation prone, readily aggregating into β-sheet-rich homodimers by forming extensive inter-peptide backbone hydrogen bonds. Hetero-dimerization of one Aβ with one medin also co-aggregated into β-sheet-rich heterodimers, displaying similar self-assembly dynamics to their individual homodimerization. Furthermore, the formation of Aβ-medin hetero-aggregates did not impede Aβ and medin from recruiting additional Aβ and medin peptides to grow into larger β-sheet-rich aggregates. The potentially toxic β-barrel oligomers [35, 36], as observed in the individual aggregations of Aβ and medin [19, 26, 27, 33], were also present in their co-aggregated hetero-complexes. Apart from the co-aggregation of Aβ and medin, we also directly observed their cross-seeding in the simulations in which preformed Aβ fibrils recruited isolated medin monomers to form elongated β-sheet. Overall, our comprehensive simulations suggested that the interplay between Aβ and medin, involving co-aggregation and cross-seeding, may contribute to their pathological aggregation, shedding light on the co-occurrence of AMA and AD. Hence, targeting medin might offer a novel therapeutic approach to preserving brain function during aging and AD by enhancing vascular health.


## METHODS AND MATERIALS

### Molecular systems
  The amino acid sequences of human medin and Aβ42 (referred to as Aβ in this study) used in our simulations are depicted in Figure 1A, which has been widely employed for studying the amyloid aggregation of medin [12, 15, 19] and Aβ [26, 29, 37]. Since the solution structure of the medin monomer is not yet determined, we initiated the medin monomer simulations with a fully extended conformation. The initial structure of Aβ used in our simulations was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB: 1z0q), derived from an NMR study in an aqueous medium [38]. To investigate the conformational dynamics of medin and Aβ monomers and ensure sufficient conformational sampling, we conducted 30 independent DMD simulations for each monomer starting from randomized velocities. Each independent simulation lasted up to 600 ns to minimize potential bias from initial states. The representative centroid structures of the top 10 most populated conformations of the medin monomer, accounting for approximately 45.7% of the saturated conformations, were employed as the initial medin structures for the subsequent homo- and hetero-oligomerization, as well as cross-seeding simulations. To avoid potential bias linked to using the same medin structure in all simulations, the initial medin structure for each independent trajectory in the multiple peptide simulations was randomly selected from the 10 most populated conformations.

  To investigate the conformational dynamics during the self-aggregation and co-aggregation of medin and Aβ, we conducted simulations of four different systems, containing two medin, two Aβ, one medin with one Aβ and two medin together with two Aβ peptides. In these multi-peptide simulations, the composite medin and Aβ peptides were initially positioned randomly within a cubic simulation box, adopting different orientations, while maintaining a minimum intermolecular atomic distance greater than 1.5 nm. For each molecular system, we conducted 50 independent DMD simulations commencing from distinct initial coordinates and velocities. Furthermore, we explored cross-seeding between medin and Aβ through simulations in which an isolated medin monomer interacted with a preformed Aβ fibril comprised of 20 peptides (PDB: 5oqv [29]). Although investigating medin fibril seeding Aβ was of great interest, the structures of medin fibrils are yet to be determined. As an alternative approach, we opted for Aβ fibril seeding on the medin monomer. The initial structure of the medin monomer was randomly chosen from the top 10 representative centroid structures among the most populated conformations obtained in our monomer simulations. Further information regarding simulations of each molecular system can be found in Table 1.


### DMD simulations
  All simulations were conducted using atomic DMD [34, 39, 40] with an EEF1 implicit solvent model [41] at a temperature of 300 K. DMD is a fast and predictive molecular dynamics algorithm that employs optimized stepwise functions to model continuous potential functions, akin to traditional MD simulations [32, 34, 40]. The step function potentials used in these simulations were adapted from the Medusa force field, which has been extensively benchmarked and proven accurate in predicting protein stability changes upon mutation [42], protein–ligand binding affinity [42, 43] and ab initio protein folding [32]. Similar to most traditional MD force fields, the Medusa force field takes into account both bonded interactions (such as covalent bonds, bond angles and dihedrals) and non-bonded interactions (including van der Waals forces, solvation effects, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions). The hydrogen bond was explicitly modeled by a reaction-like algorithm [32, 44]. The screened electrostatic interactions between charged atoms were computed by the Debye–Hückel approximation with the Debye length assigned of ∼10 Å, which corresponds to ~100 mM of NaCl under physiological conditions. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three directions. The DMD software is accessible to academic researchers through Molecules In Action, LLC (www.moleculesinaction.com). In our simulations, we utilized units of mass, time, length and energy, with values of 1 Da, approximately 50 fs, 1 Å and 1 kcal/mol, respectively. Given its swift computational speed and improved sampling efficiency, DMD has found widespread use among our research team [45–47] and others in the scientific community [48–50] for studying protein folding and aggregation.


### Analysis methods
  Secondary structure analyses were conducted using the Dictionary Secondary Structure of Protein (DSSP) method [51]. Hydrogen bond formation was identified when the N···O distance was less than 3.5 Å and the N–H···O angle exceeded 150°. Additionally, pairwise residue contacts were defined when the heavy atoms of two non-sequential sidechains or main chains had their interatomic distances within 0.65 nm. Cluster analysis for medin and Aβ monomers was conducted employing the Daura algorithm [52] with a backbone atom deviation cutoff of 0.55 nm. To create a two-dimensional (2D) free energy surface, also known as the potential mean force (PMF) surface, we utilized the formula −RT ln P(x, y), where P(x, y) represents the probability of a conformation having specific parameter values of x and y.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### Conformational dynamics of Aβ and medin monomers
  The conformational dynamics of Aβ and medin monomers were explored through 30 independent 600 ns DMD simulations. Prior to data analysis, we performed equilibrium and convergence assessments for each molecular system. This involved examining the temporal evolutions and probability distribution functions within two distinct time intervals for various parameters, including the radius of gyration (Rg), the count of backbone hydrogen bonds, heavy atom contacts and the composition of secondary structure elements (Figures S1–S4 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). As demonstrated in three representative trajectories selected from the pool of 30 independent DMD simulations, both molecular systems achieved steady states during the final 300 ns, exhibiting fluctuations without significant shifting trends in the aforementioned structural parameters (Figures S1 and S3 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). The distribution of all these structural parameters from the ensemble of 30 independent DMD simulations was remarkably similar for both systems (Figures S2 and S4 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/), signifying reasonable convergence in both Aβ and medin monomer simulations. The conformational dynamics of Aβ and medin monomers were monitored by examining the time evolution of the secondary structure (Figure 1B and C, Figures S1 and S4 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Both Aβ and medin exhibited dynamic β-sheet conformations, but the β-sheet conformation in medin monomers was more stable with longer dwell times compared to that in Aβ monomers.

  The analysis of averaged secondary structures revealed that both Aβ and medin monomers primarily adopted unstructured coil and β-sheet conformations (Figure 1D and F), yet they displayed distinct conformational profiles (Figure 1E and G). Specifically, the probability distribution of β-sheet content for each Aβ monomer peaked in the range of 0–60%, while the peak for medin monomers was in the range of 50–70% (Figure 1E and G). The β-sheets in Aβ monomers were primarily formed by segments Aβ10–21 and Aβ29–41 (Figure 1H). Segment Aβ1–9 also exhibited weak β-sheet conformations but predominantly adopted unstructured conformations. Prior NMR measurements combined with MD simulations have also revealed that Aβ monomers predominantly adopt unstructured conformations with dynamic β-sheet structures [53, 54]. The β-sheet-populated regions within Aβ monomers in our simulations (Figure 1H) were also consistent with findings from previous all-atom replica exchange molecular dynamics (REMD) simulations, utilizing diverse force fields, including OPLS-AA, AMBER99sb-ILDN and CHARMM22* [55]. In addition, these secondary structure propensities are consistent with those observed in experimentally characterized Aβ fibril models [29–31] and previous MD simulations [20, 26]. The medin monomer exhibited four β-sheet regions around residues 7–16, 19–29, 31–40 and 42–49, with a notably stronger β-sheet tendency in the latter three segments (Figure 1I), consistent with prior NMR measurements [16]. Davies et al., utilizing ab initio protein modeling, 13C-direct detection NMR measurements and conventional MD simulations, demonstrated that monomeric medin comprises a stable core of three β-strands [16]. In addition to β-sheet, residues 1–18 also displayed unstructured and helix conformations, which was also consistent with prior NMR and CD measurements [16–18]. The top four most populated conformations of both Aβ and medin monomers were mainly composed of β-sheet structures, but constituted a much higher proportion of total conformations for medin (~33.94%) than Aβ (~11.46%) (Figure 1J and K). This difference suggested that Aβ exhibited a more diverse range of conformations while medin’s conformational ensemble was more centralized, consistent with their distinct structural stabilities (Figure 1B and C).


### Homo- and hetero-dimerization dynamics of Aβ and medin
  To investigate the influence of cross-interactions between Aβ and medin on their aggregation, we conducted dimerization simulations involving two Aβ, two medin and one medin mixed with one Aβ42. Each molecular system underwent 50 independent DMD trajectories, each lasting 600 ns. Analyzing the time evolution of structural parameters, including the radius of gyration, the count of inter-peptide hydrogen bonds and contacts, as well as the composition of secondary structures, for three representative trajectories out of the 50 independent DMD simulations, along with considering the probability distributions of these structural parameters within two distinct time intervals for all independent DMD simulations (Figures S5–S10 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/), we observed that all three molecular systems reached equilibrium during the final 300 ns. The significant conformational changes of each peptide in the saturated states, corresponding to its initial structure, were examined through the backbone atoms root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis (Figure S11 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/), indicating that potential biases from initial states were well avoided. The analysis of dimerization dynamics, involving the monitoring of changes in secondary structure, inter-peptide hydrogen bonds, contacts and snapshots at specific simulation times (Figure 2A and C, Figures S5 and S7 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/), revealed that both Aβ and medin readily formed stable homodimers characterized by β-sheet-rich structures. Furthermore, the assessment of dimerization free energy projected as a function of the radius of gyration and inter-peptide main-chain hydrogen bonds (indicative of inter-chain β-sheets) indicated that the formation of inter-peptide β-sheet-rich conformations was more favorable than isolated monomer conformations (Figure 2B and D). Overall, the dynamics of homo-dimerization and the analysis of the free energy landscape suggested that both Aβ and medin, as amyloidogenic peptides, exhibited a preference for adopting β-sheet-rich aggregated states.

  The analysis of simulations with one Aβ mixed with one medin revealed that these two peptides spontaneously co-aggregated to β-sheet-rich heterodimers (Figure 2E and Figure S9 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Much like the Aβ-Aβ and medin-medin homo-dimerization simulations, the free energy landscape of Aβ-medin hetero-dimerization suggested that the formation of inter-peptide β-sheets were also favorable, as the lowest energy basin was rich in inter-peptide main-chain hydrogen bonds (Figure 2F). The Aβ-medin hetero-dimerization revealed that the cross-interactions between Aβ and medin led to their aggregation into β-sheet-rich heterodimers, mirroring their individual self-interactions that resulted in the formation of β-sheet-rich homo-dimers. Whether through individual homo-dimerization or mixed hetero-dimerization of Aβ and medin, the saturated aggregates consistently exhibited a noticeable number of inter-peptide contacts and backbone hydrogen bonds (Figure S12 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/), indicating that either of the peptides could interact with themselves or with each other, forming β-sheet-rich oligomers.

  The average secondary structure content of Aβ and medin in their respective homodimers, mixed heterodimers, and isolated monomer indicated that both homo- and hetero-dimerization enhanced the β-sheet conformations compared to their isolated monomers (Figure S13 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). For instance, in both the homodimers and heterodimers, the β-sheet content of Aβ and medin exceeded that of their corresponding isolated monomers. The analysis of average secondary structure propensity per residue (Figure 3A and B) revealed that the β-sheet-dominant regions of Aβ and medin in their respective homodimers also exhibited a similar β-sheet propensity in their mixed heterodimers. Specifically, dimerization of Aβ either with itself or with medin promoted the β-sheet formation in residues 10–21 and 30–41 from unstructured conformations. Residues 10–21 and 30–41 of Aβ exhibited significant β-sheet propensities in the Aβ monomers and homodimers, along with the heterodimer of Aβ mixing with medin. Interestingly, prior studies also showed that the fragments of Aβ16–22 [56, 57], Aβ29–42 [58, 59], Aβ31–40 [60] and Aβ33–42 [60–62] could independently self-assemble into β-sheet aggregates in silico and in vitro. Meanwhile, similar β-sheet regions around residues 16–21 and 29–32 were also observed in the simulations for the aggregation of Aβ15–35 segments [63]. In addition, the isolated fragment of Aβ21–30 mainly assumed unstructured and helical conformations in a prior simulation study [64], consistent with the relatively weak β-sheet tendency around this region in the full-length Aβ monomers and dimers from our simulation.

  In the case of medin, the partial helices of monomers in the N-terminal were converted into β-sheet upon dimerization. The medin generally exhibited four distinct β-sheet regions around residues 7–16, 19–29, 31–40 and 42–49 in the monomers, homodimers and Aβ-medin heterodimers. Previous experiments demonstrated that fragments of medin32–41, medin42–49 and medin31–50 independently formed β-sheet-dominated fibrils [20]. However, fragments of medin1–12, medin14–22, medin16–24 and medin1–25 segments couldn’t aggregate into β-sheet structures [20]. The self-assembly of medin42–49 into β-sheet structures was further supported by electron microscopy, solid-state NMR spectroscopy and X-ray measurements [65, 66]. The presence of medin31–37 was observed to assist medin19–25 in forming β-sheet conformations in a cyclic peptide derived from residues 19–37 of medin [67]. Overall, our simulation results, along with prior experimental studies, indicate that medin31–40 and medin42–49 drove the formation of β-sheet structures in other regions of medin, consistent with previous NMR measurements and simulations [16, 33]. Additionally, Aβ may assist regions of medin with weak β-sheet tendencies in forming β-sheet structures, such as medin7–16.

  The solvent-accessible surface areas (SASA) of Aβ residues 10–21 and 30–41 significantly decreased upon dimerization, leading to the formation of more buried β-sheet structures (Figure 3C and D). Interestingly, hetero-dimerization resulted in the β-sheet regions of Aβ being more buried compared to those in the homodimer (Figure 3C). This difference was because medin had more amino acids than Aβ, and the number of intermolecular contacts in the Aβ-medin heterodimer was greater than in the Aβ-Aβ homodimer (Figure S12A available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Comparing to the isolated medin monomer, dimerization decreased the SASA for residues 10–50 of medin, which mostly belonged to β-sheet regions, due to the formation of intermolecular contacts and hydrogen bonds (Figure 3D). In contrast to the medin homodimer, residues 19–29 and 42–50 became more exposed in the Aβ-medin heterodimer, which may assist in the further aggregation of the heterodimer, especially considering that medin42–50 is known as the strongest amyloidogenic region in medin [20].


### Hot-spots for Aβ and medin self-assembly also drive their co-aggregation
  The conformations of Aβ and medin homodimers and their hetero-dimers were further examined by computing the residue-wise contact frequency map among main-chain atoms (Figure 4 and Figure S14 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Based on the intra-peptide contact frequency maps, the structural elements observed in Aβ and medin within their respective homodimers were also evident in the Aβ-medin heterodimers (e.g. the contact patterns in the contact map along with corresponding structures in Figure S14A and B available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). For example, Aβ exhibited an obvious β-hairpin motif with β-strands around residues 10–21 and 30–41, alongside two less populated β-hairpin motifs formed by residues 1–16 and 28–42, in both the Aβ homodimer and Aβ-medin heterodimer (Figure S14A available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Medin in either homodimers or heterodimers displayed similar diverse intra-chain β-sheet contact patterns, including β-hairpins between residues 9–16 and 19–26, between residues 20–28 and 31–39 and between residues 32–39 and 43–50, as well as the parallel β-sheet between residues 19–23 and 45–50 (Figure S14B available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/).

  The analysis of inter-peptide contact frequency maps of Aβ-Aβ homodimers revealed that the fragments of Aβ10–21 and Aβ30–41 interacted either with themselves or with each other, facilitating the formation of inter-peptide β-sheets in Aβ aggregates (Figure S14C available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Similarly, the segments of medin7–16, medin19–29, medin31–40 and medin42–49 not only contributed to the formation of intra-chain β-sheets but also participated in forming inter-peptide β-sheets of medin homodimers (Figure S14D available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). This phenomenon, where the same interactions stabilizing intra-peptide structures also contribute to the formation of inter-peptide structures in their respective homodimers, is also commonly observed in the formation of high-order protein–protein complexes via domain-swap [68–70]. The β-sheet-rich conformations (Figure 2A), along with the corresponding β-sheet regions (Figure 3A) and the hot-spot binding region (Figure S14C available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/) observed in the Aβ homo-dimerization simulations, align with prior atomistic REMD simulations employing Amber99SB-ILDN and Gromos-53A6 force fields [71–73].

  In the Aβ-medin heterodimers, the analysis of inter-peptide contact frequencies among main-chain atoms revealed a strong cross-interaction between Aβ and medin by forming inter-peptide β-sheets. This interaction primarily involved the β-strands of Aβ10–21 and Aβ30–41 interacting with β-strands of medin7–16, medin19–29, medin31–40 and medin42–49 (Figure 4A). The inter-peptide β-sheet contact patterns and the corresponding structural motifs with aligned sequences indicated that the driving force for these β-sheet pairing was the association among hydrophobic residues (Figure 4A). Furthermore, we calculated the average number of inter-peptide contacts formed by heavy atoms for each residue in Aβ and medin within their respective homodimers and heterodimers (Figure 4B and C). Interestingly, Aβ residues 10–21 and 30–41, as well as medin residues 7–16, 19–29, 31–40 and 42–49, showed a substantial number of inter-peptide contacts in their respective homodimers and also exhibited a significant number of inter-peptide contacts in the Aβ-medin heterodimers (Figure 4B and C). These findings suggested that the same interactions driving the self-assembly of Aβ and medin also contributed to their co-aggregation.


### The formation of Aβ-medin hetero-aggregates did not hinder the capability of Aβ and medin to recruit additional Aβ and medin peptides in forming larger β-sheet-rich aggregates
  To further investigate the capability of multiple Aβ and medin peptides in forming larger aggregates than dimers, we conducted an additional set of 50 independent DMD simulations with two Aβ mixed with two medin peptides. Analysis of co-aggregation dynamics revealed that the four peptides spontaneously formed stable β-sheet-rich hetero-complexes (Figure 5A and B and Figure S15 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). No significant conformational changes in the inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds were observed during the last 400 ns, indicating that all simulations reached their saturation states (Figure 5A and B, Figure S15 and S16 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). Examination of the saturated conformations in the representative trajectories demonstrated that two Aβ peptides and two medin peptides could either bind separately or collectively, forming inter-peptide β-sheets stabilized by intermolecular hydrogen bonds and contacts. Conformational analysis showed that hetero-aggregates exhibited a significant number of Aβ-medin inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds, along with an abundance of Aβ-Aβ and medin-medin inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds (Figure 5C). Additionally, we calculated the conformational free energy landscape as a function of the number of Aβ-medin inter-peptide contacts against the inter-chain medin-medin, Aβ-Aβ and medin-Aβ hydrogen bonds for the hetero-complex (Figure 5D). This analysis suggested that interactions involving two Aβ or two medin peptides either separately or collectively with the others were favorable in forming inter-peptide β-sheets, as indicated by the lower free energy of their corresponding main-chain hydrogen bonds. The co-aggregation of one Aβ mixed with one medin, as well as two Aβ mixed with two medin, indicated that the formation of Aβ-medin hetero-aggregates did not hinder their ability to recruit other Aβ and medin peptides to create larger aggregates. Both Aβ and medin were amyloidogenic peptides with the capacity to recruit either themselves or others around their β-sheet ends, allowing them to grow into larger β-sheet oligomers, irrespective of whether they formed homo- or hetero-aggregates.


### The cross-interaction led to the formation of potentially toxic β-barrel hetero-complexes involving Aβ and medin
  With a well-defined structure and alignment with the ‘amyloid-pore’ hypothesis of toxicity [36, 74, 75], the β-barrel oligomer, initially discovered in an αB crystallin fragment [35] and frequently observed in toxic amyloid fragments [76–80] and full-length peptide [26, 33, 47, 81, 82] aggregation simulations, has been proposed as a potentially cytotoxic oligomeric form in amyloid aggregation. For instance, the formation of the β-barrel intermediate has received experimental and computational support for full-length Aβ [26, 27], hIAPP [81, 82] and medin [19, 33]. The wild-type Aβ showed a greater inclination for β-barrel formation compared to the AD-protective A2T substitution but less than the AD-causative mutations D7N and E22G [26]. Introducing the S20G substitution in hIAPP increased its amyloidogenicity and cytotoxicity, resulting in a substantial increase in β-barrel formation compared to the wild-type peptide [81, 82]. The β-barrel formations were exclusively observed in toxic human calcitonin, with no presence in the non-toxic salmon calcitonin [47]. Another recent computational simulation study directly demonstrated that the formation of a β-barrel oligomer led to membrane leakage [83]. Interestingly, β-barrel intermediates were also observed in the aggregation of two medin peptides, one Aβ mixed with one medin, as well as two Aβ mixed with two medin (Figure 6A–D). Our earlier Aβ aggregation simulations demonstrated that β-barrel intermediates only appeared in systems with a number of simulated peptides no less than three [26]. Despite the capability of Aβ to form β-barrel intermediates, no β-barrel intermediates were observed in the Aβ dimers due to the insufficient number of peptides required to reach the minimum size for β-barrel oligomer formation. Overall, our co-aggregation simulations of Aβ and medin mixtures suggested that the cross-interaction between Aβ and medin not only resulted in the formation of β-sheet-rich hetero-aggregates but also led to the formation of potentially toxic β-barrel hetero-complexes.


### Cross-seeding of Aβ fibrils with medin resulted in fibril growth by recruiting medin to bind to the fibril elongation ends and form inter-peptide β-sheets
  The co-localization of Aβ and medin not only led to their co-aggregation but also induced cross-seeding between them [11, 12]. To investigate cross-seeding between Aβ and medin, we conducted an additional 50 independent 800 ns DMD simulations in which a preformed Aβ fibril interacted with one isolated medin monomer. Given the lack of atomistic fibril structures for medin, our investigation focused on the interaction between Aβ fibrils and medin monomers. The binding and conformational dynamics of the medin monomer with a preformed Aβ fibril seed were investigated by examining the time evolution of inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds, the secondary structure of each residue in the medin monomer and corresponding snapshots at early and saturated stages (Figure 7A–C). Due to the differences in initial states in each independent DMD simulation, the isolated medin monomer either initially attached to the lateral surface and then slid to the elongation surface or directly attached to the elongation surface of the Aβ fibril. Eventually, the isolated medin monomer would convert into a β-sheet with the β-strands perpendicular to the fibril growth-axis, stabilized by the main-chain inter-peptide hydrogen bonds, sharing similar structural characteristics within the Aβ fibril [29–31]. The PMF as a function of inter-peptide contacts and backbone hydrogen bonds between medin and the Aβ fibril was further analyzed using data from all 50 independent DMD simulations to capture the entire process (Figure 7D). The entire cross-seeding process featured three distinct low free-energy basins. The first basin was rich in inter-peptide contacts but lacked medin-Aβ hydrogen bonds, corresponding to the initial binding of medin to the lateral surface of the Aβ fibril. The other two basins were abundant in both inter-peptide contacts and hydrogen bonds, indicating the formation of inter-chain β-sheets at the elongation end of the Aβ fibril. Additionally, the conformational basin with more inter-peptide contacts also exhibited a greater number of inter-peptide hydrogen bonds. Conformations with the highest number of hydrogen bonds, indicating that most of the medin residues were interacting with Aβ at the fibril end as β-sheets, featured higher free energy, potentially due to the entropy loss as medin became more ordered (Figure 7D). Residue-wise contact frequency map analysis further confirmed that medin predominantly attached to the elongation edge as the new growing β-sheet of the Aβ fibril, mainly driven by hydrophobic and electronic interactions, as illustrated in the representative binding motifs in Figure 7E. In addition, the positively charged residues K16 of Aβ could also attract the negatively charged medin19–27 segment, binding to the lateral surface of the Aβ fibril. Complementary to the experiments of medin fibrils seeded Aβ aggregation in vitro [11, 12], our results also provide direct evidence that the cross-interaction between Aβ and medin may assist their fibrillization through cross-seeding in silico. Such binding of monomers inducing fibril growth was also commonly recognized as the rapid elongation process [84, 85] in amyloid aggregation. Interestingly, Xi et al. [86] demonstrated that the seeding effects caused by preformed fibril templates, inducing conformational conversion to β-sheet for amyloid peptides, are not very sensitive to the sequence details of the template peptides. Our observation of Aβ fibril cross-seeding medin was in line with the hypothesis proposed by Xi et al. [86]. Additionally, this hypothesis also suggests that the seeding of Aβ by medin fibril templates is expected in silico, as observed in vitro [12].


## CONCLUSION
  Taking into account the co-localization of Aβ and medin in cerebral blood vessels in mouse models and human AD tissue, as well as considering the potential influence of endogenous medin levels on Aβ aggregation [11, 12], we conducted comprehensive atomic DMD simulations to explore the effects of their cross-interaction on pathological aggregation at the molecular level. Our results demonstrated that both Aβ and medin monomers primarily adopted unstructured and β-sheet conformations, with the β-sheet conformations of medin being notably more stable than those of Aβ. The dynamics of dimerization conformations and free-energy landscapes revealed that both peptides were amyloid prone peptides, readily aggregating into β-sheet-rich homodimers by forming inter-peptide hydrogen bonds among their main-chain atoms. Interestingly, when one Aβ molecule was mixed with one medin molecule, they readily co-aggregated into β-sheet-rich heterodimers, displaying similar aggregation dynamics to their individual homodimerization simulations in silico. Moreover, the formation of Aβ-medin hetero-aggregates did not hinder the ability of Aβ and medin to recruit additional isolated Aβ and medin peptides, leading to the growth of larger β-sheet-rich aggregates. The residues responsible for driving the individual self-assembly of Aβ and medin also played a critical role in initiating their co-aggregations. The potentially toxic β-barrel oligomers [35, 36] observed in the individual aggregations of Aβ and medin in prior studies [19, 26, 27, 33] were also present in their co-aggregated hetero-complexes. In addition to the co-aggregation of Aβ and medin, their cross-seeding was also directly observed in the simulation of preformed Aβ fibrils recruiting isolated medin monomers to form elongated β-sheets, complementing the findings from experiments involving medin fibrils seeded with Aβin vitro. Overall, our comprehensive simulations suggest that the cross-talk between Aβ and medin, including their co-aggregation and cross-seeding, could contribute to their pathological aggregation [11, 12], shedding light on the co-occurrence of AMA and AD and offering insights for future therapeutic development.
