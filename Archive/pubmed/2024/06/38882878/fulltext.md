# Herbal Composite Preparation and Investigating its Efficiency to Inhibit Biofilm Formation and Virulence Factors of Prevotella Intermedia and Porphyromonas Gingivalis – Formulation of Mouthwash Using a Herbal Composite and Evaluating its Anti-microbial Activity


## ABSTRACT
  Herbal composite preparation was studied with the aim of inhibiting the virulence factors of two dental pathogens: Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. A novel herbal composite was developed using the herbal extracts of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata. During the study, the following observations were noted. The minimal inhibitory concentration of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composites (WBc) was obtained for the test concentration of 20 μg/ml (16 ± 0.57 mm and 15 ± 0.75 mm of inhibitory zones against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis, respectively). Biofilm inhibition assay results revealed about 0.51 ± 1.25 mg/ml and 0.53 ± 0.57 mg/ml of minimal biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC) against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis, respectively. The effect of WBc on lactic acid production showed that 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml concentrates reduced up to 80% and 70% in Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis, respectively. Formulated herbal mouthwash showed good stability under all three different test conditions (5°C, 25°C, and 40°C) as the color, odor, phase separation, and homogeneity were not changed for the period of 3 months. The anti-bacterial activity of formulated mouthwash (30 μg/ml) exhibited maximum inhibitory zones of about 18 ± 0.75 mm and 19 ± 1.05 mm against the respective test bacteria – Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. Amplification of mfa1 and clpB genes showed 246 bp and 294 bp fragments of P. gingivalis and 238 bp and 280 bp fragments of P. intermedia during agarose electrophoretic analysis. The docking report revealed -5.84 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found three hydrogen bonding between the quercetin and target protein, mfa1 of Porphyromonas gingivalis. The target protein, clpB of Prevotella intermedia, and quercetin had -6.72 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found four hydrogen bonds between them. The developed composite could be optimized in future to develop a novel and biocompatible herbal mouthwash for the prevention of different dental caries and gingival inflammation associated with dental biofilm formation.


## INTRODUCTION
  More than 415 bacterial species were reported to be present in subgingival biofilm and can have a role in periodontal infections. Most of these species are considered as strong biofilm producers and are highly associated with different types of dental infections (Paster et al., 2001).[1] Mättö et al. (1997)[2] highlighted the role of some of these species, like Porphyromonas gingivalis, Prevotella nigrescens, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, and Bacteroides forsythus. Carrasco et al. (1999)[3] emphasized the ability of several bacteria to gain resistance against different types of antibiotics (beta-lactams, fluoroquinolone, nitroimidazoles, cephalosporins, etc.) that are used against dental pathogens.

  Different mouth rinses containing chlorhexidine gluconate as the major component are used to control dental biofilm. However, repeated use of this compound sometimes causes tooth stains and loss of taste sensation and may lead to the development of bacterial resistance.[45] Hence, development of alternative biologically active antibacterial natural agents is necessary, which could act synergistically against dental biofilm that causes dental caries and periodontal diseases.[6] Chandra Shekar et al. (2015)[7] described the need for herbal medicines as an alternative to chemical antibacterial agents. Active ingredients in plant medicines have the ability to restore health, with minimal adverse effects and maximum efficiency. Moreover, they are alcohol- and sugar-free. Herbs, either alone or in combination, were proven to be safe and effective against dental caries, gingival bleeding and mouth ulcers.[7] Kukreja and Dodwad (2012)[8] reported that natural mouthwashes could be formulated easily and also considered safe as they are plant-based natural products.

  In hospitalized patients with periodontal disorders, Naiktari et al. (2014)[9] compared the effectiveness of triphala mouthwash with that of 0.2% chlorhexidine (CHX). The triphala mouthwash was found to be an effective antiplaque agent, like 0.2% CHX. Hence, it greatly helps control periodontal disorders by lowering plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation.

  Sedigh-Rahimabadi et al. (2017)[10] evaluated Punica granatum var pleniflora for controlling gingivitis among diabetic patients. This study demonstrated that the use of conventional herbal P. granatum var pleniflora (Golnar) mouthwash is a safe and effective modality in the treatment of gingivitis in diabetic patients.

  Based on these different herbal mouthwashes formulated and tested in patients, in our present study, we aimed to develop a herbal composite using two different herbal extracts (Bauhinia variegata + Wrightia tinctoria).

  Bauhinia variegata Linn. (Leguminosae) bark in indigenous medicine is used as a tonic and in the treatment of wounds and ulcers.[11] This herb is also employed in folk medicine to treat a variety of ailments, such as inflammatory illnesses.[12] Twigs of these trees are reported to be used as natural toothbrushes and to reduce toothache (Kadhirvel et al., 2010).[13] Lectins from Bauhinia variegata had attained biomedical and biotechnical attention for the prevention of early adhesion and biofilm formation by different types of bacteria at the orodental sites.[14]

  Wrightia tinctoria R.Br. (family: Apocynaceae), sometimes known as “Indrajau,” is found all over the world and is particularly common in India. Wrightia is named after a Scottish botanist and physician, William Wright (1740–1827). Ethnomedically, this plant’s bark is chewed to heal toothaches brought on by dental caries and is also used as a galactagogue to ease gastrointestinal pain.[15] Filling the tooth cavity with freshly crushed plant extracts of its leaves and roots is reported to be used for relief of dental pain.[16]

  Our literature search showed a lack of scientific research work on these two herbs for the treatment of any dental infections or inflammations. Hence, for the first time, these herbs were developed as a herbal composite and the following objectives were framed.

  This study was carried over to study the extraction protocol of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata, to develop a novel herbal composite using the extracts of selected plants, to demonstrate the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) and biofilm inhibition assay of the herbal composite, to investigate the virulence gene expression of Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis using quantitative reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), to formulate and develop a mouthwash, and to determine the molecular docking of the plant compound (quercetin) and virulence gene factors (mfa1and clpB) of test bacteria.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Procurement of Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis
  Test organisms highly significant with etiological characteristics associated with periodontitis were selected for the study. The selected bacteria (Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis) are presented in Figure 1a.


### Procurement and extraction of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata (Lin et al., 2012)[17]
  Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata leaf powders were collected from the farmhouse, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India [Figures 1b and c]. Using ethanol, solvent extractions of the leaf powders were carried out using a standard Soxhlet extraction apparatus. Collected extracts were transferred to a Petri dish separately and dried in an oven at 50°C; the extracts were scrapped and kept in air-tight brown amber containers at room temperature.


### Herbal composite preparation using Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata (Rafiqul Islam et al., 2015)[18]
  Using the 1 ml (100 μg/ml) extracts of both plants, the herbal composite [Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composite (WBc)] was developed. Briefly, the Wrightia tinctoria extracts were stirred using a magnetic stirrer at 180 revolutions per minute and 40 degrees Celsius in a beaker. Bauhinia variegata extracts were then added drop-by-drop to the previously mentioned extract at a rate of 1 ml per minute. For 2 hours, the magnetic stirring condition was maintained until the composite had fully developed. Prior to antimicrobial activity and other tests, developed composites were kept in brown amber bottles at a cold temperature.


### MIC of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composites (WBc) (Al-Madi et al., 2019)[19]
  The MIC of developed WBc was determined against the test organisms (Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis) using the well diffusion method. A sterile nutrient broth [(g/L) beef extract: 3 g, peptone: 5 g, sodium chloride: 5 g, yeast extract: 5 g, final pH 7.0 ± 0.2)] was used to inoculate all the test cultures, and they were then given 24 to 48 hours to proliferate. Plates of sterile Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA) were made and allowed to set. Swabs were used to evenly spread 0.1% inoculum suspensions of the test organism over the agar surface in each case. On the agar surface of each plate, 6 mm wells were cut while maintaining sterility. Developed WBc fractions containing 10 μg/ml, 20 μg/ml, and 30 μg/ml were added to the wells, whereas 4 μg/ml of streptomycin was added as a standard. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a negative control. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. The MIC of WBc was determined to be the lowest dilution that expressed significant inhibitory zones against the test bacteria.


### Biofilm inhibition assay of WBc (O’Toole et al., 2011)[20]
  The biofilm inhibition efficacy of the developed WBc was analyzed using a modified micro-dilution technique described by O’Toole et al., (2011).[20] The color shift in the microplate wells was used to calculate the minimal biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC); the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent necessary to remove biofilm was assessed.


### Effect of WBc on lactic acid production (Rudin et al., 2023)[21]
  The effect of the herbal composite (WBc) on P. gingivalis and P. intermedia lactic acid production was evaluated for 50 μg/ml, 100 μg/ml, 200 μg/ml, and 400 μg/ml. Each culture was harvested and given two washes in phosphate-buffered saline (OD600 = 0.5–0.6). In a 24-well plate, cells were resuspended (1:10) in buffered peptone water enhanced with 0.2% sucrose. WBc in DMSO was serially diluted twice before being applied to the wells. A DMSO-supplemented bacterial suspension was utilized as a negative control. For 2 hours, bacteria were incubated. Following the removal of the cells by centrifugation (8,000 g, 5 min, 4°C), the supernatant was used to measure the level of lactic acid in the sample using a lactate assay kit from Sigma-Aldrich. The measurements were performed and recorded as graphical values.


### Formulation of herbal mouthwash (Pascual et al., 2023)[22]
  The herbal mouthwash was prepared using the developed WBc with other natural ingredients. In Table A, the formulation of herbal mouthwash is presented. Sodium benzoate (food grade, Sigma, Bangalore, India) was used as an osmolytic preservative in the formulation; liquorice as a sweetener, cinnamon as a natural flavoring agent, and neem as a natural preservative were also used in the formulation.

  The developed mouthwash was tested to determine its pH and stability in different conditions. With the aid of a digital pH meter, the pH of the prepared herbal mouthwash was determined. A standard buffer solution was used to calibrate the pH meter. The mouthwash was weighed and diluted in 50 cc of distilled water, and its pH was measured.[23]

  Accelerated stability tests, which subject the product to elevated temperatures in accordance with ICH rules, are used as a generic technique for forecasting the stability of any product (ICH, 2003).[24] The color and odor were also examined physically and visually during this stability testing. For the developed formulation of mouthwash, a 3-month stability study was conducted. The samples were kept at 3–5°C and 25°C with a 60% relative humidity and 40°C with a 2% relative humidity.[25] The homogeneity, phase separation, color, and odor were evaluated under the above conditions for the specified period.


### Antibacterial activity of WBc against test bacteria (Khan and Kour, 2016)[26]
  Developed WBc was determined against the test organisms (Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis) using the agar well diffusion method. A sterile nutrient broth[(g/L) beef extract: 3 g, peptone: 5 g, sodium chloride: 5 g, yeast extract: 5 g, final pH 7.0 ± 0.2)] was used to inoculate all the test cultures, and they were then given 24 to 48 hours to proliferate. Plates of sterile Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA) were made and allowed to set. Swabs were used to evenly spread 0.1% inoculum suspensions of the test organism over the agar surface in each case. On the agar surface of each plate, 6 mm wells were cut while maintaining sterility. About 20 μl of composite fractions containing 10 μg/ml, 20 μg/ml, and 30 μg/ml was added to each well, whereas 4 μg/ml of streptomycin was used as a standard, and DMSO was used as a negative control. After that, the plates were incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. In all the infected MHA plates, the antimicrobial activity was measured in terms of the zone of inhibition surrounding the wells. The millimeter measurements and records of the inhibition clear zones were made.


### Detection of virulent genes of test organisms using PCR analysis (Arciola et al., 2002)[27]

#### Culturing and DNA extraction from test bacteria
  The test organisms, P. gingivalis and P. intermedia, cultured and the genetic materials were extracted using the method described by Arciola et al. (2002).[2728]


#### PCR amplification of target genes
  PCR reactions were performed using the modified method of Arciola et al. (2002).[27] The pairs of primers were designed for the amplification of the mfa1 and clpB genes separately. Primers specific for mfa1 and clpB genes were picked on the gene sequences by the Primer-3 (Version 7.0) software program. The primers were synthesized by Chromous Biotech, Bangalore, India. In Table B, the forward and reverse primers for each target genes are presented.


### Molecular docking of quercetin, the bioactive compound of the developed herbal composite with target genes of test organisms
  Quercetin, the bioactive compound of the developed herbal composite (WBc), was selected from the repository software and Pubchem database, and the binding energy was investigated after docking with target genes in each test organism. In Table C, the target genes and the respective test bacteria are presented.

  Different sets of software for obtaining molecular docking images were used in the study. The following are the repository software and Pubchem database details for obtaining molecular docking images: PCD, PubChem, ACD/ChemSketch Freeware, Open Babel, Molinspiration, Auto Dock, and Discovery Studio Visualizer were used to sketch the molecular docking images.


## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### MIC of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composites
  The MIC of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composites was determined using the standard agar well diffusion method. During the analysis, no inhibitory zones were found evident for 10 μg/ml concentration against both test bacteria (Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis). For the test concentration of 20 μg/ml, about 16 ± 0.57 mm and 15 ± 0.75 mm of inhibitory zones were found evident, and hence, this concentration was selected as MIC of developed composites. The higher concentration (30 μg/ml) exhibited inhibitory zones of about 17 ± 1.05 mm and 17 ± 0.75 mm against the respective test bacteria Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. The obtained values were compared with the standard antibiotic in parallel to confirm the antibacterial activity. The standard antibiotic showed slightly better activity (20 ± 0.57 mm and 20 ± 0.75 mm) than all three concentrates of developed composites, indicating that a slightly higher concentration is essential to achieve a similar inhibitory zone size. In Table 1 and Figure 2, the inhibitory zones of developed composites against test bacteria are illustrated.


### Biofilm inhibition assay of herbal composites
  Herbal composites developed in the present study showed anti-biofilm activity (eradication) against both test organisms. About 0.51 ± 1.25 mg/ml and 0.53 ± 0.57 mg/ml of MBEC against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis were found evident. Similarly, the standard drug also showed 0.58 ± 0.57 mg/ml and 0.59 ± 0.75 mg/ml of MBEC against test bacteria. The obtained values were found significantly similar to the MBEC values of the standard antibiotic, indicating the anti-biofilm activity of the developed herbal composites against the test organisms [Table 2].


### Effect of the herbal composite on lactic acid production and acid tolerance
  The effect of the herbal composite on the bacterial acidogenicity and the lactic acid production in bacterial cultures that were treated with 50 μg/ml, 100 μg/ml, 200 μg/ml, and 400 μg/ml of the herbal composite for 2 h was monitored. As shown in Figure 3, the composite reduced lactic acid production in a dose-dependent manner. From the graph, it was evident that about 25% lactic acid production was reduced by 50 μg/ml concentrate of the composite, and 50% lactic acid production was reduced by 100 μg/ml concentrate of the composite, while 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml concentrations reduced up to 80% of lactic acid production by test bacteria (P. gingivalis).

  An almost similar effect of the composite on the lactic acid produced by P. intermedia was found evident [Figure 4]. From the graph, it was evident that about 30% lactic acid production was reduced by 50 μg/ml concentrate of the composite, and 100 μg/ml concentrate of the composite decreased 50% lactic acid production, followed by 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml, reducing up to 70% of lactic acid production.

  According to the available scientific literature, the majority of dental pathogens have important acidogenic and aciduric features that contribute to their pathogenicity. Further, many dental pathogens have the capacity to convert dietary carbohydrates to lactic acid and survive in low-pH environments inducing alterations in dental biofilms and consequently leading to demineralization of dental tissues. One of the most important factors in lowering pathogenic virulence and promoting non-cariogenic oral biofilm is inhibition of the pathogens’ acidogenicity and aciduricity. The herbal composite used in this investigation demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in acidogenicity of both test organisms, with the effects being most noticeable at concentrate concentrations of 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml.

  Similar results were found by Barreca et al. (2014).[29] The researchers analyzed the effect of phloretin, a plant metabolite, on lactic acid production and acid tolerance by Streptococcus mutans. The results showed that it reduced S. mutans acidogenicity and survival in an acidic environment in a dose-dependent manner, with the strongest effects occurring at 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml.[29]


### Stability test of formulated mouthwash
  In Table 3, the results obtained for different stability test parameters are presented. Under all three different test conditions (5°C, 25°C, and 40°C), the color and odor did not change for the period of 3 months. No phase separation between the aqueous phase and composite phase was seen. The homogeneity of the product was thus found to be good.


### Antibacterial activity of formulated mouthwash
  The antibacterial activity of the developed mouthwash was determined using the standard agar well-diffusion method. During the analysis, no inhibitory zones were found evident for 10 μg/ml concentration against both test bacteria (Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis). For the test concentration of 20 μg/ml, about 17 ± 1.05 mm and 17 ± 0.75 mm of inhibitory zones were found evident, and hence, this concentration was selected as MIC of the developed mouthwash. The higher concentration (30 μg/ml) exhibited maximum inhibitory zones of about 18 ± 0.75 mm and 19 ± 1.05 mm against the respective test bacteria Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. The obtained values were compared with the standard antibiotic in parallel to confirm the antibacterial activity. The standard antibiotic showed slightly better activity (21 ± 0.57 mm and 21 ± 0.75 mm) than all three concentrates of developed mouthwash, indicating that a slightly higher concentration is essential to achieve a similar inhibitory zone size. In Table 4 and Figure 5, the inhibitory zones of the developed mouthwash against test bacteria are shown.

  This WBc mouthwash shows promising and potent antibacterial activity. This may be due to the presence of secondary metabolites in Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata solvent extracts, including alkaloids, anthraquinones, flavonoids, phenolics, saponins, tannins, and terpenoids, which explain the antimicrobial properties. A number of earlier studies attribute the antimicrobial properties, antioxidant properties, and anticancer properties of Bauhinia variegata to the phytochemicals and other similar biological compounds it contains. Pettit et al. (2006) reported that the plant’s aerial portions contain 6-butyl-3-hydroxy flavanone, amino acids, dimeric flavonoids, flavone glycosides, lutine, and phenyl fatty ester; most of these compounds were found attributed to antibacterial and antifungal activities.[30] Pandey (2007)[31] and Mahmoodally et al. (2005)[32] reported that the plants’ antibacterial properties are due to their secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and other phenolic substances. Dixon et al. (1983)[33] found that flavonoids are hydroxylated phenolic substances and are produced by B. variegata in response to the microbial assault role of flavonoids.

  Tsuchiya et al. (1996)[34] described the mode of action of phytochemicals on the bacterial cell components responsible for the cell death. Antibacterial activity is probably due to their ability to form complexes with extra-cellular and soluble proteins within bacterial cell walls, leading to the disruption of microbial cell membranes. Aboaba and Efuwape (2001)[35] highlighted that these herbs contain non-toxic glycosides which can get hydrolyzed to release phenolics which are toxic to microbial pathogens. Sikkema et al. (1994)[36] reported the significance of terpenoids and their antimicrobial efficacy against different microbes; they showed that the antibacterial activity was exhibited by the loss of membrane integrity and dissipation of proton motive force in the bacterial membrane structures, leading to cell disintegration and cell death.

  The antibacterial activity of developed WBc, which naturally contains different types of bio-constituents, is responsible for the obtained inhibitory activity against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis in our present study.


### Detection of virulent genes
  Two pairs of sequences were designed as primers (forward and reverse) for the amplification of the mfa1 and clpB genes with previously published sequences.[3738] During the analysis, 246 bp and 294 bp fragments of target gene mfa1 of P. gingivalis and 238 bp and 280 bp fragment of target gene clpB of P. intermedia were obtained. In Table 5 and Figure 6, the PCR amplicons of mfa1 and clpB gene fragments, along with the marker DNA ladder, are presented.


### Molecular docking of quercetin, the bioactive compound of the developed herbal composite with mfa1 genes of Porphyromonas gingivalis, and determining binding energy
  Quercetin is the strong antibacterial triterpenoid compound shown to be present in the developed herbal composite. In the present study, the molecular docking of quercetin against the target biofilm-producing protein expressed in the periodontitis-causing pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis was investigated.

  The protein used for the docking studies is the minor fimbrium subunit, and its transcriptional activator proteins are mfa1. Based on the quantity of hydrogen bonds created during the docking process, the docking energy in terms of binding energies of quercetin and the minor fimbrium subunit coding gene, mfa1, was examined.


### Docking analysis of the minor fimbrium subunit coding Mfa1 with quercetin
  The structure of quercetin was obtained from the Pubchem database, visualized using ACD ChemSketch, optimized, and saved in MDL MOL (Mol-inspiration) format. Next, the format was converted to PDB using Open Babel’s molecular converter [Figure 7].


#### mfa1
  The minor fimbrium subunit mfa1 of Porphyromonas gingivalis was retrieved from uniport. The PDB database did not contain the structure of the mfa1 protein. As a result, it was modeled using the Swissmodel server and the 5NF3-A chain template [Figure 8]. Using the SAVES-PROCHECK server to evaluate the modeled structure, it was discovered to be of good quality because 90.6% of the residues fell in the most favored zone and only one residue fell in the disallowed region. The PDB id of the protein is B2RHG1.

  Docking of mfa1 + quercetin and visualizing hydrogen interactions between the compounds using Acceryls Discovery Studio Visualizer are presented in Table 6. Docking images in both three-dimensional and two-dimensional structures are presented in Figures 9 and 10, respectively. In Figure 9, the quercetin is presented in black-colored scaled balls and a green-colored stick model, and mfa1 is presented in a brown-colored stick model.

  The overall docking energies of the target protein, the minor fimbrium subunit with quercetin with the number of hydrogen bonds, are presented in Table 7. The docking report revealed -5.84 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found three hydrogen bonding between the quercetin and target protein, mfa1 of Porphyromonas gingivalis.

  The protein used for the docking studies is chaperone, and its transcriptional activator proteins are ClpB. Based on the quantity of hydrogen bonds produced during the in silico molecular docking of quercetin and the chaperone-coding gene ClpB, the docking energy in terms of binding energies was examined. The following section explains the overall docking energies of the target protein, ClpB, with quercetin and the amount of hydrogen bonds.


#### clpB
  Chaperone protein ClpB from Prevotella intermedia was retrieved from the uniport database. In the PDB database, the structure of clpB was not present. The A0A096D117-A chain’s template Alpha fold was used to model it using the Swissmodel server [Figure 11]. The modeled structure was assessed using the SAVES-PROCHECK server, and it was determined to be a highly effective model because 96.8% of the residues fell in the region that was most favored and none fell in the region that was prohibited. The PDB id of the protein was A0A2D3LJY5.

  Docking of clpB + quercetin and visualizing hydrogen interactions between the compounds using Acceryls Discovery Studio Visualizer is presented in Table 8. Docking images in both three-dimensional and two-dimensional structures are presented in Figures 12 and 13, respectively. In Figure 12, the quercetin is presented in black-colored scaled balls and a stick model, and clpB is presented in a brown-colored stick model.

  The overall docking energies of the target protein, chaperone, and quercetin are displayed [Table 9]. The target protein, clpB of Prevotella intermedia, and quercetin had -6.72 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found four hydrogen bonds between them, according to the docking report.

  To the best of our knowledge, this preliminary study uses a herbal composite (of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata) done for the first time. The limitations of our current study include a random selection of a single gene for each bacteria and the use of a commonly present antibacterial phytochemical compound in the composite quercetin. Further studies should involve a thorough analysis of the various genes along with the other bioactive compounds.


## CONCLUSION
  The MIC of Wrightia tinctoria and Bauhinia variegata composites (WBc) was obtained for the test concentration of 20 μg/ml (16 ± 0.57 mm and 15 ± 0.75 mm of inhibitory zones against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis). Biofilm inhibition assay results revealed about 0.51 ± 1.25 mg/ml and 0.53 ± 0.57 mg/ml of MBEC against Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. The effect of WBc on lactic acid production showed that 200 μg/ml and 400 μg/ml concentrates reduced up to 80% and 70% in Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis, respectively. Formulated herbal mouthwash showed good stability under all three different test conditions (5°C, 25°C, and 40°C) as the color, odor, phase separation, and homogeneity did not change for the period of 3 months. Antibacterial activity of formulated mouthwash (30 μg/ml) exhibited maximum inhibitory zones of about 18 ± 0.75mm and 19 ± 1.05 mm against the respective test bacteria Prevotella intermedia and Porphyromonas gingivalis. Amplification of mfa1 and clpB genes showed 246 bp and 294 bp fragments of P. gingivalis and 238 bp and 280 bp fragments of P. intermedia during agarose electrophoretic analysis. The docking report revealed -5.84 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found three hydrogen bonding between the quercetin and target protein, mfa1 of Porphyromonas gingivalis. The target protein, clpB of Prevotella intermedia, and quercetin had -6.72 Kcal/Mol binding energy and found four hydrogen bonds between them.
