# Sub-millisecond Atomistic Molecular Dynamics Simulations Reveal Hydrogen Bond-Driven Diffusion of a Guest Peptide in Protein-RNA Condensate


## Abstract
  Liquid-liquid phase separation mediated by proteins and/or nucleic acids is believed to underlie the formation of many distinct condensed phases, or membraneless organelles, within living cells. These condensates have been proposed to orchestrate a variety of important processes. Despite recent advances, the interactions that regulate the dynamics of molecules within a condensate remain poorly understood. We performed an accumulated 564.7μs all-atom Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations (system size ~200K atoms) of model condensates formed by a scaffold RNA oligomer and a scaffold peptide rich in arginine (Arg). These model condensates contained one of three possible guest peptides: the scaffold peptide itself or a variant in which six Arg residues were replaced by lysine (Lys) or asymmetric dimethyl arginine (ADMA). We found that the Arg-rich peptide can form the largest number of hydrogen bonds and bind the strongest to the scaffold RNA in the condensate, relative to the Lys- and ADMA-rich peptides. Our MD simulations also showed that the Arg-rich peptide diffused more slowly in the condensate relative to the other two guest peptides, which is consistent with a recent fluorescence microscopy study. There was no significant increase in the number of cation-π interactions between the Arg-rich peptide and the scaffold RNA compared to the Lys-rich and ADMA-rich peptides. Our results indicate that hydrogen bonds between the peptides and the RNA backbone, rather than cation-π interactions, play the major role in regulating peptide diffusion in the condensate.


## Introduction
  Liquid-liquid phase separation underlies the formation of biomolecular condensates1–11. The formation of condensates spatially compartmentalizes and enriches the proteins and substrates necessary for biological processes and reactions4,5. Most of the known biomolecular condensates in the cell are formed by interactions between proteins and RNA1–3,10; examples include the nucleolus, stress granules, and P-bodies9,12,13. The proteins in these biomolecular condensates can be categorized as a scaffold protein or a guest (or client) protein. Scaffold proteins and RNA form the condensates, and the guest proteins are recruited to the condensates14. The scaffold proteins often contain intrinsically disordered regions (IDR) that are characterized by low sequence complexity and net positive charge6. IDRs associate non-specifically with RNA and drive the formation of the condensates15. RGG/RG repeats constitute one important IDR class that are found in well-studied scaffold proteins such as Fused in Sarcoma (FUS)1. The positively charged RGG/RG repeats bind to the negatively charged RNA chains to form and maintain the integrity of the liquid condensate. After the formation of the protein-RNA liquid condensate, the guest proteins diffuse into and through the condensate to perform their biological functions.6 Mutations in scaffold proteins could alter the tendency of condensate formation or the morphology of the condensate, while mutations in guest proteins could affect their interactions and mobility in the condensate, leading to protein mislocalization15. These abnormal biomolecular condensates have been associated with cancer, neurodegenerative diseases and other pathologies15,16. Interactions of scaffold proteins that modulate protein-RNA condensate formation and conformation have been explored7,8,17,18.

  Despite rapid advances in the study of protein-RNA condensates, the intermolecular interactions that regulate condensate formation and guest protein dynamics are not fully understood. It is widely accepted that electrostatic interactions are critical for condensate formation7,19. In addition, Banerjee and coworkers suggested that short-range cation-π interactions may govern the behavior of liquid condensates and peptide diffusion within condensates20. This group used fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) to compare the diffusion of an Arg-rich peptide and Lys-rich peptide in a peptide-RNA condensate. They found that the Arg-rich peptide diffused more slowly than the Lys-rich peptide in these condensates, an observation that was attributed to differences in the favorability of cation-π interactions formed by guanidinium (Arg side chain) vs. ammonium (Lys side chain) groups20. However, a subsequent experimental study involving different peptides and RNA suggested that hydrogen bonds between peptides and RNA may be more important than cation-π interactions in regulating condensate formation and guest peptide diffusion21. Niu et al. showed that the ability of peptides to form condensates with RNA is impaired when the ability of the side chain to form hydrogen bonds is reduced without altering net charge. These workers observed that replacing Arg with residues resulting from a common post-translational modification, asymmetric dimethyl arginine (ADMA), led to a loss of capacity to form condensates with RNA. At neutral pH, both the Arg and ADMA side chains bear a net positive charge, but the hydrogen bonding capacity of the ADMA residue is lower. Previous studies with a peptide folding system have shown that ADMA forms stronger cation-π interactions relative to Arg22.

  Both the Arg-rich and ADMA-rich peptides could be recruited to a pre-formed condensate scaffolded by the Arg-rich peptide and RNA; however, the Arg-rich peptide diffused into and through the condensate at a slower rate than the ADMA-rich peptide. These observations suggest that the long-range interactions due to Coulombic attractions between cationic peptides and anionic RNA are sufficient for the initial recruitment of peptides into the condensate, but local hydrogen bond interactions may be required to retain and regulate diffusion of a recruited guest peptide within the condensate. Much remains to be learned about the intermolecular interactions that influence diffusion of a guest peptide in condensates scaffolded by biopolymers.

  Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can provide insight into the intermolecular interactions involved in condensate formation and the diffusion of guest peptides in the condensate. Nevertheless, conducting all-atom MD (AA-MD) simulations to study condensate formation is computationally demanding, primarily due to the substantial quantity of proteins and RNA involved and the challenge of achieving biologically relevant timescales23,24. To overcome the high computational cost of simulating condensate formation, coarse-grained MD (CG-MD) simulations in implicit solvent have been used, where a group of atoms are represented as a coarse-grained bead. CG-MD simulations have been shown to reproduce the concentration of peptides in condensates and the dilute phase as well as the phase transition curve for condensate formation. This demonstrates the accuracy of CG-MD simulations in predicting the thermodynamics properties of condensates23. CG-MD simulations, however, lack the atomic resolution to investigate molecular interactions and also often predict faster dynamics than experimental observations. Although the applications of AA-MD simulations to study the formation of the condensate remain challenging, they have been successfully applied to simulate and reproduce the liquid-like behavior of the condensed phase of simpler systems, such as the 35-residue elastin-like peptide (ELP)25. For example, by using AA-MD simulations, Pomès and coworkers successfully determined the specific interactions between ELP residues that form the condensate and analyzed the ELP diffusion behavior in the melt-like or condensed phase11. Similarly, Barducci and coworkers performed AA-MD simulations of 12-residue peptides from the N-terminal disordered region of DEAD-box helicase 4 to determine the main molecular interactions in the already formed condensate26. AA-MD simulations can provide the atomic details of the intermolecular interactions and dynamics of guest peptide diffusion in the condensed phase. This will be invaluable for understanding how guest protein diffuse and interact with various components in the condensate.

  In this work, we performed ~2,000 AA-MD simulations (referred to as MD simulations thereafter) involving a condensed phase formed between an RNA oligomer and a cationic peptide (A in Figure 1). These simulations were used to compare the diffusion of three different guest peptides within the condensed phase. For each system, one guest peptide molecule was inserted in the condensate simulation box. Altogether, we obtained a cumulative length of 564.7 μs, and the average length of each MD simulation was around 200 ns (see Table 1 and Fig 1). The guest peptides differed in the nature of cationic groups in six of the side chains. Peptide A contained Arg at the variable positions, peptide B contained asymmetric dimethyl Arg (ADMA) Lys at these positions, and peptide C contained Lys at these positions. As a result of the side chain differences, these peptides varied in their ability to form hydrogen bonds and cation-π interactions with RNA. Using the integrative generalized master equation (IGME) method27, we calculated the binding rate and dissociation constants of these guest peptides with the condensates. We observed that the Arg-rich peptide A has the smallest KD value with the condensate; thus, A bound the strongest to the condensate and diffused slowest in the condensate relative to the Lys-rich peptide B or the ADMA-rich peptide C. This finding is consistent with a previous fluorescence microscopy experiment21. More importantly, we found that there is a significant increase in hydrogen bonds between the Arg-rich peptide and the backbone of the RNA, and no noticeable increase in the number of cation-π interactions compared to the other two peptides. Our results support the hypothesis that hydrogen bonding between peptide side chains and the RNA backbone is the main factor that regulates the binding and diffusion of peptides in condensates formed by a peptide/RNA combination.


## Methods

### Force field parameters for the asymmetric dimethyl arginine (ADMA) sidechain
  To obtain the force field for ADMA, geometry optimization and electrostatic potential calculation were done using Gaussian 1628. Geometry optimization was performed using the Becke, three-parameter, Lee–Yang–Parr method and a basis set of 6–31 g* (no. B3LYP/6–31 G*). We then performed an electrostatic potential calculation using the Hartree–Fock method with a basis set of 6–31 g* (no. HF/6–31 g*). The partial charges of PET4 were then fitted using the RESP module in AmberTools2129. The non-bonded and bonded parameters of PET4 were obtained from the GAFF forcefield30.


### MD simulations of the protein-RNA condensate
  To prepare the condensate system, we followed the preparation done for condensate formation from an experiment21. In the experiment, 0.1 mM of peptide was mixed with roughly 0.8 mM of RNA nucleotides21. Thus, for each scaffold peptide, there are 8 RNA nucleotides in our simulation box to mimic the 1:8 peptide:RNA-nucleotide ratio in the experiment. The sequence of the 28-residue scaffold peptide was taken from the C-terminal domain of the FUS peptide residue 471–497, where the aspartic acid residues were substituted to glutamic acids21 (Fig. 2a). The sequence of the 8-nucleotide RNA is shown in Fig. 2a. Eight scaffold peptides and eight RNA chains were placed in a cubic box. The size of the box was chosen such that the concentration of the peptide was 7.4 mM to match19 the reported experimental peptide concentration in the condensate21. The system was then solvated in 56,408 TIP4P-disp31 water molecules. In addition, 74 sodium and 56 chloride ions were added to reach a 0.05 M salt concentration and to neutralize the system. The total number of atoms in the system was 231,032. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME)33,34 algorithm was used to calculate the Coulombic interactions, with the short-range cut off at 1 nm. The Lennard-Jones interactions were also cut off at 1 nm. The energy of the system was minimized using the steepest descent algorithm. Following this, NVT equilibration was performed for 1 ns with position restraints (with a force constant of 1,000 kJ mol–1 nm–2) on all the heavy atoms of the peptide and RNA chains at 300 K. V-rescale thermostat35 was used with a coupling constant of 0.1 ps. The system was then equilibrated under NPT conditions at 1 bar for 1 ns with a position restraint (with a force constant of 1,000 kJ mol–1 nm–2) on all heavy atoms of the peptide and RNA. The Parrinello-Rahman barostat36 was used with a coupling constant of 2 ps. An MD simulation was performed for 150 ns under NPT conditions. We calculated the autocorrelation function of the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) of the solute molecules (8 peptides and 8 RNA molecules) in the 150-ns MD simulation. The surface area of the solute molecules is a good indicator of the solute molecules’ conformations during the simulation. As shown in SI Fig. 1, the autocorrelation function of SASA decays to around zero for the solute molecules, which indicates that 150 ns is sufficient to equilibrate the initial condensed phase simulation. Five conformations of protein-RNA condensates were extracted from the last 50 ns of the simulation. The hydrogen atoms were constrained using LINCs37, and the Leap-frog integrator with a step size of 2 fs was used. All MD simulations were performed using Gromacs 2019.638, amber99sb-disp31 force field for the peptides, and amberff99bsc0χOL3 force field for the RNA39.


### MD simulations of the condensate with the guest peptides
  There are three simulation systems, differentiated by the residue at the X sites of the guest peptide, i.e., arginine, asymmetric dimethyl-arginine, and lysine; these correspond to system A, B, and C, respectively (Fig. 1b). One guest peptide was randomly placed in the simulation box of the five conformations of protein-RNA condensate extracted in the previous section, which are used as initial conformations for the MD simulations. The size of system A, B and C were 225,670, 225,706, and 225,658, respectively (Table 1). The Coulombic and Lennard-Jones interactions were calculated using the same method as the MD simulations of the condensates. For simulation system, the energy was then minimized using the steepest descent algorithm. An NVT equilibration simulation followed by NPT equilibration was performed for 1 ns using the same parameters as the MD simulations of the condensates. For each system, five production MD simulations were performed under NPT conditions for 200 ns. In total, there are 15 200-ns Production MD simulations. For all simulations, hydrogen atoms were constrained using LINCS37, and the Leap-frog integrator with a step size of 2 fs was used. All MD simulations were performed using Gromacs 2019.638, the amber99sb-disp force field31 for peptides and amberff99bsc0χOL3 force field for the RNA39.


### Folding@home simulations of the condensate with a guest peptide
  The final structure of the MD simulations in the previous section was used as the initial structure for the MD simulations performed at Folding@home40,41. The MD simulations were initiated using a simulation core based on OpenMM 7.4.142. The Langevin middle integrator43 with 2 fs step size and friction coefficient of 1.0 ps−1 was used. In this work, we simulated only the condensate or condensed phase. Our condensate simulation includes the condensate network (scaffold peptide and RNA) and the guest peptide solvated in water molecules and counterions (Na+ and Cl−). The scaffold peptides and RNA chains form an extensive network of interactions (so-called condensate network hereafter) which is expanded by the periodic boundary condition in our MD simulations (Fig. 1d and SI Fig.5). Additionally, since the simulation is already of the condensed phase, we did not directly simulate the recruitment of the guest peptide into the condensate.


### Control MD simulations of a single scaffold peptide or RNA in solution
  We also performed control MD simulations of a single scaffold peptide or RNA in solution. For these MD simulations, a scaffold peptide was placed in a dodecahedron box, where the box size was determined such that the distance between the solute and the edge of the box was at least 2 nm. For the simulation of a single chain RNA chain, the RNA chain was added to a dodecahedron simulation box and the box size was set such that the distance between the RNA and the edge of the box was at least 1 nm. For both systems, the simulation box was solvated with TIP3P32 water, and NaCl was added to reach a 0.05 M concentration. The system was energy minimized using the steepest descent algorithm. This was followed by NVT (at 300 K) and NPT (at 1 bar) equilibrations for 1 ns with position restraint on the heavy atoms of the solute (force constant = 1,000 kJ mol−1 nm–2). The short-range cut-off for the Lennard-Jones and Coulombic interactions were both 1 nm. The long-range Coulombic interactions were calculated using the PME33,34 algorithm. Five independent MD simulations were performed for 800ns under NPT conditions for each system. The thermostat and barostat used were the same as for the MD simulation of the condensate in Sec. 2.4. The hydrogen atoms were constrained using LINCs37, and the Leap-frog integrator with a step size of 2 fs was used. The MD simulations were performed using Gromacs 2019.638 and the amber99sb-disp force field31 for the peptides and amberff99bsc0χOL3 force field for the RNA39.


### Calculation of transition rates and dissociation constants of guest peptides diffusion in condensates
  To compute the binding rates of the guest peptide to the condensate network in the condensed phase, we first assign all MD conformations to two conformational states, namely the bound and dissociated states. An MD conformation is assigned to the bound state when the guest peptide is in contact with the condensate network (where the shortest distance between an atom of the guest peptide and an atom of the condensates is within 4 Å). An MD conformation that does not meet this criterion is assigned to the dissociated state. Next, we applied our recently developed IGME method27,44 to compute the rates for the transition between the bound and dissociated states. The IGME method is based on the generalized master equation framework45–47, and it has been shown to be an efficient and robust way to estimate the rates for transitions between various conformational states from an ensemble of MD simulations27. Specifically, the two-state transition probability matrices (TPM) were calculated for all three systems with maximum likelihood estimation to enforce the detailed balance requirement48. The TPMs were then used as input for the IGME method27. There are two hyperparameters involved in the IGME construction: i.e.: memory kernel decay time (τK) and length of data used for fitting (L). In Fig. 4c and d, we reported the transition rates between the bound and dissociated states (Kon and Koff), where the error bars were computed from bootstrapping. In each round of the 20-round bootstrapping, we randomly selected the trajectories with replacement and calculated the TPMs at lag times from 1 to 50 ns with a 1 ns interval. Then, we systematically scanned the values of τK and L between 1 and 50 ns with a 1 ns interval to generate IGME models and calculate the transition rates. From each bootstrapping, we chose the top 5% of IGME models (62 models, SI Fig. 3). Finally, the average values and the error bars of Kon and Koff in Fig. 4c and d were computed from all the top 5% of models of the bootstrapping (i.e. 20×62 = 1240 models). To assess the robustness of our calculated transition rates concerning the choice of the in-contact thresholds (4 Å), we also employed two other values, 3 Å and 5 Å, to classify the bound and dissociated states in our MD simulations (SI Fig. 2b–d). Using these thresholds, we did not observe any change in terms of the trend of transition rates and dissociation constants for the three guest peptides in condensate (SI Fig. 2b–d and Fig. 4c&d).

  To calculate the lifetime of the guest peptide in the bound and dissociated state, we first chose the top IGME model and outputted the T^, for each system (system A, B, and C). Using the T^, we then performed Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulations to generate 4 trajectories with 10 million steps each. From each trajectory, we timed how long the guest peptide stays in either the bound (lifetime of bound state) or dissociated state (lifetime of dissociated state). We then reported the average over all timed lifetimes for each state of each system


### Calculation of the number of hydrogen bond and cation-π interactions between guest peptide and condensate
  We calculated the number of hydrogen bond interactions between the residues at the X sites of the guest peptide with either the RNA phosphate, sugar, or base. A hydrogen bond is formed if i) the distance between the electronegative atom of the donor and the acceptor atom is within 3.5 Å, ii) the angle between the hydrogen of the donor, electronegative atom of the donor, and the acceptor is within 30°. fWe also calculated the number of cation-π between the residues at the X sites of the guest peptide with the RNA base. To calculate the number of cation-π interactions, two distances were defined depending on the residue at the X sites of the guest peptide and the RNA base. The CZ atom of Arg, the CZ atom of ADMA, or the NZ atom of Lys of the guest peptide (system A, B, or C, respectively) was used for the distance calculation. Then two atoms of the RNA bases were chosen for the distance calculation, i.e., i) C4 and C5 of Adenine, ii) N1 and C4 of Cytosine, iii) C4 and C5 of Guanine. Then, two pairwise distances between the previously mentioned atoms of the guest peptide and the two atoms of the RNA base were calculated. If both pairwise distances were within 5 Å, the cation-π interaction was formed.


### Guest peptide real-time recruitment experiment
  To observe the recruitment of the guest peptide into the condensate, 15 μL of a solution containing 0.4 mM peptide A, which includes 4 mol% Fl-1, was mixed with 15 μL solution containing 1 μg/μL RNA in 15 μL 50 mM Tris buffer (pH = 7.5). The droplets were observed under the microscope after 2 hours. Then, the 15 μL of the solution containing 0.4 mM of guest peptide (either the Arg-rich, ADMA-rich, or Lys-rich peptide, which includes 4 mol% TMR-labeled derivative of the guest peptide) in 50 mM Tris buffer was added and gently mixed. The videos were then immediately obtained using Leica SP8. The fluorescein and tetramethylrhodamine units were sequentially excited one line at a time, in order to reduce the cross-talk between the two fluorophores. The recruitment video of the Arg-rich guest peptide was reported in Ref. 21. The recruitment videos of the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich guest peptides to the condensate were reported in this work. Further details of the real-time recruitment experiment were described in Ref. 21.


## Results and Discussions

### Scaffold peptides and RNA chains form a network of intermolecular interactions in the condensed phase
  MD simulations can provide insight into the intermolecular interactions in the condensed phase. Here, we performed MD simulations of the condensed phase for the mixture of 8 scaffold peptides and 8 RNA molecules (where each RNA contains 8 nucleotides, and thus results in a 1:8 ratio of peptide:nucleotides) with a peptide concentration of 7.4 mM, the same concentration of peptide in the condensate as determined by experiment (inset figure of Fig. 1a)21. The sequence of the scaffold peptide was derived from the C-terminal domain of FUS, which has been shown to form liquid condensates when mixed with RNA21. The sequence of the RNA was taken from the yeast 35S ribosomal RNA49, as it is the most abundant RNA in the yeast cell. In addition, we inserted one guest peptide into the condensate simulation box. We simulated three kinds of systems, depending on the guest peptide inserted, Arg-rich (A), ADMA-rich (B), or Lys-rich (C) peptide (Fig. 1b, Table 1).

  To ask whether the scaffold peptide and RNA form a multivalent condensate network, we analyzed the structural properties of the condensate part (scaffold peptide and RNA) of the simulations. Both the scaffold peptides and RNA chains have a larger solvent accessible surface area (SASA) in the condensed phase than as single chains in solution (Fig. 1c). This observation suggests that the scaffold peptides and RNA chains are more extended in the condensed phase and therefore that they are more accessible to form intermolecular interactions, which is consistent with a previous MD simulation study25. Furthermore, the number of solute molecules in contact (partners) with each scaffold peptide or RNA is calculated for the condensed phase simulations. Both scaffold peptides and RNA chains are simultaneously in contact with three to four other molecules on average (Fig. 1d). The highly dynamic and extensive interactions between the scaffold peptides and RNA chains create the condensate network (SI Fig. 5a). As demonstrated in SI Fig. 5a, the scaffold peptides and RNA can rapidly break and form their interactions with each other during the simulation, preventing the formation of irreversible aggregates. This interaction network of the condensate is expanded via the periodic boundary condition in our MD simulations (SI Fig. 5b). These structural and dynamic properties of the condensate were observed in previous MD simulations of the condensed phase26. Evidently, we did not observe the dilute solution phase of the scaffold peptide or RNA, since there is a negligible fraction of isolated scaffold peptides or RNAs in our simulation (Fig. 1D). These observations indicate that the peptide concentration and simulation box volume were appropriate to observe the condensed phase. In addition, by calculating the probability of intermolecular residue-residue interaction in the condensate, we observed that the positively charged scaffold peptide tends to form more extensive intermolecular interactions with the negatively charged RNA than with other scaffold peptides (darker heatmap between scaffold peptide and RNA in Fig. 2a). These observations indicate that our condensed phase MD simulations can reproduce the complex multivalent network in the liquid condensate.


### Guest peptides preferentially interact with the RNA chains in the condensate via hydrogen bonding
  To understand the molecular interactions underlying guest peptide diffusion in condensate, we further analyzed the interactions between the guest peptides and the condensate network. The three different guest peptides have the same net charge (+5) but differ by the residue at the X sites, i.e., arginine Arg-rich (system A), asymmetric dimethyl arginine ADMA-rich (system B), and lysine Lys-rich peptides (system C) (Fig. 1b). For each system, one guest peptide was placed in the scaffold protein-RNA condensate simulation box. A previous fluorescence confocal microscopy study has demonstrated that these three peptides can be recruited into the condensate formed by peptide A and total yeast RNA21. W2121e observed that the Arg-rich peptide diffuses into and through the condensate at a slower rate than either the ADMA-rich or Lys-rich peptides21 (supporting video of Ref21 and SI video 1 and 2). Therefore, to observe the diffusion of the guest peptide in the condensate with atomic resolution, we performed around 750 200-ns MD simulations on average for each system consisting of ~225,000 atoms using Folding@Home (see Table 1 for a summary of our MD simulations).

  To gain structural insight into how the guest peptide interacts with the condensate network, we analyzed the propensity for the residues in the guest peptide to interact with the residues in the condensate network, as visualized in the heatmap plots in Fig. 2b–d. Notably, in all three systems, we observe a higher probability for guest peptide residues to interact with an RNA oligonucleotide (10% to 25%), and a smaller probability (<5%) for guest peptide residues to interact with a scaffold peptide residue, as depicted in Fig. 2b–d. We further reported the occupancy probability of the condensate network, which is defined as accumulated probability of MD conformations where any residue in the guest peptide interacts with a given scaffold peptide residue or RNA oligonucleotide in the condensate (see the bottom occupancy plots in Fig. 2b–d). We find that the RNA oligonucleotides in the condensate network have a higher occupancy than the scaffold peptide residues for all three systems (Fig. 2b–d). We also reported the occupancy probability of the guest peptide, where we calculated the accumulated percentage of MD conformations in which any residue/nucleotide in the condensate network interacts with a given residue in the guest peptide. As shown in right occupancy plots in Fig. 2b–d, the positively charged residue at the X sites of the guest peptides generally have a higher occupancy than the other residues in the guest peptides. To provide better signal-to-noise ratio regarding the occupancy and role of the X-site residues, We calculated the average occupancy of the residues at the X sites and their neighboring residues (X−1 and X+1 sites) in the guest peptide for all three systems (SI Fig. 4). The X-site residues have the highest occupancy compared to their neighboring residues in all three systems. Overall, our results show that the positively charged residue at the X sites of the guest peptides tends to interact more with the negatively charged RNA oligonucleotides, despite the presence of negatively charged residues in the scaffold peptides. We note that the residues at the X sites can provide hydrogen donors while RNA contain hydrogen acceptors. Thus, in addition to the long-range Coulombic interactions between the positively charged sidechains at the X sites and negatively charged RNA phosphate, the guest peptides and RNA may also interact via hydrogen bonds. We then analyzed the hydrogen bonds between the residue at the X sites and different parts of RNA in the next section, to further explore the specific interactions between guest peptides and RNA.


### Arg-rich peptides form more hydrogen bonds with RNA of condensate compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides
  To examine the extent of these hydrogen bond interactions, we quantified the number of hydrogen bonds and the percentage of MD frames where hydrogen bonds occur (occupancy) for three distinct interactions: the guest peptide’s X site residue with (a) the RNA phosphate, (b) the RNA base, and (c) the RNA sugar (as depicted in Fig. 3a–c). We find that the RNA residues in the condensate interact with the Arg-rich guest peptide on average ~50% of the time compared to an average ~30% occupancy with the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich guest peptides (bottom occupancy plot Fig. 2b–d). We expect that the strong hydrogen bond interactions between the Arg-rich peptide and the condensate will increase its lifetime bound to the condensate. Our simulations also show that the Arg-rich peptide residues interact with the condensate network ~60% of the time compared with ~40% occupancy of the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides (right occupancy plot Fig. 2b–d). Moreover, the average occupancy when X is Arg is significantly larger than when X is either ADMA or Lys (SI Fig. 4), which is consistent with our observation that Arg residues form more persistent interactions with the condensate network. These observations indicate that the Arg-rich peptide binds more strongly to the condensate network than the ADMA-rich or Lys-rich peptides, despite all three guest peptides having the same net positive charge. Further analysis show that this is because Arg forms a greater number and more persistent hydrogen bonds with all components of RNA compared to ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides (Fig. 3a–c). This is expected as the Arg sidechain contains five hydrogen bond donors, while both ADMA and Lys can only provide three (Fig 3e). Additionally, Arg can form more favorable bidentate hydrogen bonds with the RNA phosphate compared to those formed by Lys50, whereas ADMA can only form monodentate hydrogen bonds (Fig 3e)21.

  Although Arg residues have a greater number and occupancy of hydrogen bonds with RNA compared to ADMA and Lys residues, the greater proportion is not uniform across the various components of RNA. Specifically, the increases in hydrogen bonds between Arg and the RNA phosphate is more significant than those between Arg and the RNA sugar or the RNA base, as illustrated in Fig. 3a–c. To quantify these findings, the number of hydrogen bonds between the RNA phosphate and Arg is roughly 10 times greater than with ADMA and 4 times greater than with Lys, as depicted in Fig. 3a. In addition, the number of hydrogen bond between the RNA base and Arg is 3 times greater than with ADMA and 1.8 times greater than with Lys, as shown in Fig. 3b. Lastly, the number of hydrogen bonds between the RNA sugar and Arg is 1.3 greater than with ADMA and 1.9 greater than with Lys, as shown in Fig. 3c. All these observations indicate that the increase in hydrogen bond occupancy between Arg and the RNA phosphate is more significant than that between Arg and the RNA base or the RNA sugar (Fig. 4a–c). This further suggests that the stronger interaction between the Arg-rich peptide and RNA can be due to the hydrogen bonds between Arg and the RNA phosphate. The greater proportion of hydrogen bonds between Arg and the RNA phosphate is due to the arginine’s ability to form bidentate hydrogen bonds with the RNA phosphate, as opposed to ADMA, which can only form a monodentate hydrogen bond, or Lys, for which a bidentate H-bond seems less favorable than for Arg50. This distinction arises from the ability of the guanidinium group in Arg to simultaneously form two hydrogen bonds (due to its suitable geometry) with the oxygen atoms of the phosphate group in the RNA phosphate (bidentate hydrogen bonds). In contrast, the geometry of the hydrogen acceptors in the RNA sugar and base prevents the formation of bidentate hydrogen bonds with Arg (Fig. 3e).

  A previous study has suggested that the cation-π interactions play a critical role in the protein-RNA condensate20. To examine if the cation-π interactions contribute to the binding of the guest peptides to the condensate, we determined the average number of cation-π interactions (Fig 3d). Surprisingly, we found that the number of cation-π interactions and occupancy of the RNA bases with Arg is lower compared to ADMA (Fig 3d). These observations suggest that the hydrogen bonding interactions is of paramount importance, rather than cation-π interactions, for the stronger binding of the Arg-rich peptide to the RNA in the condensate compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides.


### Arg-rich peptide diffuses slower in the condensate compared to the ADMA-rich or Lys-rich peptides
  Since the Arg-rich peptides form stronger hydrogen bonds with the RNA of the condensate network, we hypothesize that the Arg-rich peptides will diffuse slower in the condensate. To further analyze the dynamics of guest peptide inside the condensate, we calculated the association rates (kon), dissociation rates (koff), and binding affinity (KD) for the binding of the guest peptide to the condensate network (formed by the scaffold peptide and RNA) in the condensed phase. We first calculated the shortest distance between the guest peptide and the condensate network. As seen in SI Fig. 2A, there is a clear sharp peak at distances shorter than 4Å, which indicates that the guest peptide is in contact with the condensate network. There is no other peak observed at distances larger than 4Å, suggesting that, at this distance, the guest peptide does not form specific interactions with the condensate network and freely diffuses in the aqueous space between the condensate network in the condensed phase. Based on this observation, we categorized the MD simulation frames into either bound or dissociated states. The guest peptide was considered bound when it was in contact with the condensate network, which is defined as having any atoms of the guest peptide within 4Å of any atoms of the condensate network. It was considered dissociated when the guest peptide was not in contact with the condensate network or was freely diffusing between the condensate network (Fig. 4a–b). The shortest distance between the guest peptide and the condensate network of some representative trajectories is shown for all three systems (Fig. 4b). We find that the Arg-rich peptide is mostly bound to the condensate network, while the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides rapidly oscillate between the bound and dissociated states (Fig. 4b). We then calculated the transition rates between the dissociated and bound states for the three systems using the IGME method (see Sec 2.6 for details). Among the three guest peptides, the Arg-rich peptide dissociates the slowest from the condensate cluster, approximately 0.4 times slower than the ADMA-rich peptide and 0.2 slower than the Lys-rich peptide (koff, Fig. 4c). Additionally, the Arg-rich peptide binds around 2.0 times faster to the condensate compared to the ADMA-rich peptides and 1.3 times faster compared to the Lys-rich peptides (kon, Fig. 4c). Using these transition rates, we calculated the KD for all three systems. Our results show that the Arg-rich peptide has the strongest binding affinity to the condensate scaffold, and the ADMA-rich peptide has a slightly stronger binding affinity compared to the Lys-rich peptide (Fig. 4d). Notably, the diffusion of the guest peptide in the condensate depends on two events, i) its binding/unbinding to the condensate network; ii) its movement in the aqueous space between the condensate network (when it is dissociated from the condensate network) in the condensed phase. Based on our calculation of the guest peptide lifetime in the bound and dissociated state, it takes on average 7.6, 2.5, and 1.2 μs (lifetime of the bound state) for Arg-rich, ADMA-rich, and Lys-rich guest peptides to dissociate from the condensate network, respectively. However, it only takes the free Arg-rich, ADMA-rich, and Lys-rich guest peptides on average 0.0555, 0.1135, 0.0771 μs (lifetime of the dissociated state), respectively, to find its next binding partner in the condensate. This can be explained by the high concentration of the condensate network, which creates a crowded environment (SI Fig. 5b).

  Overall, we believe that the binding/unbinding between the guest peptide is rate limiting in the overall diffusion process, while the translation of the guest peptide between the condensate network has minimal contribution. This also suggests that our binding affinity calculation can appropriately represents the overall diffusion of the guest peptide in condensate. The strong binding affinity of the Arg-rich peptide can be attributed to its ability to form more hydrogen bonds with the RNA phosphate compared to the ADMA-rich or Lys-rich peptides (Fig. 3a). Moreover, despite having similar number of hydrogen bonds, the ADMA-rich peptide has stronger binding affinity and slower dissociation than the Lys-rich peptide (Fig. 3c), which could be explained by its larger number of cation-π interactions (Fig. 4d). Therefore, the cation-π interaction still plays an important role in regulating peptide diffusion in the condensate. The increased binding affinity of the Arg-rich peptide to the condensate can be attributed to the significantly greater number of hydrogen bonds between Arg and the RNA phosphate compared to ADMA or Lys.

  Furthermore, we calculated the cross-correlation between the association/dissociation of the guest peptide to the condensate scaffold when interactions between the residues at the X-sites and different parts of RNA were present (SI Fig. 6). For systems A and C, where the guest peptide is Arg-rich and Lys-rich, respectively, the association/dissociation is significantly better correlated with the presence of a hydrogen bond between the X-site residue and the RNA phosphate than with the cation π interactions (SI Fig. 6). This suggests that the hydrogen bond with the RNA phosphate is more important than cation π in regulating the binding/unbinding of the guest peptide to the condensate in these two systems. Notably, the cross-correlation between the association/dissociation of the Arg-rich guest peptide is also significantly better with the presence of hydrogen bonds between the RNA phosphate and X-site residue of guest peptide compared to other interactions in all systems. This corroborates our conclusion that the hydrogen bonds between the guest peptide and the RNA phosphate contribute the most to the binding of the guest peptide to the condensate scaffold and its diffusion in the condensate. On the other hand, for system B, with the ADMA-rich guest peptide, the association/dissociation is similarly correlated with the presence of the hydrogen bond when compared with the cation π, suggesting their equal importance in regulating the binding/unbinding of the guest peptide to the condensate scaffold. This observation suggests that the cation π interaction may also play a role in regulating the diffusion of the guest peptide, consistent with our observation on the binding affinity of the ADMA-rich guest peptide to the condensate scaffold.

  Our calculations agree with reported fluorescence confocal microscopy experiments21. The movies from these experiment (with a 30-second resolution, see supporting video of Ref21 and SI Video 1 and 2) show that the Arg-rich peptide diffuses to the center of the condensate after around 60 seconds, whereas the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides diffuse to the center of the condensate after roughly 30 seconds10. More interestingly, the Arg-rich peptide appears to follow a different recruitment mechanism compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides. The Arg-rich peptide was first observed to enter the condensate after ~3.5 minutes, while the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides were recruited much faster, at ~30 seconds21. Moreover, the Arg-rich peptide recruitment occurs from a small region on the periphery of the droplet, which then spread to the center.

  Conversely, ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides are absorbed from all sides of the droplets. Gellman and coworkers hypothesized that the Arg-rich peptides could associate with residual RNA in the solution to preassemble liquid condensates or large aggregates before being recruited into the droplet, whereas the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides could not form similar condensates or aggregates under the experimental condition21. The ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides may enter the pre-formed droplet as single molecules or smaller aggregates. This would explain the slow and localized entry of Arg-rich peptides and the fast and uniform entry of ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides into the droplets. Although we did not directly simulate the recruitment of the guest peptide into the condensate, our MD simulations can support this hypothesis, as we observe that the Arg-rich peptide has a higher tendency to associate with the RNA compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides (Fig. 2b–d, 4c–d).

  We find that the Arg-rich peptide, which diffuses the slowest in the condensate, can form a greater number of hydrogen bonds with RNA compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides. Although a cation-π interaction can be as strong as a hydrogen bond51, an Arg residue can form multiple hydrogen bonds with RNA. As a result, we believe that the multivalent hydrogen bonds that Arg can form with RNA contribute more to the slow diffusion rate of the Arg-rich peptide in the condensate compared to cation-π interactions. These multivalent hydrogen bonds contribute to the stronger binding affinity observed for the Arg-rich peptide to the condensate compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides. Nevertheless, our results show that the cation-π interaction may play a secondary role in regulating the diffusion of the guest peptide in the condensate. Our all-atom MD simulations of the condensed phase have provided molecular insight into the diffusion of guest peptides in the condensate. In the future, we expect that CG-MD simulations will be useful to observe condensate formation of a peptide and RNA mixture involving a larger system and longer RNA chains52–54. The molecular details could be obtained from the reconstruction of the conformations from the initial CG-MD sampling to all-atom structures. A similar approach has been employed to study liquid condensates involving the FUS or LAF-1 protein55. These simulations will be invaluable in understanding the driving forces of condensate formation.35


## Conclusion
  In this work, we performed sub-millisecond all-atom MD simulations, with a cumulative simulation time of 564.7μs, to investigate the binding affinity and diffusion dynamics of three guest peptides (i.e., Arg-rich, ADMA-rich, and Lys-rich peptide) in a protein-RNA condensate. We find that the Arg-rich peptide forms a greater number of hydrogen bonds compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides, leading to stronger binding affinity of the Arg-rich peptide. As a result, the Arg-rich peptide diffuses more slowly in the condensate compared to the ADMA-rich and Lys-rich peptides. We also examined the role of cation-π interactions in peptide binding to the condensate and found that the number of cation-π interactions between the RNA bases and Arg is lower compared to ADMA. These results indicate that the multivalent hydrogen bonds between Arg and RNA phosphates are the main contributors to the slow diffusion rate of the Arg-rich peptide in the condensate, compared to the cation-π interactions. More interestingly, we find that the ADMA-rich peptide diffuses more slowly in the condensate compared to the Lys-rich peptide, despite having similar numbers of hydrogen bonds. We show that this difference is due to the larger number of cation-π interactions for the ADMA-rich peptide relative to the Lys-rich peptide. Thus, our simulations suggest that cation-π interactions may play a secondary role in regulating the diffusion of the guest peptides in the condensate. Our MD simulations are consistent with a previous experimental study21 and provide invaluable in understanding the factors that control guest protein diffusion in protein-RNA condensates.
