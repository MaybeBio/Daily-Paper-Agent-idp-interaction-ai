# Backbone Interactions and Secondary Structures in Phase Separation of Disordered Proteins


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) are one of the major drivers underneath the formation and characteristics of biomolecular condensates. Due to their inherent flexibility, the backbones of IDPs are significantly exposed, rendering them highly influential in and susceptible to biomolecular phase separation. In densely packed condensates, exposed backbones have a heightened capacity to interact with neighboring protein chains, which might lead to strong coupling between the secondary structures and phase separation and further modulate the subsequent transitions of the condensates, such as aging and fibrillization. In this mini-review, we provide an overview of backbone-mediated interactions and secondary structures within biomolecular condensates to underscore the importance of protein backbones in phase separation. We further focus on recent advances in experimental techniques and molecular dynamics simulation methods for probing and exploring the roles of backbone interactions and secondary structures in biomolecular phase separation involving IDPs.


## Introduction
  Biomolecular condensates are an important mechanism of subcellular compartmentalization and are closely associated with numerous cellular activities [1–5] and human diseases [6–12]. They are characterized by liquid-like droplets of concentrated proteins and often nucleic acids, among which intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) or regions (IDRs) have been shown to be an important player in their formation through biomolecular phase separation [13–19]. Besides being disordered, the ability of IDPs to form multiple weak interactions with themselves and other molecules, known as multivalency, is central to their frequent roles as the scaffolds of the condensates. The protein backbone and side chains can engage in diverse interactions, including electrostatic and polar interactions, π-interactions, hydrogen bond interactions, and hydrophobic interactions, among others [20]. Much progress has been made in delineating the contributions and balance of various types of interactions in governing phase separation, towards a quantitative understanding of the role that sequence, post-translational modifications, and cellular environments play in the regulation of biomolecular condensates [20–25].

  The most prominent and arguably the defining feature of a protein is the peptide backbone. The interactions and intrinsic conformational properties of the backbone shape the space of possible structures a protein may have, such as α-helices and β-sheets [26]. Backbone interactions alone can provide a sufficient force to drive the collapse of unfolded protein chains [27]. The reason is that water is not a good solvent for the protein backbone; even polar IDPs such as polyglutamine and glycine-serine block co-polypeptide exist as compact but disordered globular structures in water [28, 29]. Sidechains modulate the peptide chain expansion by lowering the effective concentration of backbone amides and further through hydration and sidechain-mediated interactions [30]. Importantly, it has been shown that the single-chain dimension of an IDP is a strong indicator of its phase separation propensity [31, 32]. As such, the backbone can be expected to play vital roles in shaping intermolecular interactions that drive biomolecular phase transitions.

  IDPs remain highly flexible and sample dynamic conformations in biomolecular condensates [33–35]. With increased peptide concentration, the exposed backbone can be expected to play an even larger role in mediating intermolecular interactions and modulating the physical properties of the condensates. At the same time, the conformational properties of IDPs, such as chain expansion and transient local structures, would be reshaped by phase transitions, especially during subsequent transformations from the initial liquid-like states [36–40]. For instance, Raman spectroscopy revealed a helix-to-β-sheet transition during the aging of condensates formed by the TDP-43 C-terminal domain (CTD) [40]. Low-complexity aromatic-rich kinked segments (LARKS) in fused in sarcoma (FUS), heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (hnRNPA1), and nucleoporin 98 (nup98) can form cross-β structures during gelation, where the cross-β sheets are facilitated by the van der Waals and hydrogen-bond interactions among backbones and stabilized by hydrophobic side chains [41]. At the same time, the dynamics of the backbone prevents the excessive burial of interface areas, allowing these cross-β structures reversible and dynamic. These findings highlight the importance of protein backbones in bimolecular condensates.

  This mini-review mainly focuses on the roles of backbone in biomolecular condensates. We highlight the backbone-mediated interactions in driving biomolecular phase separation and discuss the coupling between secondary structure and phase separation. We also provide an overview of the ongoing efforts in probing backbone interactions and structures, with a special focus on the role of molecular dynamics simulations in providing crucial molecular-level insights.


## Backbone-mediated interactions in biomolecular phase separation
  Compared to side chains, the protein backbone exhibits a simple and generic chemistry independent of the amino acid sequence, characterized by a series of alpha-carbons joined by peptide bonds. Consequently, the types of backbone-mediated interactions are relatively limited and mainly involve the peptide bond itself, including hydrogen bonding and various π-interactions (Figure 1). In addition, the self-interactions of backbones set the background against side chain-mediated interactions and are a key factor that controls the peptide chain collapse and association in phase separation.


### Hydrogen-bond interactions.
  Each peptide bond contains a carbonyl group (C=O), which is a good hydrogen-bond acceptor, and, except for proline, an N-H group, which is a good hydrogen-bond donor. Through interacting with each other and with water and functional groups from side chains, the backbone is rich in hydrogen-bonding potential. Within the densely packed condensates, the backbones of IDPs are primed to form the backbone and backbone-sidechain hydrogen bonds, which are important for the self-association and phase separation of IDPs [42–44]. For example, even each individual backbone hydrogen bond has been shown to have a great effect on the self-association of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) [44]. Within the region of residues 316–339 of the low-complexity domain (LCD), the elimination of single hydrogen bonds can reproducibly yield phenotypic deficits of droplets, indicating decreased phase separation propensity. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of glycine-mediated hydrogen bonds in phase separation, especially for IDPs featuring Gly-rich regions or RGG/RG motifs [42, 43]. In the study of GK-16, a small peptide related to mussel foot proteins, the association of the peptide is primarily driven by backbone hydrogen bonds, with up to 50% occupancy [42]. In addition, backbone-mediated hydrogen bonds contribute significantly to the incorporation of nucleic acids [45, 46] and the formation of transient secondary structures [36, 41, 47] in biomolecular condensates.


### π-interactions.
  π-interactions arise between sp2-hybridized groups, commonly found in aromatic rings. Backbone amide groups are also sp2-hybridized and can engage in π-sp2 interaction with aromatic side chains, or sp2-sp2 interactions with themselves or side-chain amide, carboxyl, or guanidinium groups. Therefore, π-interactions have the potential to contribute to phase separation. Through characterizing the frequency and correlations of π-interactions in a non-redundant protein set from the RCSB protein data bank, Vernon et al. suggested that backbone-mediated π-interactions are widespread across all 20 types of residues and critical to IDP phase separation [48]. These interactions were particularly prominent in smaller residues including Gly, Ser, Thr, and Pro, where the backbone peptide bonds were more exposed. Several other studies exploring the molecular force driving phase separation also support that backbone amide groups frequently participate in π-interactions with backbones or side chains, albeit with varying degrees of influence on the phase separation process [35, 43, 49, 50].


### Other effects.
  Polyglycine or oligoglycine peptides are utilized as model peptides to evaluate the solvent quality of water for protein backbones and study unfolded protein conformational preferences. Both experimental and simulation results reveal the formation of collapsed globular structures for polyglycines in the aqueous solvents, indicating that water serves as a comparatively poor solvent for polypeptide backbones [27, 29]. Surprisingly, even polyglutamines, abundant in hydrophilic side chains, exhibit collapsed structures in aqueous solutions [51]. The primary driving force behind the collapse of protein backbones remains debatable. Some researchers propose intrapeptide hydrogen bonds as the main factor [27, 52], while others emphasize C=O dipole-dipole interactions along the backbone [53, 54]. In either case, interactions within backbones are favored over those between the backbone and water. Several reports have demonstrated that the degree of single-chain collapse of an IDP is strongly correlated to its phase separation behavior and that the coil-to-globule transition is an effective proxy for predicting the phase diagram of protein phase separation [32, 55, 56]. Therefore, the inherent conformational preference and self-interaction of the backbone are key factors governing the phase separation of IDPs. Understanding how the sequence affects IDP phase separation thus must consider the global consequence on the peptide chain conformation, in addition to direct side chain-mediated interactions.


### Probing backbone interactions within condensates.
  The condensates consist of crowded biomolecules, have high viscosity, and often display viscoelastic properties [57–59]. For some condensates, molecules within dense phase are in a dynamic exchange with the dilute phase, reflecting a transient and dynamic nature of intermolecular interactions. It’s challenging to probe backbone interactions within such a heterogeneous and dynamic environment experimentally. So far, NMR spectroscopy has emerged as a leading technique to measure transient interactions of IDPs, which can provide atomic-level information on highly dynamic systems including biomolecular condensates [60]. Chemical shift perturbations and two-dimensional heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) have been used to map the residue-level interactions within condensates, where the interactions are detected through the changes in the position or intensity of the resonances [61–63]. Detailed information on specific interactions can be further probed by the nuclear Overhauser effects (NOEs), which are sensitive to short-distance interactions within ~6 Å [43, 50, 64]. For example, by measuring NOEs between amide protons and other hydrogen atoms, strong intermolecular interactions between the aromatic side-chain and backbone amides (hydrogen bond and/or π-sp2 interactions) are found to be present in the condensed phase of the cell cycle associated protein 1 (CARPIN1) [65]. The 2D 13C-HSQC-NOESY-15N-HSQC experiment has revealed interactions between the backbone and 15N-attached side chains between the glycine backbone and the arginine side chain (hydrogen bond and/or sp2-sp2 interactions) [43].

  N-methylation is one of the most important chemical modifications to regulate biological functions [66], which can also provide valuable insights into the contributions of various interactions to phase separation [44, 67, 68]. In particular, Zhou et al. have utilized it to assess the importance of backbone hydrogen bonds [44]. In the region of residues 316 to 339 (except for Pro) of TDP-43 LCD, the hydrogen on each individual backbone nitrogen atom was replaced by the methyl group to prohibit the formation of Nα-H based hydrogen bonds. Subsequent turbidity measurements by the absorbance at 600 nm and light microscopic imaging for phase separation of these Nα-methylated TDP-43 LCD variants demonstrated that decreasing the backbone hydrogen-bonding capacity in LCD always weakened protein self-association and phase separation.


## Secondary structures in IDP phase separation
  IDPs are not random polymers. Instead, they often contain transient local and long-range structure features [69–71]. On one hand, these structural features can be expected to impact IDP phase separation propensity, as their presence modulates the global chain collapse and availability of the backbone for intermolecular interactions. On the other hand, the drastic environmental differences between the dilute and condensed phases suggest a strong likelihood of IDP conformational responses upon phase separation. Furthermore, post-translational modifications frequently affect the structure and dynamics of IDPs and contribute to their phase separation [21, 72–75]. There is thus an important need to understand the coupling of transient structures and phase separation of IDPs. Yet, the question of how secondary structure relates to phase separation remains open (Figure 2). Some reports suggest that phase separation can promote the folding of IDPs, while others have reported no discernible difference between structures in dilute and condensed phases.


### Does phase separation promote β-structure formation?
  It’s suggested that phase-separated droplets can further facilitate gelation or fibrillation to form labile fibrils and hydrogels through aging [76]. Several studies have reported the formation of β-structures in this aging process, for example in the fibrillation of hnRNPA1 disease-causing mutant and the gelation of hnRNPA2 [38, 39, 77]. Upon aging for 4 h, conformations with increased β-sheet structures are observed in the droplet of TDP-43 CTD, which continue to increase in the following 20 h of aging [40]. Recently, short LARKS motifs have been identified within LCDs of RNA-binding proteins that can form cross-β structures in the hydrogels [36, 41]. These observations indicate that phase separation can promote the formation of β-structures. However, to date, few works demonstrated the presence of β-sheet within liquid-like droplets, although some β-turns have been reported [37, 78–80]. In contrast, another set of studies have suggested that IDPs within condensates remain entirely disordered [50, 64, 68, 81, 82]. In particular, NMR spectra of phase-separated proteins show high similarity with those of monomeric and dispersed proteins in the dilute phase. However, β-structures may exist with low population and have short life-times in the condensate, which might be hard to be detect by NMR.


### Do transient helices promote phase separation?
  Transient helices are the most common residual structures observed in IDPs and can be quantified using NMR. It has been the main focus of limited studies of the roles of residual structures in phase separation. The most prominent example is TDP-43, which exhibits strong residual helicity (~ 50%) in the conserved region of the C-terminal domain spanning from residues 320–343, which has been suggested to mediate intermolecular α-helical contacts and enhance TDP-43 phase-separation [47]. Further study shows a strong correlation between residual helicity and phase separation propensity, where helix-enhancing mutants decrease the saturation concentration [83]. In contrast, reduced helicity in several fungal protein Whi3 mutants has been shown to drive phase separation [84]. It was suggested that increasing helicity promotes the oligomerization of Whi3 in the dilute phase to weaken the propensity of phase separation. Clearly, there is an intricate relationship between residual structures and phase separation. The impact of residual helices (or other transient structures) needs to be understood in the specific context of how they may perturb various backbone and side chain-mediated interactions and hydration properties in the monomeric, oligomeric and phase-separated states.


### Probe secondary structures within condensates.
  Once again, NMR has been the most important approach to probing secondary structures of IDPs within both dilute and condensed phases, capable of providing residue-level structural information. The presence of transient local structures can be assessed through chemical shifts, in particular those of backbones. The difference between the observed chemical shifts and corresponding random coil values, known as the secondary chemical shifts, can be used to identify and estimate transient secondary structures in disordered proteins within condensates (reviewed in Ref. [60]). Besides, circular dichroism (CD) [78, 79], Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) [79, 85], and Raman spectroscopy [40, 80, 82] have also been used to detect the overall levels of secondary structures. For example, random or disordered structures exhibit a CD minimum at ~195 nm, while a single minimum of ~220 nm indicates β-structures and double minima around ~210 nm and ~222 nm report α-helices [78, 79]. For FTIR and Raman spectroscopy, the amide-I region (1600–1700 cm−1), which corresponds to C=O vibrations, is the most sensitive part for determining the secondary structures. In detail, regions 1644–1650 cm−1, 1655–1668 cm−1, 1670–1682 cm−1, and 1683–1685 cm−1 are assigned to coils, α-helix, β-sheet, and β-turn, respectively [86–90]. Therefore, the changes within these regions can be indicative of the formation or transition of secondary structures.


## Molecular dynamics simulations of phase separation
  Compared to experimental approaches, computational modelling like molecular dynamics (MD) simulations could provide superior spatial and time resolutions for understanding the molecular basis of the thermodynamic, structural, and dynamic properties of biomolecular phase separation [33, 91–98]. A critical challenge, however, is that the phase separation involves highly crowded proteins or peptides and occurs on large length and time scales not readily accessible to traditional atomistic simulations, even with important advances in GPU-accelerated algorithms [99–102] and special-purposed computing hardware [103]. As such, the application of all-atom MD simulations in studying phase separation is usually restricted to small model peptides [104–106]. For example, Rauscher et al. simulated a model condensate containing 27 copies of elastin-like peptides, (GVPGV)7, and observed that the peptide backbone formed transient and sparse hydrogen-bonded turns but individual chains were maximally disordered [105]. Galvanetto et al. performed ultra-large-scale condensate simulations containing 96 prothymosin-α and 80 histone H1 molecules, which included roughly 4 million atoms and a total simulation time of 6 μs [34]. It was suggested that there was enough sampling to obtain a meaningful comparison with experiments on diffusion constants and average FRET distances and reconfiguration times [34]. The simulations revealed important clues to the apparent discrepancy between high viscosity and sub-μs chain reconfiguration times, including the similarity between the heterodimer interface in the dilute phase and interchain contacts in the condensed phase, as well as short lifetimes of interchain contacts.

  To date, most condensate simulations relied on coarse-grained (CG) models, which drastically reduce the system size and at the same time deploy smooth effective interaction potentials. Numerous CG models have been developed for simulating phase separation of IDPs, including the HPS [107–109], HPS+π [110], FB-HPS [111], HPSx-mW/2021 [112], MOFF [113], rebalanced MARTINI [114], CALVADOS [115, 116], and Mpipi model [117]. Except for MARTINI, the majority of these models are Cα-only models where each residue is represented by a single CG bead. However, due to the lack of an explicit description of backbones, most CG models are not suited to reproduce the local secondary structures of IDPs or backbone-mediated interactions [92]. To this end, we have developed the hybrid resolution (HyRes) CG model [118–120], which contains an atomistic description of the backbone with intermediate resolution of side chains. The HyRes model shows a good performance in reproducing transient secondary structures of IDPs and their dynamic interactions with folded proteins [121]. It was recently shown to be suitable for simulating spontaneous phase separation of IDPs and could capture the effects of single mutations on a short 23-residue low complexity peptide, GY-23 derived from the squid-beak derived protein HBP-1 [122].

  For TDP-43 CTD, HyRes simulations successfully recapitulated the apparent correlation between residual helicity and phase separation propensity of alanine and proline mutants [122]. A lesson, though, is that residual helicity may not contribute directly to phase separation by directly promoting helix-helix interactions in the condensed phase. In particular, the probabilities of helix-helix contacts are strictly proportional to the residue helicity, and there is no apparent cooperativity of helix-mediated contacts. Two artificial “helicity” mutants were designed to further analyze how side chain and backbone interactions contribute to TDP-43 CTD phase separation, where dihedral restraints were imposed to artificially increase the helicity of wild-type (WT) protein to the same level of G335A, or reduce the helicity of G335A to the level of WT. The analysis revealed that residual helicity appeared to play an indirect role in affecting TDP-43 CTD phase separation, by modulating the availability of backbone for intermolecular interactions. The apparent correlation between residual helicity and phase separation propensity of TDP-43 CTD variants results from nontrivial effects on the balance between backbone and sidechain-mediated interactions. Once further optimized, such as using the CALVADOS protocol [115, 116], HyRes may represent a viable tool for efficient exploration of the interplay between residual structures and phase separation of IDPs.

  To reduce the computational cost but retain the atomistic precision, a promising approach involves integrating both all-atom and CG simulations [35, 91]. Here, CG simulations could be first employed to generate equilibrated configurations of the condensate, which are back-mapped to atomistic ones for the subsequent all-atom MD simulations. For example, Zheng et al. reconstructed an atomistic condensate of 40 FUS LCD or LAF-1 RGG from the conformation obtained from simulations based on the HPS CG model [35]. The subsequent all-atom simulations revealed more details about the underlying molecular interactions within the condensate. An important limitation of such an approach is that atomistic simulations of limited timescales will not be able to sample large conformational changes not captured by CG simulations, such as condensation-induced secondary structural transitions. The utility of the hybrid all-atom/CG approach is thus limited.
