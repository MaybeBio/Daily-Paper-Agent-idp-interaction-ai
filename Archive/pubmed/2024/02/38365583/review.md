## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, background, methods, results, discussion, conclusions) as provided.
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific and technical evaluation of the proposed GLCN-DTA model, its methodological novelty, experimental validation, and reported results. Editorial and stylistic aspects are considered only insofar as they affect clarity and reproducibility.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose GLCN-DTA, a graph-based deep learning model for drug-target affinity prediction. The model integrates a graph learning module that learns a soft adjacency matrix, replacing the fixed adjacency matrix used in prior graph-based approaches, to refine the structure of drug and protein graphs. The authors claim improved accuracy and robustness over existing methods on four benchmark datasets (Davis, KIBA, Metz, ToxCast).
- **Visible evidence base** Full text of the manuscript, including methods description, mathematical formulations, hyperparameter settings, and results tables (Tables 5-10) and figures (Figures 1-5).
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Code availability is not stated. Details on dataset preprocessing, train/validation/test splits (beyond counts), and reproducibility of the Pconsc4 contact map generation are not fully described. Statistical significance testing for performance differences is not reported. Details on the baseline implementations and hyperparameter tuning for baselines are not provided.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a relevant problem in drug-target affinity prediction and proposes a reasonable extension of existing graph-based methods by incorporating a learnable soft adjacency matrix. The idea is not entirely novel, as similar graph learning mechanisms have been explored in other domains, but its application to DTA prediction with both drug and protein graphs is a sensible contribution. The experimental evaluation covers multiple datasets and includes ablation studies. However, the reported improvements over baselines are marginal in some cases, and the manuscript lacks critical details on experimental setup, statistical validation, and reproducibility. The claims of "robustness" and "high accuracy" are not fully supported by the evidence presented. The manuscript is clearly written but would benefit from a more rigorous evaluation and discussion of limitations.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in computational drug discovery, bioinformatics, and machine learning applied to chemistry and biology. The work is of interest to those developing graph-based models for molecular property prediction and those seeking to improve DTA prediction accuracy for virtual screening applications.
- **Major strengths**
    1.  The problem is relevant and well-motivated.
    2.  The proposed method is clearly described and integrates a reasonable mechanism (learnable soft adjacency) into an established architecture.
    3.  Evaluation on four datasets, including two less commonly used ones (Metz, ToxCast), is a positive aspect.
    4.  Ablation studies on key hyperparameters provide some insight into model behavior.
- **Major Concerns**
    - **R1-M1** (Blocking: Yes): The reported performance improvements over baselines are marginal and lack statistical validation. For example, on the Davis dataset, the MSE improvement over the best baseline (0.215 vs 0.215 for WGNN-DTA) is negligible, and the CI improvement is 0.003. On KIBA, the MSE improvement is 0.013 over SubMDTA. Without error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests (e.g., paired t-test or Wilcoxon), it is impossible to determine if these differences are significant. This is a critical issue for a paper whose primary claim is improved accuracy.
    - **R1-M2** (Blocking: Yes): The manuscript lacks sufficient detail on the experimental setup to ensure reproducibility. Specifically, the exact train/validation/test split ratios (beyond the counts in Table 3), the random seed(s) used, the data preprocessing steps (e.g., how SMILES were canonicalized, how protein sequences were filtered), and the hyperparameter tuning procedure for the proposed model and all baselines are not described. The statement "each dataset is partitioned into various parts" is vague. Without this information, the results cannot be independently verified.
    - **R1-M3** (Blocking: No): The novelty of the graph learning module is limited. The soft adjacency matrix learning mechanism is directly inspired by prior work (references [57, 58]) and is applied here to drug and protein graphs. The authors should more clearly articulate the specific challenges in DTA prediction that this mechanism uniquely addresses, beyond a general statement of "richer representations." A deeper analysis of what the learned adjacency matrices represent (e.g., are they capturing meaningful chemical or structural interactions?) would strengthen the contribution.
    - **R1-M4** (Blocking: No): The comparison with baselines is incomplete. The authors compare against a limited set of methods, and for some (e.g., DGraphDTA, WGNN-DTA), they cite results from a secondary source [5] rather than running the methods themselves. This introduces potential inconsistencies in experimental conditions. A more thorough comparison, ideally with re-implemented baselines under identical conditions, is needed. Additionally, the absence of recent state-of-the-art methods (e.g., transformer-based models) in the comparison is a concern.
- **Minor Comments**
    - **R1-m1** (Axis: Clarity): The Background section is somewhat repetitive, with the problem statement and the proposed solution being restated multiple times. The text could be streamlined.
    - **R1-m2** (Axis: Reproducibility): The authors state that the protein contact map is generated using "established methods such as Pconsc4" but do not provide the specific version, parameters, or a reference for the exact implementation used. This needs to be specified.
    - **R1-m3** (Axis: Presentation): In Table 9, the results for the "w/o graph learning" ablation are presented as relative decreases (e.g., "↓0.03"). It would be clearer to present the absolute values of the metrics for both the full model and the ablated version.
    - **R1-m4** (Axis: Clarity): The description of the graph learning module's loss function (Eq. 3) is brief. The authors should explain the intuition behind the exponential term and the Frobenius norm penalty more clearly, and how the hyperparameters η and γ were chosen.
    - **R1-m5** (Axis: Completeness): The authors mention that the model does not distinguish between ordered and disordered proteins and that this may limit applicability. This is a valid point, but it is only discussed in the conclusion. A more detailed discussion of this limitation in the context of the results (e.g., performance on datasets with different protein types) would be valuable.
    - **R1-m6** (Axis: Technical): The authors state that a two-layer GLCN is used "by default." It would be useful to show the impact of varying the number of layers in the main results, not just in the ablation study, to justify this choice.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
    - Lack of statistical significance testing for all reported performance comparisons.
    - Insufficient detail on data splits, preprocessing, and hyperparameter tuning for reproducibility.
    - Incomplete baseline comparison, with some results taken from secondary sources.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
    - **Originality**: Moderate. The application of a learnable adjacency matrix to DTA prediction is an incremental extension of existing graph learning techniques. The core idea is not new, but its specific application context is somewhat different.
    - **Scientific importance**: Moderate. Improving DTA prediction is important, but the demonstrated improvements are marginal and not rigorously validated. The potential impact is limited by the lack of clear evidence for a significant advance over the state of the art.
    - **Interdisciplinary readership**: The topic is of interest to a specialized audience in computational biology and cheminformatics. The methods are not likely to attract a broad readership beyond this community.
    - **Technical soundness**: The proposed architecture is technically sound, but the experimental validation is insufficient to support the claims. The lack of statistical tests and incomplete baseline comparisons are significant weaknesses.
    - **Readability for nonspecialists**: The manuscript is generally well-written and the method is explained clearly. However, the motivation and the specific advantages of the proposed approach could be articulated more effectively for a broader audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The manuscript addresses a relevant problem and presents a reasonable method, but the core claims of improved accuracy and robustness are not convincingly supported due to the lack of statistical validation and incomplete experimental details. Major revisions are required to address these concerns.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that GLCN-DTA possesses "advantages in terms of robustness and high accuracy" is not fully supported, as the performance gains over baselines are marginal and not statistically validated.
- The claim that the graph learning module "efficiently refines the graph's structure and filters useless nodes" is not directly evidenced by any analysis of the learned adjacency matrices.
- The claim that the model is "robust to complex structures" is not tested, e.g., through experiments on noisy or incomplete data.
- The generalizability of the model to unseen protein classes (e.g., intrinsically disordered proteins) is acknowledged as a limitation but not explored.
