## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses an important and underexplored question: the active role of DNA in modulating the conformational dynamics and oligomeric state of a transcription factor. The combination of single-molecule spectroscopy and molecular dynamics is appropriate and potentially powerful. However, the evidence as presented in the text is incomplete in several key areas. The central claim that DNA destabilizes the dimeric state relies on indirect measurements (FRET populations, anisotropy) and a single biochemical assay whose details are not fully described. The interpretation of the FRET and anisotropy data in terms of "folded" versus "disordered" states is plausible but not rigorously established, particularly given the known complexities of dye dynamics and local flexibility. The manuscript would be strengthened by clearer presentation of the quantitative analysis, explicit controls, and a more direct demonstration of the proposed negative allosteric mechanism.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of transcription factor biology, protein dynamics, intrinsically disordered proteins, and single-molecule biophysics. The work challenges the conventional "folding-upon-binding" paradigm and proposes a new regulatory mechanism where DNA acts as a negative allosteric modulator of protein dimerization. This could have implications for understanding FoxP-related diseases and for the broader understanding of how DNA sequences can influence protein conformational landscapes.
- **Major strengths** 1) The study addresses a significant and timely question about the bidirectional influence between DNA and transcription factor structure. 2) The combination of multiple single-molecule techniques (FRET, anisotropy, BVA, PDA, fFCS) with atomistic simulations is methodologically ambitious and appropriate for studying conformational dynamics. 3) The use of a binding-deficient variant (R53H) as a control in the dimerization assay is a good experimental design. 4) The authors are careful to note the limitations of simple models and acknowledge the need for more complex descriptions in some cases.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence strength for central claim
  - **Claim pointer** "DNA binding promotes a disordered fuzzy-like FKH dimer, likely directly impacting the dimer-dependent gene suppression function of FoxP proteins" and "DNA binding destabilizes the protein" (Summary, Discussion).
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "DNA binding controls the dimerization of the FKH domain", Figure 4, Table S8.
  - **Concern** The central claim that DNA binding destabilizes the dimeric state is supported primarily by a single biochemical assay (Figure 4) and by indirect single-molecule FRET measurements (Figure 3B). The biochemical assay uses a BODIPY-labeled C57 variant and an unlabeled R53H variant, but the text does not provide sufficient detail on how the dimerization state is unambiguously distinguished from monomeric states, how the stoichiometry of the labeled:unlabeled complex is verified, or how the presence of DNA affects the assay readout independently of its effect on dimerization. The FRET data show a decrease in high-FRET population for dimers in the presence of DNA, but this could be due to multiple factors, including dye orientation changes, local unfolding, or a shift in the dimer-monomer equilibrium. The authors do not provide a quantitative model linking the FRET changes to a specific change in dimerization free energy.
  - **Why it matters** The proposed mechanism of DNA-mediated negative regulation of dimerization is the main novel contribution of the paper. If this claim is not robustly supported, the significance of the work is substantially diminished.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed description of the dimerization assay, including controls demonstrating that the observed anisotropy changes are due to dimerization and not to DNA-induced conformational changes within a monomer. Include a quantitative analysis of the FRET data that explicitly models the dimer-monomer equilibrium and its shift upon DNA binding. Ideally, provide an independent measure of the dimerization affinity (e.g., by sedimentation equilibrium or a different solution-based method) in the presence and absence of DNA.

  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Interpretation of single-molecule data
  - **Claim pointer** "The monomeric form of the forkhead (FKH) domain of the human FoxP1 behaves as a disordered protein and increases its folded population when it dimerizes" (Summary).
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "The monomeric state of FoxP1's FKH domain exchanges between folded and disordered states", Figures 2B–2E, Tables S1–S5.
  - **Concern** The assignment of FRET populations to "folded" and "disordered" states relies on comparison with static and dynamic FRET lines, but the text does not provide sufficient detail on how these lines were computed, what parameters were used, and how sensitive the conclusions are to those parameters. The anisotropy data are interpreted in terms of "flexibility," but the relationship between steady-state anisotropy and backbone flexibility is not straightforward, especially for dyes attached via flexible linkers. The claim that the monomer is "disordered" is based on the absence of a high-FRET population in one variant (C18-C78) and the presence of a broad low-FRET population, but this could also be consistent with a partially folded or molten-globule state. The rxDMD simulations are mentioned but not described in sufficient detail to assess whether they support the experimental interpretation.
  - **Why it matters** The characterization of the monomeric state as disordered is foundational to the paper's narrative. If this interpretation is not robust, the subsequent claims about DNA-induced disorder are weakened.
  - **Resolution test** Provide a more detailed description of the FRET line calculations, including the parameters used and the sensitivity of the conclusions to those parameters. Show the raw FRET efficiency histograms and the fits for all conditions. For the anisotropy data, provide a more nuanced interpretation that accounts for dye dynamics. Describe the rxDMD simulation setup and analysis in more detail, and show a direct comparison between simulated and experimental observables.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that DNA binding "negatively controls the stability of the dimeric FoxP1:DNA complex" is not directly supported by the data presented. The FRET and anisotropy data show changes in conformational populations, but a direct measurement of complex stability (e.g., a dissociation constant) is not provided.
- The claim that the monomeric FKH domain "behaves as a disordered protein" is supported by the absence of a high-FRET population in one variant and by simulations, but this is an indirect inference. A more direct demonstration (e.g., by NMR or small-angle X-ray scattering) would be needed to fully establish this.
- The statement that "DNA binding promotes a disordered fuzzy-like FKH dimer" is speculative and not directly demonstrated. The data show an increase in low-FRET population, but this could be interpreted in multiple ways.
- The claim that "the DNA-mediated reversible regulation on FKH dimers suggests that FoxP1-dependent gene suppression is unstable" is an extrapolation from in vitro data to a cellular context and is not directly supported by the experiments presented.
