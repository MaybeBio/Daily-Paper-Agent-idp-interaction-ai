# Quantum defect sensitization via phase-changing supercharged antibody fragments


## Abstract
  Quantum defects in single-walled carbon nanotubes promote exciton localization that enables potential applications in biodevices and quantum light sources. However, the effects of local electric fields on the emissive energy states of quantum defects, and how they can be controlled, are unexplored. Here, we investigate quantum defect sensitization by engineering an intrinsically disordered protein to undergo a phase change at a quantum defect site. We designed a supercharged single-chain antibody fragment (scFv) to enable a full ligand-induced folding transition from an intrinsically disordered state to a compact folded state in the presence of the cytokine. The supercharged scFv was conjugated to a quantum defect to induce a substantial local electric change upon ligand binding. Employing the detection of a proinflammatory biomarker, interleukin-6, as a representative model system, supercharged scFv-coupled quantum defects exhibited robust fluorescence wavelength shifts concomitant with the protein folding transition. Quantum chemical simulations suggest that the quantum defects amplify the optical response to the localization of charges produced upon antigen-induced folding of the proteins, which is difficult to achieve in unmodified nanotubes. These findings portend new approaches to modulate quantum defect emission for biomarker sensing and studying protein biophysics and to engineer proteins to modulate binding signal transduction.


## Introduction
  Semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) are cylindrical nanostructures composed of a graphitic lattice of sp2 carbons. SWCNTs fluoresce in the near-infrared region (NIR, 800–1700 nm).1 Quantum defects are covalent functional modifications on SWCNTs that modify the otherwise crystalline semiconductors.2 Quantum defects create new fluorescence peaks (E11−) ~100-300 meV redshifted from the nanotube host emission (E11).3 The defect fluorescence introduces new chemical sensitivities on quantum defect-modified SWCNTs (QWNTs) that are determined by the chemical nature of the defect, making quantum defects the molecular focal point for chemical detection.3, 4 The quantum defect-induced fluorescence peak is extremely sensitive to the local microenvironment around the added functional group and can be systematically controlled through tailoring the functional groups.5, 6 Chemical control of local quantum defects is becoming increasingly exciting for their potential as single-photon sources for quantum computing, biodevices, and single-electron transistors.7, 8 Recent advances in protein sensing with QWNTs report that conjugation of protein-binding moieties such as fatty acids, biotin, and aptamers modulate the dielectric properties of the surrounding environment, quantified by induction-polarity parameters, that can influence defect emission.9-11 Much is still unknown, however, about the effects of local electric fields on the electronic energy states of quantum defects and how they can be controlled and produce robust signals under biologically-relevant conditions, e.g., high protein and salt concentrations.

  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) are proteins or protein domains that undergo a phase transition between disordered and folded states as part of their function.12 A subclass of IDPs is unstructured until they bind to another protein or small molecule ligand – a process termed ligand-induced folding.13 Dramatically increasing the surface net charge of a well-folded protein, known as supercharging,14 can turn proteins into ligand-induced-folders by destabilizing the folded state of the protein via charge-charge repulsion, and the additional folding energy that results from ligand binding is sufficient to drive the phase transition from an extended unliganded state to a compact folded bound state. Such modifications have been shown to improve protein solubility, stability, and reversibility,15 while greatly increasing the electric field changes associated with ligand binding.16 For these reasons, supercharged IDPs represent an attractive target for biodevices, however, they have yet to be introduced into a sensing device. One of the main challenges is that IDP supercharging has only been used on designed and natural helical bundle proteins, which have simple folding topologies and are highly designable17, while the design rules to engineer this functionality into more complex folds, such as antibodies, have yet to be determined. New approaches to engineer proteins may improve analyte binding and sensitize entities to transduce the binding as a signal readout, i.e., quantum defect emission would lead to the development of robust molecular sensors for bioanalyte detection and biophysical studies.

  Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a proinflammatory cytokine that is closely associated with multiple medical conditions. Serum IL-6 level is elevated in patients with cytokine release syndrome (CRS). CRS is an acute systemic inflammatory syndrome caused by infections, autoimmune diseases, and hematologic conditions, including sepsis.18 For instance, cytokine release syndrome is common in cancer patients receiving chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, occurring in up to 50% of patients. Close monitoring and aggressive use of anti-IL6 therapy have been integral in managing patients receiving CAR-T cell therapy.19, 20 Therefore, the development of robust biosensors would facilitate the early assessment of risk and timely measurement of disease progression.

  In this work, we investigated quantum defect responses to localized environmental changes and the potential for protein design to amplify the transduction of antigen binding (Figure 1). We designed phase-changing scFvs that recognize human IL-6 (hIL-6). The scFv was made capable of performing the full phase transition from an intrinsically disordered state to a compact folded state upon antigen binding by supercharging the scFv surface and removing internal disulfide bonds, to result in a substantially modified antibody fragment. We conjugated the supercharged scFvs to QWNTs featuring a 4-carboxylaryl defect and observed quantitative and specific wavelength shifts of quantum defect fluorescence in response to hIL-6 in serum, which was not observed from non-supercharged controls. Quantum chemical modeling indicated that the sensitivity can be attributed to the large electric field changes produced upon antigen-induced folding of the proteins in the proximity of the defects. This work demonstrated that quantum defects can be sensitized to changes in local electric fields at the defect site, in this case induced by a protein phase transition, and that antibody fragments can be engineered into IDP-like proteins, to undergo large phase transitions via supercharging.


## Experimental Section

### Supercharged scFv design.
  The structure of IL-6 in complex with a neutralizing antibody (PDB ID: 4ZS7) was used as the basis for our scFv design. A GG(SGG)7 sequence was used to link together the antibody variable regions in a heavy/light chain orientation. Disulfide bridges were replaced with alanine-leucine pairs21 to enable a full folding transition. This sequence is referred to as the wildtype anti-hIL-6 scFv, α-IL6wt. Using the solved crystal structure as a reference, solvent-exposed residues on beta strands that were not involved in antigen binding or at the interface between variable regions were chosen for mutation to aspartic acid. The introduction of these mutations changed the overall net charge of the scFv from −2 to −25. This sequence is referred to as the supercharged anti-hIL-6 scFv, α-IL6sc.


### Protein purification.
  scFvs were expressed as insoluble inclusion bodies in NiCo(DE3) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) e. coli cells in the pET28a vector (EMD Biosciences Inc., Darmstadt, Germany). Cells were grown at 37 °C until an optical density at 600 nm. of 1 was reached, after which the temperature was dropped to 18 °C, 0.5 mM IPTG (GoldBio Inc., St. Louis, MO) was added, and the cells were incubated overnight. Cell pellets were harvested and resuspended in 50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM sodium chloride, 10 mM imidazole pH 8 (Buffer A). After adding 50 ug/mL DNAse and AEBSF, cells were disrupted via rench press and centrifuged at 17,000 g for 30 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the remaining fraction was resuspended in 50 mM sodium phosphate, 8 M guanidinium hydrochloride pH 8 (Buffer B). Cells underwent three rounds of freeze-thaw cycles and were centrifuged at 17,000 g for 30 minutes. The supernatant was added to Ni-NTA beads, washed with Buffer B, and eluted with 50 mM sodium phosphate, 8 M guanidinium hydrochloride pH 4.5 Proteins were then dialyzed overnight into 20 mM Tris, 100 mM sodium chloride, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT pH 8. TEV protease was added to the proteins, which had undergone partial precipitation, and the reaction was allowed to occur at room temperature for 5 hours. The sample was centrifuged at 5,000 g for 10 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitated protein was resuspended in Buffer B. Samples were again incubated with Ni-NTA beads and the flow-thru was collected. Proteins were further purified and refolded in a single step on a superose 12 gel filtration column (50 mM phosphate, 500 mM NaCl pH 8. Column was preloaded with a bolus of 50 mM phosphate, 500 mM NaCl, 1 M arginine pH 8. hIL-6 was expressed as an MBP-fusion protein in an identical manner as the scFvs. Cell pellets were resuspended in Buffer A, lysed via 3 rench press, centrifuged at 17,000 g for 30 minutes and the supernatant was loaded onto Ni-NTA beads. Proteins were eluted using 50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM sodium chloride, and 250 mM imidazole at pH 8. TEV protease and 1 mM DTT were added and incubated overnight at 4 degrees C. Proteins were dialyzed into Buffer A and reloaded onto Ni-NTA beads. The flowthrough was collected and purified using a superose 12 gel filtration column.


### Circular dichroism.
  Circular dichroism spectra were recorded with an Aviv Biomedical Circular Dichroism Spectrometer Model 400 in a 0.2 cm light path quartz cell, using a bandwidth of 1–2 nm, an averaging time of 2 s, and averaged over two scans. Because of the high sodium chloride concentrations, spectra were recorded between 215 nm and 250 nm, with background correction.


### Microscale thermophoresis.
  Human IL-6 was fluorescently labeled using fluorescein NHS according to manufacturer instructions: The labeling reaction was carried out in 50 mM borate, 100 mM sodium chloride, pH 8.5. Unreacted dye was removed by dialysis and a G-25 desalting column. MST experiments were performed in 50 mM sodium phosphate pH 8 using a NanoTemper Monolith NT.115 and standard capillaries. The concentrations of scFv were determined by UV absorbance. Data was analyzed with PALMIST.22


### Intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence measurements.
  We measured salt-induced folding using a DM45 spectrofluorometer (Olis Inc. Athens, GA). Samples were excited at 280 nm and emission measured at 348 nm. Slit widths were 3.06 nm. 100 data points were collected for each scan with a 0.5 sec integration time. Five scans were performed for each NaCl concentration and averaged to the final value. Measurements were performed in 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8 with varying concentrations of NaCl. Fluorescence intensity as a function of NaCl was fit with Equation 1, modified from that of Santoro and Bolen:23  (1) I=(IF0+mF∗[NaCl])+(IU0+mU∗[NaCl]∗e−ΔGfolding,0−m∗[NaCl]RT)1+e−ΔGfolding,0−m∗[NaCl]RT

  Where IF0 and IU0 refer to the fluorescence intensity of the folded and unfolded states at zero [NaCl], mU and mF account for the slopes of the pre- and post-transition regions. ΔGfolding,0 is the free energy of folding at 0 [NaCl] and m is the dependence of the free energy of folding on NaCl activity. This equation assumes a linear dependence of the folding free energy on αNaCl over the experimental range of 0.1 – 4.0 M. This has been shown to be true for several cations in the case of the designed heme binding protein H4(−24) over the cation activity range at which the average acidic side chain-side chain distance is significantly shorter than the Debye length.24


### Purification of (6,5)-SWCNT.
  Raw SWCNT material, CoMoCAT SG65i (Sigma-Aldrich) was dispersed in 1 wt/v% sodium deoxycholate (DOC, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) aqueous solution at a nanotube concentration of 1 mg/mL using tip-sonication at 6W (Sonics & Materials, Inc) and 4 °C for 1 hour, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 30 min. The 85% supernatant was used to obtain (6,5) enriched SWCNT solution based on the previously reported protocol.25 The final purified (6,5) SWCNTs were stabilized in 1.04% DOC solution to maintain long-term colloidal stability.


### Covalent functionalization of purified (6,5)-SWCNT.
  4-carboxylbenzene tetrafluoroborate was freshly synthesized from 4-aminobenzoic acid (≥99%, Sigma Aldrich) and nitrous acid.3 The synthesized diazonium salts were added to the aqueous solution of purified (6,5) enriched SWCNTs in 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, ≥99.0%, Sigma Aldrich) at the diazonium salt to carbon of (6,5)-SWCNT molar ratio of 1 to 275. The SWCNT and diazonium mixture was illuminated with a mercury arc lamp (X-Cite 120Q, Excelitas) at room temperature. After 20 minutes of illumination, the QWNT solution was filtrated through Cytiva Vivaspin® 20 filters (100kDa MWCO) to remove unreacted diazonium salts and concentrate the QWNT solution for bioconjugation.


### Protein conjugation to 4-carboxylaryl QWNT.
  The QWNTs in 1% SDS were reacted with N-Ethyl-N’-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, ≥99%, Sigma Aldrich) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, >98%, Sigma Aldrich) in a [quantum defects]:[EDC]:[NHS] ratio of 1:10:25 for 30 minutes at room temperature. 1.2uL of β-mercaptoethanol was added to the solution to quench unreacted EDC. After 15 minutes, the solution was diluted 10 times using 1 wt/v % DOC, ultrafiltrated by AMICON filters (100kDa MWCO), and diluted with 1XPBS to adjust the final concentration of DOC to 0.1%. Purified scFv solution in 1XPBS was added in a ratio [sulfo-NHS ester-aryl defect]:[scFv]=1:10. Solution was reacted for 3 hours on a rotator at 4 °C. The resulting solution was dialyzed in a dialysis device (Spectrum™ Spectra/Por™ Float-A-Lyzer™ G2, 1000kDa MWCO) using 0.1% DOC in 1XPBS at 4°C to reduce free scFvs in the solution by a factor of 10.


### Near-infrared fluorescence spectroscopy of scFv-QWNTs.
  Fluorescence emission spectra of (6,5)-SWCNTs with scFv-coupled quantum defects (scFv-QWNTs) were acquired using a fiber-coupled laser system and a 1D InGaAs NIR detector. The samples were excited with an 808nm laser power supply (1W; Optoelectronics Tech. Co., Ltd). The light path was shaped and fed into the back of an inverted IX-71 microscope (Olympus), where it passed through a 20× NIR objective (Olympus) and illuminated the samples in a 96-well clear flat bottom UV-transparent microplate (Corning). Emission from the SWCNTs was collected through the objective and passed through a dichroic mirror (875 nm cutoff, Semrock). The light was f/# matched to the spectrometer using several lenses and injected into a Shamrock 303i spectrograph (Andor, Oxford Instruments) with a slit width of 100 μm, which dispersed the emission using an 86 g/mm grating with 1.35 μm blaze wavelength. The spectral range was 723–1694 nm with a resolution of 1.89 nm. The light was collected by an iDus 1.7 μm InGaAs (Andor, Oxford Instruments) with an exposure time of 0.1–0.25 seconds. An HL-3-CAL-EXT halogen calibration light source (Ocean Optics) was used to correct for wavelength-dependent features in the emission intensity arising from the spectrometer, detector, and other optics. A Hg/Ne pencil-style calibration lamp (Newport) was used to calibrate the spectrometer wavelength. Background subtraction was conducted using a well in a 96-well plate filled with 20% FBS. Following the acquisition, the data were processed with custom codes written in MATLAB that applied the aforementioned spectral corrections and background subtraction and fitted the fluorescence emission peaks with Lorentzian functions.


### Atomic force microscopy (AFM).
  A stock solution of scFv conjugated (6,5)-QWNTs at 1 mg/mL in 1X PBS was diluted 20× in dH2O and plated on a freshly cleaved mica substrate (SPI) for 4 min before washing with 10 mL of dH2O and blowing dry with argon gas. An Olympus AC240TS AFM probe (Asylum Research) in an Asylum Research MFP-3D-Bio instrument was used to image in AC mode. Data was captured at 2.93 nm/pixel XY resolution and 15.63 pm Z resolution.


### Quantum chemical modeling.
  We modeled the charge effects using a 4 nm (1 unit cell) long (6,5)-SWCNT that contains an N-methylbenzamide and hydroxyl quantum defect pair in the ortho L90 configuration (using the notation of Gifford et al.26) located at the center of the nanotube. The pairing group is required to maintain a closed shell electronic structure and the choice of a hydroxyl group is based on its likelihood of formation in aqueous environments.27 To reduce end effects and prevent edge states from altering the band gap of the nanotube, we terminated the nanotube ends with hydrogen atoms.28 We first optimized the structure using the B3LYP functional and the STO3G basis set, as implemented in the Gaussian 16 software package.29 For time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations, we positioned two point charges 1.41 nm away from the defect center. Specifically, the charges were placed 1 nm away along the QWNT axis and 1 nm perpendicular to the QWNT surface. Note that the geometry changes due to the presence of charges are negligible (<0.05 Å). We varied these charges from 0 to 1 e−, enabling us to study the effects of different charge states on the nanotube.


## Results

### Design of signal-amplifying scFvs.
  We based the supercharged IDP design on the original sequence of an antibody against human IL-6 (hIL-6) with the goal of producing a large conformational and electric field change upon antigen binding. First, a WT scFv was produced using an X-ray structure of an hIL-6 neutralizing antibody fragment30 (PDB ID: 4ZS7). The sequences of the two variable regions (VH and VL) were linked together by a flexible glycine-serine linker with the orientation VH–linker-VL to generate the scFv sequence we term α-IL6wt (Figure 2A). To enable a complete extended-to-folded phase transition, each of the two cysteine pairs was mutated to valine and alanine.31, 32 Structure modeling using Alphafold233, 34 and RoseTTAFold235 predicts that the α-IL6wt sequence has an identical fold and binding interface compared to the modeled complex of α-IL6wt and hIL-6 (Figure S1). This protein, termed α-IL6wt, was expressed and purified and its hIL-6 binding affinity quantified using microscale thermophoresis with fluorescently-labeled hIL-6 (Figure 2B). α-IL6wt recognizes its target with a dissociation constant (KD) of 400 ± 100 nM. The reverse construct, VL–linker-VH, did not bind hIL-6.

  We then supercharged of the α-hIL-6 scFv by changing 28 solvent-exposed side chains located on beta strands of α-IL6wt into aspartate residues, as well as two arginine-to-leucine mutations which would have placed two negative charges in close contact, resulting in a protein in which 13.7% of the total protein sequence is acidic (Figure 2A). Attempts to incorporate glutamate residues resulted in insoluble proteins. Aspartate positions were chosen to maximize the distance between surface-exposed acidic side chains. The resultant protein, α-IL6sc, increases the net charge from −2 to −24. Circular dichroism (Figure S2) and fluorescence measurements (Figure 2C) demonstrate that α-IL6sc undergoes a disordered-to-ordered transition with increasing sodium chloride activities: The free energy of folding at no NaCl is +4.5 kcal/mol and NaCl linearly stabilizes folding at a rate of −3.4 kcal/mol·M. We conducted microscale thermophoresis measurements that demonstrate α-IL6sc binds hIL-6 with a sodium chloride-dependent affinity – increasingly higher sodium chloride activities screen sidechain charge-charge repulsion, reducing the electrostatic folding energy penalty for binding and increasing net affinity (Figure 2D and 2E). At salt concentrations greater than 2M, when the scFv is fully folded prior to binding, the KD value is 800 ± 200 nM, similar to that of α-IL6wt. At lower salt concentrations (0.5 M), where the energy barrier to α-IL6sc folding is larger, the KD value increases up to 8 ± 1 μM. Plotting ΔG of both folding and binding shows that both values scale linearly with NaCl activity above the folding transition, with a ΔG difference between folding and binding (ΔΔGdock, the interaction energy between the folded protein and the ligand) of −10.48 kcal/mol (Figure 2F). Apparent binding affinity stops increasing at the sodium chloride activities than those at the protein folds, suggesting that α-IL6sc has at least one partially folded intermediate. The ionic strength-dependent folding and ligand affinity did not significantly change within the approximate pH range of serum (pH values between 7 and 8, Figure 2G), likely due to the fact that serum pH values are significantly higher than aspartate side chain pKa’s. Finally, we confirmed that the binding affinity and the salt-induced folding behavior of the scFv are not significantly changed up to sodium deoxycholate concentration of 0.1% while the dynamic range started to decrease at higher concentrations (Figures S2-S3, Table S1).


### Synthesis of scFv-coupled QWNTs.
  We developed QWNTs to couple the scFv covalently to the SWCNT sidewall via a sp3 quantum-well defect site. The (6,5) chirality SWCNT was covalently functionalized with 4-carboxyl aryl defects via diazonium chemistry (see Methods).3 Functionalization was confirmed by the decreased host nanotube emission (E11) at ~993 nm and the evolution of bright E11− emission at 1,149 nm (Figure 3A). We adjusted the intensity ratio between E11 and E11− of QWNTs to 1:2 to 1:5 via functionalization to ensure that both emission bands were visible to enable observation of subtle changes in emission wavelength. The scFv was then conjugated to the QWNT via carbodiimide crosslinker chemistry. Note that the conjugation is not site-specific, and multiple primary amine groups of scFvs (15 and 11 in α-IL6wt and α-IL6sc, respectively) can be attached to a carboxyl aryl defect. Free proteins were then removed by dialysis in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% sodium deoxycholate. The absorption spectrum and fluorescence emission of the QWNT remain the same after scFv conjugation (Figure 3A, Figure S4), suggesting no aggregation occurs throughout the conjugation and dialysis processes. The surface charge (zeta potential) of α-IL6sc-coupled QWNTs (α-IL6sc-QWNTs) is more negative than the QWNTs and α-IL6wt-QWNTs, presumably due to the highly negative α-IL6sc on the QWNT surface (Figure 3B). Correlograms of dynamic light scattering measurements shift to a longer lag time after the bioconjugation, indicating an increase in the average hydrodynamic size of the scFv-QWNT complexes (Figure 3C, Figure S5). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging (Figure 3D, Figure S6) also suggests the covalent conjugation of scFvs to QWNTs. In contrast to the smooth surface along the QWNT, the scFv-coupled QWNTs show surface patterns due to the surface-bound scFvs. The density of scFvs on QWNTs ranged from 1-4 per 100 nm.


### Fluorescence response of scFv-coupled QWNTs.
  We next investigated the fluorescence response of the scFv-coupled QWNTs in response to the ligand-induced scFv folding transition. scFv-QWNT complexes were titrated with hIL-6 in 20% serum. 2.5 μM hIL-6 resulted in a 2 nm shift in the wavelength of the E11− band of a-IL6sc-coupled QWNTs (Figure 4A). The E11− band exhibited quantitative redshifting at increasing concentrations of hIL-6, while the E11 band remained at the same wavelength (Figure 4B). The dynamic range of the spectral response was 0.08–10μM of hIL-6, consistent with the binding isotherm curve of free α-IL6sc observed by microscale thermophoresis (Figure 2D). The E11− fluorescence redshifted up to 4 nanometers at increasing concentrations of hIL-6, with a detection limit of 80 nM and sensitivity up to 189 nM. Spectral responses saturated within 4 hours of incubation (Figure 4C). The slow response of the E11− can be attributed to the screening by other proteins in 20% serum (6.4 – 14 mg/mL total proteins in the solution) and the interactions between deoxycholate (bile surfactant stabilizing QWNTs in aqueous media, 0.1 % in solution), scFvs, and ligand that may modulate the binding kinetics of scFvs. Non-target proteins, including bovine serum albumin (BSA), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and tumor growth factor β1 (TGFβ1) did not induce wavelength shifts in the scFv- coupled QWNTs (Figure 4D). We next explored whether a high salt concentration (2M of sodium chloride) affects the spectral response. No hIL-6-dependent spectral response was observed (Figure S7), presumably due to smaller changes in ligand binding-induced local electric field near the quantum defects because the protein was already folded.

  We also assessed the long-term functionality of the scFv-coupled QWNTs (Figure 4E). Sensor complexes stored at 4°C for a week remained physically stable, detecting hIL-6 in serum with the same sensitivity as when freshly prepared. This high physical stability and long-term functionality is potentially due to the fact that the stronger electrostatic charge of supercharged proteins and the relatively low density on the QWNT sidewall reduce the chance of protein aggregation, improving the stability of α-IL6sc on QWNTs.

  To assess the effects of protein supercharging on sensor functionality, we performed identical experiments using α-IL6wt as with α-IL6sc-coupled QWNTs. In hIL-6 titration experiments, a much smaller shift in E11− peak is observed using α-IL6wt (1 nm at the highest concentration, 10 μM), as compared to the supercharged α-IL6sc (Figure 4F). We find no significant difference in the E11− peak wavelengths between a-IL6wt and a-IL6sc coupled QWNTs in the presence of hIL-6 (Figure S8). These comparative studies suggest that protein supercharging engenders significant changes in the local electric field upon ligand-induced folding, inducing significantly-enhanced spectral shifts on the quantum defects to which they are attached.


### Quantum Chemical Modeling.
  We then utilized TD-DFT modeling to elucidate how the protein charges induce spectral shifts of the E11− band (Figure 5). Our computational model included a (6,5)-SWCNT, 4 nm in length with hydrogen end capping, an N-methylbenzamide defect, and a hydroxyl pairing group located near the midsection of the tube. Due to the substantial size (~5 nanometers) and complex geometry of the folded α-IL6sc, it was impractical to explicitly include it in the DFT calculation (Figure 5A). Instead, a methyl amide bond was simulated to represent the peptide bond between the aryl defect and α-IL6sc. Given the highly charged surface and size of the scFv relative to the QWNT, it is reasonable to assume that several charges from the scFv could be brought close to the defect in various directions. Although the exact distances between any charged groups on the scFv and the N-methylbenzamide defect are not experimentally determined, the primary effect anticipated from varying these distances is a change in the effective charge near the defect. To emulate the collective charge effects of the scFv to the QWNT emission, we positioned two point-charges at a distance of 1.41 nm from the defect center and varied these charges from 0 to 1 e− (Figure 5B).

  These TD-DFT simulations show that, with increasing local negative charge, both the E11 and E11− peaks steadily redshift (Figure 5C). In the unfunctionalized SWCNT model, there are minimal shifts in the E11 peak (Figure 5D). For the QWNT, the simulation indicated that the E11 exhibits a relatively small red shift, similar to the unfunctionalized SWCNT, but E11− shows a substantially larger shift, consistent with the experimental observation. When both charges had a magnitude of −1e each, the E11− peak shifted by ~5.5 nm, while the E11 peak only shifted by ~1.3 nm. These TD-DFT results support our hypothesis that the strong negative charges of α-IL6sc draw closer to the defect center when α-IL6sc couples to hIL-6 and folds, and that this increased local electric field leads to larger spectral shifts on the E11− peak as compared to the SWCNT E11 peak. This result is consistent with previous report that quantum defects are accompanied by a permanent dipole that significantly perturbs the local electrostatic environment of the trapped exciton, and they can act as an antenna to the environment for promoting charge-induced optical responses.6


## Discussion
  We investigated how protein engineering can amplify ligand-binding signal transduction via quantum defect emission and the environmental responsiveness of quantum defects to local electric fields. The scFv of the scFv-QWNT complex undergoes ligand-induced folding in proximity to quantum defects, resulting in quantitative bathochromic shifts in the defect fluorescence. Quantum chemical modeling found that the high net charge and large conformational change of supercharged IDPs confer changes in local electric fields adjacent to the defect centers, substantially modulating the electronic energy level of the defect state.

  While we present the first redesign of an antibody-derived scFv to form a supercharged ligand-induced folder, this approach can be extended to any natural or designed ligand binding protein that recognizes either other proteins, as described here, or small molecules for quantitative and mechanistic understanding of biochemical processes.24, 36 This approach opens a new dimension in biodevice development, combining the specificity inherent to biological recognition, the extraordinary electric field changes inherent to ligand-induced folding in supercharged proteins, and the NIR fluorescence of quantum defects to create robust, versatile, and sensitive biosensors.

  Our findings led to the development of a new class of optical biodevice comprised of QWNTs conjugated to a phase-changing supercharged scFv to detect biomarkers in serum and, potentially, other biological media. Due to improved physical stability and long-term functionality, we envision that sensor design can be implemented in measurements in underresourced settings. However, outstanding questions remain to be addressed. Additional chemical modifications are required to confer biocompatibility for use within biological systems. For instance, wrapping with biocompatible polymers8 may be necessary. In addition, protein design for reversible binding37 would enable dynamic monitoring of analytes. To provide clinically meaningful readouts,38, 39 the limit of detection and dynamic ranges can be further improved by protein engineering. The KD value of the supercharged scFv designed for this work is higher than that of the previously observed for the Fab fragment (13-21 pM).40 Both the change from Fab to scFv41 as well as the removal of disulfide bonds32 have been shown to decrease stability, and this combined destabilization could impart an energetic cost to the folding-coupled binding. The scFv sequence may be further optimized to increase binding affinities to target proteins by better packing the core and improving stability as well as improving the CDR sequences. The binding signal may be further enhanced by site-specific conjugation to quantum defects to facilitate more specific orientation to optimize the localization of charges with respect to the QWNT. Finally, non-covalent surface passivation to screen interference from non-specific binding may further improve the sensitivity of such sensor platforms.42
