# Time-resolved NMR detection of prolyl-hydroxylation in intrinsically disordered region of HIF-1α


## Significance
  Prolyl-hydroxylation of proteins is a distinct intracellular response to oxygen availability. Hypoxic-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) possesses two hydroxylatable prolines that regulate its oxygen-dependent protein degradation, but the precise impact of each proline has remained unclear. Here, we introduce 13C-direct detection NMR-based assay to directly interrogate their respective roles. Although nonphysiological protein concentrations and oxygen tension were used due to the nature of NMR, our assay reveals the comparative efficiencies of the two prolines in a relative timeframe. With the ever-expanding list of prolyl-hydroxylated proteins and disease-associated mutations in key components of the general oxygen-sensing pathway, our assay will be invaluable to gain mechanistic underpinnings in biological consequences of prolyl-hydroxylation in normal and pathologic conditions such as Pacak–Zhuang syndrome and von Hippel–Lindau disease.


## Results

### HIF-1α Oxygen-Dependent Degradation (ODD) Domain Is Intrinsically Disordered.
  We purified the full-length ODD domain of HIF-1α, a region demonstrated with multiple PTMs in addition to the stability-regulating hydroxylation of P402 and P564 (34–36). The structure of ODD has not been determined likely due to a high percentage of prolyl residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Indeed, circular dichroism (CD) study suggests that ODD adopts a highly flexible conformation (Fig. 2A). This is consistent with the overall structure predicted by AlphaFold 2 (37), showing that only two short α-helices are present whereas the remainder of the ODD is likely unstructured (Fig. 2B). The entire ODD displayed low pLDDT (predicted local distance difference test) scores by AlphaFold 2 (Fig. 2C), suggesting low confidence of predicting the 3D coordinates of such region and therefore is a predictor of disorder. Previous 1H-15N HSQC spectrum of a shorter ODD segment lacking both target prolines (residues 404 to 477) also indicated that such region is largely unfolded (38). However, the role of the two prolines, separated by more than 150 residues, in the unstructured region of HIF-1α is yet to be fully explained as these prolines have been previously characterized by the aforementioned methods that examined single-proline peptide substrates (8, 12). Here, advancing from the currently available 13C-direct detection NMR methodology, we directly monitored hydroxylation-dependent spectral changes of the two target prolines (P402 and P564) in the context of full-length ODD.

  We acquired the 1H-15N HSQC spectrum of full-length ODD, and confirmed the intrinsically disordered conformation for ODD as a majority of resonances are highly clustered in the spectrum (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The SSP scores of all assigned residues, which estimated the secondary structure propensity of each residue based on the difference between the experimentally determined chemical shifts and the expected random coil values (39), were mostly less than ±0.2, indicating the overall intrinsic disorder of ODD (Fig. 2E). We conducted 3D NMR experiments for backbone cross-peak assignments and assigned 177 of 181 residues of ODD (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Tables S1 and S2) with chemical shift values of the short ODD segment similar to the previous assignment (38) except for 1HN values due likely to the temperature and pH differences.


### 13C-Detected 2D CON Permits Direct Measurement of Prolyl-Hydroxylation.
  To directly investigate P402 and P564, we conducted 13C-detected 2D CON experiments on 15N- and 13C-labeled ODD, which gave rise to better dispersion of various resonances, especially for those with 15N chemical shift values between 120 to 125 ppm (Fig. 3), compared to the 1H-15N HSQC spectrum (Fig. 2D), More importantly, cross-peaks of proline residues were observed and highly dispersed in a well-isolated spectral region downfield in the 15N dimension, allowing us to assign the 15N chemical shift values of proline residues (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2).

  When ODD was double-hydroxylated using recombinant PHD2 in an in vitro hydroxylation assay (40), distinct cross-peak shifts were observed for both target prolines. Specifically, prolyl-hydroxylation led to 5.5-ppm and 5.6-ppm upfield displacements in the 15N dimension, and 0.5-ppm and 0.4-ppm downfield in the 13CO dimension (for the i−1 residues, A401 and A563) for P402, and P564, respectively (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Chemical shift changes of the two prolines in the 2D CON spectrum were large enough and did not overlap with other cross-peaks, which demonstrated the advantage of 13C-direct detected experiments for studying prolyl-hydroxylation. No other resonances of proline residues exhibited such dramatic chemical shifting, confirming P402 and P564 are the only two proline residues undergoing hydroxylation. P567 also showed minor perturbation, which likely resulted from its proximity to hydroxylated P564. Spectral changes were also observed in the 1H-15N HSQC spectrum for residues surrounding P402 and P564 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In the 3D spectra, prolyl-hydroxylation led to 7.5-ppm downfield shifting of 13Cβ values of P402 and P564 due to the added electron-withdrawing hydroxyl group on the neighboring Cγ (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Tables S1 and S2), which is an unambiguous indicator of formation of 4-hydroxyprolines (41). 15N chemical shift values of the residues following the two prolines, A403 and Y565, respectively, exhibited shifts larger than 1 ppm, whereas all other residues were indiscernibly affected. Compared to 13Cβ, the 13Cα and 13CO values of P402 and P564 did not show such extensive shifting when hydroxylated. Moreover, the SSP scores of double-hydroxylated ODD were highly similar to the ones of nonhydroxylated ODD (Fig. 2E). These data suggest that the overall protein conformation was not affected by prolyl-hydroxylation.


### Hydroxylation Efficiency of P564 Predominates over P402.
  Next, we set up a real-time hydroxylation reaction by mixing ODD with the purified catalytic domain of PHD2 and the necessary cofactors including 2OG, ascorbic acid, FeCl2, and O2 and acquired a series of 2D CON spectra at 25 °C to monitor the reaction. These 2D CON spectra clearly showed time-dependent change of signals in the P402 and P564 resonances (Fig. 4A). We examined the catalytic progress of hydroxylation in real-time by measuring the signal intensity for P402 and P564 and found that hydroxylation of P564 reached 50% and 95% occupancy in 0.4 and 1.6 h, respectively. In contrast, P402 hydroxylation was significantly less efficient, reaching 50% in 9.5 h and less than 95% in 19.9 h (Fig. 4 B and C). These data demonstrate the predominant turnover of P564 hydroxylation over P402 in the context of full-length ODD.

  Since both prolines can be hydroxylated by PHD2, we sought to understand whether hydroxylation of the two prolines is mutually influenced by studying the two single mutants, ODD P402A (i.e., retains a single hydroxylation site at P564 in CODD) and ODD P564A (i.e., retains a single hydroxylation site at P402 in NODD) (Fig. 4D). The deletion of P564 (i.e., P564A substitution) increased P402 hydroxylation rate, but did not approach that of P564 hydroxylation (Fig. 4 B and C). In contrast, the deletion of P402 (i.e., P402A substitution) marginally affected P564 hydroxylation rate as compared to the efficient P564 hydroxylation rate in the context of wild-type (WT) ODD. These results suggest that P564 is the predominant, more efficient target for PHD2 irrespective of the presence of P402. The presence of P564 had a marked effect on P402 hydroxylation, which was minimal prior to the completion of P564 hydroxylation. Moreover, P402 hydroxylation rate was dramatically slower even in the absence of P564, which could not be sufficiently explained by the similar binding affinity of PHD2 to ODD P402A and ODD P564A (Fig. 4 E and F and SI Appendix, Table S3) measured via biolayer interferometry (BLI). Thus, the preferential efficiency of prolyl-hydroxylation at P564 over P402 remained unclear.


### i+1 Tyrosine Contributes to Efficient Prolyl-Hydroxylation.
  Considering that the intrinsic disorder of ODD persists upon prolyl-hydroxylation and that the binding affinity of PHD2 to full-length ODD P564A is similar to that of ODD P402A, we asked whether the efficiency of prolyl-hydroxylation is influenced by the surrounding residues of P402 and P564 within their respective ODD motifs. Sequence conservation analysis of the two ODD motifs revealed that NODD and CODD in many species possess identical residues at certain locations, such as leucine in the −5 and −2 positions relative to the two target prolines and on the distal C-terminal side of the motifs, as well as an alanine in the −1 positions (Fig. 5A). Residues in other positions are more variable between NODD and CODD. In identifying the key residues that influence the rate of prolyl-hydroxylation, the strictly conserved ones are of particular interest as a previous study showed that the PHD homologs expressed in Trichoplax adhaerens and humans were able to hydroxylate ODD substrates in both species (42), suggesting that certain amino acids have been present and retained since the earliest development of the HIF oxygen-sensing pathway. In early animals, only one ODD motif is present and resembles the human CODD motif more than human NODD. Residues in the −1, +1, and +2 positions relative to proline within CODD are with small side chain (Ala), aromatic side chains (Tyr or Phe), and aliphatic side chains (Ile or Val), respectively (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the +1 and +2 positions in NODD are mostly occupied by alanine. NODD shares the same residue (Ala) with CODD at the −1 position so likely such residue at this position does not differentiate P402 and P564. The presence of an aromatic residue at the +1 position is intriguing. It has been observed that the presence of an aromatic next to a proline (Xaa-cis-Pro or cis-Pro-Xaa, where Xaa can be any amino acid) increases the formation of cis-proline (43). Proline is known for forming cis peptide bond due to the cyclic nature of the side chain, while other 19 amino acids disfavor such bonding due to the steric repulsion between their own Cα atom and the Cα atom of the following residue (44) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). As P564 is adjacent to an evolutionarily conserved tyrosine at the +1 position but not P402, we asked whether this tyrosine influenced the rate of prolyl-hydroxylation, potentially by causing an increased cis population of P564.

  We generated and measured prolyl-hydroxylation of two double mutants, ODD P402A/Y565A (retaining WT P564) and ODD A403Y/P564A (retaining WT P402), both having a mutation at the +1 position relative to the proline to be studied (Fig. 4D). Real-time kinetic measurements showed that the Y565A mutation negligibly affected the rate of P564 hydroxylation, whereas the introduction of a tyrosine to the NODD motif substantially enhanced P402 hydroxylation (Figs. 4C and 5 B and C) by more than sevenfold. These results suggest that the presence of tyrosine at the +1 position contributes to a more efficient prolyl-hydroxylation process. To understand whether the i+1 tyrosine promoted the rate of P402 hydroxylation by increasing the cis proline population, we measured the kinetics of WT ODD with cyclophilin A (CypA), a prolyl isomerase that catalyzes rapid prolyl-isomerization in the unstructured regions of various proteins (45–47). The addition of CypA caused the broadening of several resonances in the vicinity of multiple prolines of ODD, such as A403 near P402 and I566 near P564 and P567, to varying degrees (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). This indicated that the cis/trans interconversion of P402 and P564 was accelerated due to CypA, bringing the rate of prolyl-isomerization from slow to intermediate exchange on the NMR scale. Under this circumstance, the rate of prolyl-hydroxylation, at least for P402 in NODD without an i+1 tyrosine, would likely be enhanced as the cis-proline conformer was more rapidly produced in the presence of CypA and depleted from the isomerization equilibrium by PHD2 catalysis, leading to even faster production of the cis population. However, when we conducted the real-time kinetic measurement on WT ODD mixed with CypA in two different molar ratios, we found that the rate of prolyl-hydroxylation was negligibly affected (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). These data suggest that the enhancing influence of i+1 tyrosine on the prolyl-hydroxylation is not due to the altered cis-proline population.

  We next asked whether the reaction efficiency contributed by +1 tyrosine was due to its influence on the binding affinity to PHD2. BLI binding experiments showed that both mutations of residue at +1 position (ODD P402A/Y565A and ODD A403Y/P564A) mildly weakened binding compared to the respective counterpart (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Table S3). For example, PHD2 Kd binding affinities to ODD P564A (retaining WT P402 and A403) and ODD A403Y/P564A (retaining WT P402 with the mutational inclusion of +1 Y) were 1.00 ± 0.16 μM and 2.16 ± 0.71 μM, respectively, while PHD2 Kd binding affinities to ODD P402A (retaining WT P564 and +1 Y565) and ODD P402A/Y565A (retaining WT P564 with the loss of +1 Y) were 0.80 ± 0.07 μM and 1.18 ± 0.32 μM, respectively. These subtle changes to the binding affinity could not account for the substantial changes A403Y had on the rate of P402 prolyl-hydroxylation (Figs. 4C and 5C).


### Amino Acid Composition of CODD Contributes to Prolyl-Hydroxylation Efficiency.
  Having the peak assignment in the 1H-15N HSQC spectrum with the corresponding residues of ODD, we next sought to understand whether other residues in ODD, especially those outside of the NODD/CODD motifs, influenced prolyl-hydroxylation. We titrated PHD2 into 15N-labeled ODD supplemented with Fe2+ but not 2OG, which formed an enzymatically inactive ODD–PHD2 complex, and monitored the interaction. As resonance broadening of ODD was observed upon the addition of PHD2, intensity changes of all assigned resonances of ODD in response to different molar ratios of PHD2 were compared to the untitrated sample to identify the binding preference of PHD2 to ODD. Titration of PHD2 showed that the CODD region was preferentially recognized by PHD2 over the NODD region (Fig. 6A). A segment N-terminal to CODD, residues 517 to 548, had a comparable or greater level of response to the titration of PHD2 than CODD, whereas regions near to NODD had no discernable interaction with PHD2. We hypothesized that this region functions as an extra binding region (EBR) to PHD2, which prioritizes the initial binding and promotes a more efficient hydroxylation of P564.

  To address this notion, we generated a construct of motif-swapped ODD, by swapping the segments of NODD (residues D395-L411) and CODD (residues D556-L574) in the sequence of WT ODD (SI Appendix, Table S4). We measured hydroxylation of the two prolines to see whether ODD-EBR facilitates hydroxylation of “P402.” Comparison of both the 1H-15N HSQC and the 2D CON spectra between WT ODD and the motif-swapped ODD showed that the resonances of residues near the two prolines were minimally affected despite the rearrangement of the two ODD motifs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We observed that whereas hydroxylation of “P564” of the motif-swapped ODD was slightly slower, hydroxylation of P402 now adjacent to ODD-EBR was not enhanced (Figs. 4C and 6B). These results suggest that the ODD-EBR contributes negligibly to the efficiency of P564 hydroxylation and that the composition of the CODD motif, in particular, the +1 tyrosine, plays a pivotal role in maintaining the fast rate of P564 hydroxylation irrespective of where this region is located within ODD.


## Discussion
  Unlike other PTMs such as serine and tyrosine phosphorylation or lysine acetylation, prolyl-hydroxylation associated with protein stability is a rare event currently only found in a limited number of biologically important proteins. The collagen prolyl-4-hydroxylase as the first identified Fe(II)/2OG-dependent protein hydroxylase was found to catalyze prolyl-4-hydroxylation of collagen, elastin, and other proteins with collagenous sequences (48). The HIF prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) hydroxylate proline residues of HIF-α subunits that signals for pVHL-mediated polyubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation (27). In addition, prolyl-3-hydroxylation and prolyl-4-hydroxylation can occur on the ribosomal protein RPS23 and regulate efficiency of translation termination in an mRNA sequence-dependent manner (11).

  The mechanistic determinants by which two prolines in the context of full-length ODD undergo oxygen-dependent hydroxylation to regulate HIF-1α protein stability are not yet fully understood. P402 and P564 in the form of short peptides are both capable of binding PHD2. While NODD binds PHD2 more strongly than CODD, both peptides with hydroxyproline no longer recognize PHD2, demonstrating the consistent PTM-specific mechanism shared by the two residues of HIF-1α (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). However, previous publications, including ours, have suggested that P564 is the major degradation-regulating site that readily becomes hydroxylated in the presence of oxygen than P402 whose hydroxylation is more susceptible to oxygen depletion (8, 40, 49). Furthermore, studying prolyl hydroxylation of HIF-1α in the form of short single-proline-containing peptides likely overlooked factors including the potential mutual interplay between the two prolines, and any impact from other regions of ODD.

  By performing 13C-direct detected NMR experiments on recombinantly purified ODD, we investigated the kinetic profile of both prolines simultaneously and showed that P402 hydroxylation significantly lags P564 in real-time. Since HIF-1α is rapidly captured by pVHL upon hydroxylation and targeted for proteasomal degradation, with a half-life of approximately 5 min under normoxic conditions (50), our results suggest that under normal physiological conditions, P402 likely plays little to no role in determining the fate of HIF-1α. However, P402 hydroxylation has been previously observed in cells (8, 49), but many of the experiments used proteasome inhibitors such as MG132 to prevent HIF-1α degradation in an effort to study the otherwise hard-to-detect HIF-1α under oxygenated conditions. These experimental conditions provided sufficient time for hydroxylation of P402 to occur since HIF-1α proteins were prevented from destruction by the proteosome inhibitors. Furthermore, we previously showed that ODD with two hydroxyprolines is not targeted for degradation more efficiently than the one with only hydroxylated P402 or P564 (40). Taken together with the current findings, we suggest that the major functional differences between the two prolines are reflected in the stage of PHD-mediated hydroxylation rather than the subsequent pVHL capture and polyubiquitylation steps.

  In our study, we found that residues near the two prolines influence the efficiency of prolyl-hydroxylation. Sequence alignment of the NODD and CODD motifs expressed in multiple metazoan species shows that the +1 position from P564 is occupied by a tyrosine in vertebrates or otherwise a phenylalanine in some other species (Fig. 5A), whereas the residue at the +1 position from P402 is less conserved and most commonly an alanine. Although an aromatic residue is in the ±1 position relative to proline can increase the population of cis-proline isomers via CH/π interaction between the π aromatic face and the polarized C-H bonds of proline (43, 51), we showed that cis/trans proline isomerization has negligible impact on rate of prolyl-hydroxylation. Nonetheless, our results reveal the significance of the i+1 tyrosine in efficient prolyl-hydroxylation of HIF-1α, which was shown to be an intrinsically slow reaction compared to other hydroxylation events such as the asparaginyl-hydroxylation of the transactivation domain of HIF-1α (Km = 90 μM) (52) and prolyl-hydroxylation of collagen (Km = 40 μM) (12). The low efficiency of PHD-mediated prolyl-hydroxylation is due to the presence of the aspartic acid residue (e.g., D315 for PHD2) of the Fe2+-chelating 2-His-1-Asp triad composing the active site, which hydrogen-bonds and enables a water molecule to occupy the binding channel for oxygen (6, 53). As a result, large amounts of oxygen [Km = 250 μM (12); higher than the ambient oxygen level] for PHD2 are necessary to outcompete the metal-bound water molecule for the initiation of the hydroxylation reaction. Therefore, the promoting effect of i+1 tyrosine on the rate of prolyl-hydroxylation is likely crucial in matching the regulation of the hydroxylation capacity of HIF-1α with the physiological oxygen range for the oxygen-dependent degradation pathway.

  The capability of detecting multiple protein residues simultaneously by NMR allowed the identification of a binding region ODD-EBR that interacts with PHD2 without affecting prolyl-hydroxylation. In comparison, the local composition of the ODD motif, including the i+1 tyrosine and other surrounding residues, has a larger effect. In fact, previous studies have mentioned that a few residues in CODD, such as the positions of −1, +1, and +7 relative to P564 are important in binding to PHD2 whereas others are more promiscuous with multiple amino acid substitutions (54). However, the contribution of specific residues in the NODD/CODD motif for the step of enzymatic turnover following the initial binding is not well understood, and in our study, we identified the striking importance of tyrosine at the +1 position. While we attempted to understand the possible role of ODD-EBR in regulation of HIF-1α, we found that ODD-EBR is highly conserved in the vertebrate species but not in the nonvertebrates (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Therefore, we propose that ODD-EBR is a gained factor by the vertebrata that contributes to stronger interaction between HIF-1α and PHD2, similar to the i+1 tyrosine, to likely fine-tune the regulation capacity of HIF-1α to be more compatible with the physiological oxygen levels of the more complicated metazoan species.

  Here, we used HIF-1α to demonstrate the application of 13C-direct detection NMR-based assay on measuring prolyl-hydroxylation in IDPs/Rs. Due to the absence of a tertiary structure, contributions of the overall folding of IDPs/Rs to chemical shifts are largely averaged out, causing a drastic degeneracy of signals in the 1H-15N HSQC spectra. As most regions of IDPs are exposed to the solvent, the rapid exchange between amide protons and water molecules can also cause broadening of proton resonances. Admittedly, we were able to assign more than 97% of the residues of ODD from the 1H-detected experiments, but lack of amide protons rendered information about proline residues unavailable in the 1H-15N HSQC spectrum. 13C-direct detection NMR, which has been previously used to study proline residues in IDPs/IDRs with important roles in a variety of cellular events and signaling pathways (55, 56), is a better option for monitoring prolyl-hydroxylation. Notably, this assay reveals large chemical shift dispersion and allows monitoring of multiple proline residues simultaneously. As the list of prolyl-hydroxylated proteins (6) and PHD-targeted substrates (57) grows beyond HIF-α and collagen, as well as the number of disease-associated PHD (58) and HIF mutations (59), our assay will be useful in providing insights into the mechanism in which prolyl-hydroxylation plays a role.


## Materials and Methods

### Plasmids.
  DNA corresponding to the sequence of human HIF-1α ODD (residue 394 to 574) with a C-terminal Strep Tag II (WSHPQFEK) was cloned into a pCDF Ek/LIC vector (EMD Millipore). For motif-swapped ODD, the NODD motif was replaced with residues from CODD (residues 556 to 574: DLDLEMLAPYIPMDDDFQL) and the CODD motif was swapped with residues from NODD (residues 395 to 411: DALTLLAPAAGDTIISL). P402A, P564A, P402A/Y565A, and P564A/A403Y ODD mutants were generated by QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis. For all ODD plasmids, thrombin cleavage sites were included after the N-terminal 6×-His and before the Strep Tag II. DNA sequence for PHD2 (residues 181 to 426) and full-length CypA were codon-optimized for Escherichia coli expression, gene synthesized (Invitrogen/GeneArt), and cloned into a pET46 Ek/LIC vector (EMD Millipore). All expression constructs used in this study and their associated protein sequences are listed in SI Appendix, Table S4.


### Peptides.
  HIF-1α peptides were synthesized by GenScript (NODD: PDALTLLAPAAGDTIISL (residue 394 to 411); CODD: DLDLEMLAPYIPMDDDFQL (residue 556 to 574); NODDHyP: PDALTLLA[HyP]AAGDTIISL; CODDHyP: DLDLEMLA[HyP]YIPMDDDFQL). All peptides were N-terminally biotinylated and C-terminally amidated. Peptides were reconstituted to a final concentration of 2 mg/mL in 100% (v/v) Dimethyl sulfoxide.


### Recombinant Protein Expression and Purification.

#### HIF-1α ODD.
  WT ODD, motif-swapped ODD, and all point mutants were expressed in Rosetta-2 (DE3) cells. To produce uniformly labeled proteins, cells were grown in minimal 2× M9 media, supplemented with a final concentration of 1 g/L 15NH4Cl and 3 g/L 13C-glucose (ACP chemicals). After the culture reached an OD600 of 0.8, the culture was induced with a final concentration of 0.3 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Bioshop), and cells were grown for 20 h at 18 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, followed by resuspension in Ni-NTA binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), and 10 mM imidazole) with 1× cOmplete Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Cells were lysed using a hydraulic cell disruption system (30 kpsi; Constant Systems) and the lysate clarified by centrifugation at 32,000 g for 40 min and applied onto Ni-NTA Sepharose resin (GE Healthcare). The column was first washed with 10 column volumes (CV) of Ni-NTA binding buffer supplemented with 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 (Bioshop) followed by another 15 CV of Ni-NTA binding buffer without Tween-20. Protein was eluted with a single gradient of Ni-NTA binding buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was then incubated overnight with StrepTactin XT 4Flow resin (IBA Lifesciences) preequilibrated with StrepTactin binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). The mixture was then transferred to an empty gravity flow column to separate the resin from the flow-through solution. The protein-bound resin was washed with StrepTactin binding buffer and eluted with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 50 mM biotin. The used resin was regenerated by washing with 10 CV of 10 mM NaOH and 15 CV of StrepTactin binding buffer. The flow through was reapplied onto regenerated StrepTactin XT 4Flow resin overnight to capture any remaining unbound ODD and repurified. Elution fractions from both days were pooled from both purifications, concentrated using an Amicon 10 kDa MWCO centrifugal concentrator, and purified by gel filtration on a Bio-Rad SEC650 10 × 300 column equilibrated with Buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). Fractions containing WT or mutants of ODD were applied onto a HiRes Q 5 × 50 anion exchange column (Cytiva) preequilibrated with Buffer A. The column was washed with 3 CV of Buffer A, and the protein was eluted with over 50 CV of a 0 to 100% linear gradient Buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). Fractions containing pure protein were confirmed by SDS-PAGE and pooled. The N-terminal 6×-His tag and the C-terminal Strep Tag II was removed by incubating restriction-grade thrombin WT or mutants of ODD (EMD Millipore) (2 units/mg of ODD) for 60 h, prior to isocratic elution on a Bio-Rad SEC650 10 × 300 column equilibrated with NMR buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl)]. Fractions containing tag-cleaved protein were confirmed by SDS-PAGE, pooled, concentrated to 1.15 mM, and stored at −80 °C for future use. For BLI experiments, WT and mutants of ODD were purified with the same protocol but using natural NH4Cl and glucose for overexpression.


#### Prolyl-hydroxylated ODD.
  Double-hydroxylated ODD for backbone assignments was prepared using an in vitro PHD2 reaction. In brief, purified WT ODD was mixed with a final concentration of 5 mM α-ketoglutaric acid disodium salt dihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM L-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1 mM ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (Sigma-Aldrich). PHD2 was added in a 1:100 ratio of PHD2 to purified ODD and incubated at 37 °C for 5 h.


#### PHD2.
  PHD2 construct was transformed in BL21 (DE3) cells and grown in Luria-Bertani broth. At an OD600 of 0.8, the culture was induced with a final concentration of 0.3 mM IPTG, and cells were grown for 20 h at 18 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, followed by resuspension in Ni-NTA binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10 mM imidazole) with 1× cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Cell lysis was performed using a hydraulic cell disruption system (30 kpsi) and the crude extract was clarified by centrifugation at 32,000 g for 40 min prior to application onto Ni-NTA Sepharose column equilibrated by PHD2 binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole at pH 8.0), the column was washed by PHD2 binding buffer supplemented 50 mM imidazole and eluted with PHD2 binding buffer with 300 mM imidazole. PHD2 was then purified by gel filtration on a Bio-Rad SEC650 10 × 300 column equilibrated with 50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl at pH 7.5. Finally, PHD2 was concentrated to 15 mg/mL, aliquoted into 150 μg and stored at −80 °C for real-time hydroxylation. PHD2 used for BLI measurements was dialyzed into 50 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM EDTA pH 8.0 to remove trace amount of Fe2+ prior to SEC purification. PHD2 for BLI binding was concentrated to at least 4.35 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C.


#### CypA.
  Expression and lysis methods for CypA were similar to PHD2. After loading clarified lysate onto the Ni-NTA resin equilibrated by CypA binding buffer [20 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 500 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole at pH 6.1], the column was washed by CypA binding buffer supplemented with 50 mM imidazole and eluted with CypA binding buffer with 300 mM imidazole. CypA was then purified by gel filtration on a Bio-Rad SEC650 10 × 300 column equilibrated with 20 mM MES, 500 mM NaCl, at pH 6.1. Purified CypA was dialyzed into 50 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl at pH 7.5, concentrated to 4.1 mg/mL, and stored at −80 °C for future use.


### CD.
  WT ODD was dialyzed against 5 mM KH2PO4/K2HPO4 pH 7.4, 100 mM NaF, and 0.5 mM TCEP-HCl. CD wavelength scan was acquired on a Jasco J-1500CD spectrophotometer using a 1-mm pathlength quartz cuvette (Helma). Data were collected between 185 to 260 nm at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL and averaged over 10 accumulations. Raw CD ellipticity values were baseline buffer subtracted and converted to mean residue ellipticity (degrees cm2 dmol−1).


### NMR Spectroscopy.
  NMR experiments were performed at the UHN NMR Core Facility on a Bruker 800 MHz US2 equipped with a NEO console and a 5 mm triple-resonance 13C optimized TXO cryoprobe, Z-gradient. NMR samples were prepared in NMR buffer supplemented with 5% D2O.


#### Backbone assignments.
  HIF-1α nonhydroxylated ODD and hydroxylated ODD backbone assignments were conducted at 15 °C with 0.2 mM protein samples using the classic NMR pulse sequences: Proton-detect 1H-15N HSQC, HNCO, HNCOCA, HNCACB, CBCACONH, and 13C-direct detected 2D CON. Sodium 2,2-dimethyl-2-silapentane-5-sulfonate was used as reference. CON experiments were acquired consecutively with eight scans, 1,024 × 128 data points with acquisition time of 2,657 s followed by interscan delays of 600 s. The 13C sweep width was 13.8 ppm with the offset at 173.5 ppm and the 15N sweep width was 40 ppm with the offset at 124 ppm. The IPAP approach was used for the CON. All spectra were processed with the NMRPipe software (60) and analyzed using the CcpNmr Version-3 software (61). SSP program is available as a standalone script with instructions and can be downloaded at http://pound.med.utoronto.ca/software.html. Although 1Hα chemical shifts are also recommended (39), we were not able to assign 1Hα chemical shifts due to the poor cross-peak dispersion.


#### Real-time kinetic measurements of prolyl-hydroxylation.
  NMR buffer was gassed with 99.6% oxygen (Messer) for 40 min and then used to dilute 1.15 mM of WT or mutant ODD to 230 μM final concentration. Samples were transferred to a 5-mm NMR tube prepurged with 99.6% oxygen for 10 min. The void in the NMR tube contained approximately 1.12 × 10−4 mol of O2 (based on 2.5-mL volume). This was in high excess compared to the amount of ODD in solution (approximately 1.15 × 10−7 mol), and served as a reservoir to replenish the consumed oxygen in the aqueous phase during the reaction. However, the rate of oxygen diffusion could not be considered for the kinetic analysis. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C for efficient progress of the reaction. 1H-15N HSQC and 2D CON spectra were acquired for the samples prior to initiating the hydroxylation reaction with the addition of 150 μg PHD2 and cofactors (final concentration of 5 mM α-ketoglutaric acid disodium salt dihydrate, 2 mM L-ascorbic acid, and 0.1 mM ferrous chloride tetrahydrate). For the kinetic measurements, a series of CON spectra were acquired. Progress of hydroxylation was calculated by measuring the peak intensity for hydroxyproline over the sum of peak intensities for both nonhydroxylated proline and hydroxyproline. Kinetic measurements for each sample were performed in technical duplicates.

  Real-time kinetic measurements of WT ODD with the addition of CypA were conducted with the same procedure as described above. However, CypA was added to WT ODD after collecting the first set of the 1H-15N HSQC and 2D CON spectra. A second set of two spectra was collected for examining the peak broadening profile before the reaction was initiated by the addition of other reaction components. Kinetic measurements were performed in technical duplicates.


#### Kinetic data analysis.
  Prolyl-hydroxylation kinetic data were fit using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0). Hyperbolic curves (P564 of ODD WT, ODD P402A, ODD P402A/Y565A, and motif-swapped ODD and P402 of ODD A403Y/P564A) were fit to an exponential plateau function Y = YM – (YM – Y0)*exp(–k*x), where YM represents the maximum population and Y0 represents the starting population. Sigmoidal curves (P402 of ODD WT and motif-swapped ODD) were fit to a Gompertz growth function YM*(Y0/YM)^(exp(–k*x)). The kinetic of P402 of ODD P564A with a rapid initial rise followed by slow progression was fit to a function Y = YM*(1 – exp(–k*x)) + v*x. Confidence bands with a level of 95% were calculated and displayed as dotted lines for all curves. The elapsed time and the errors for 50% and 95% of hydroxylation completion for the target proline of each construct were calculated using the parameters of the deduced curves and the associated confidence bands.


### HIF-1α-PHD2 Interaction Studies.
  The binding affinities of PHD2 and ODD mutants (ODD P402A, ODD P402A/Y565A, ODD P564A, ODD A403Y/P564A) were measured by BLI using a single channel BLItz instrument (Sartorius). WT and mutant ODD were biotinylated in a 1:1 molar ratio using the EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher) at room temperature for 1 h. The biotinylation reaction was quenched by dialyzing the mixture into 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, and 100 mM NaCl. PHD2 was concentrated to 143 μM and diluted at least 33-fold into BLI Kinetic Buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% (w/v) BSA and 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20]. Biotinylated ODD mutant was diluted 200-fold in the BLI Kinetic Buffer to a final concentration of 3.1 μM and immobilized on a streptavidin-coated biosensor (Sartorius) for 120 s. The association of PHD2 to the bait was measured in a concentration series over 120 s. Subsequently, the BLI probe was immersed in the BLI Kinetic Buffer for 120 s to measure dissociation of the analyte. The sensorgrams were reference and step corrected, and the binding affinities and kinetic rates were calculated based on global fit of the data to a 1:1 binding model using the BLItz Pro v.1.3.0.5 software. For peptide BLI binding measurements, biotinylated HIF-1α peptides were used as the bait and diluted to 1.8 μM in BLI Kinetic buffer supplemented with 1 mM N-oxalylglycine (Sigma-Aldrich). The association and dissociation of PHD2 were performed under the same time conditions as WT and mutant ODD described above. All binding measurements were performed in technical triplicates with aliquots of PHD2 from the same purification.
