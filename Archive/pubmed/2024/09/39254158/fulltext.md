# Polymers for Disrupting Protein-Protein Interactions – Where Are We and Where We Want to Be


## Abstract
  Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) usually rely on non-covalent forces such as electrostatics, hydrophobic association, and hydrogen bonding. PPIs are central to the cellular signaling and regulatory network that underlies virtually all physiological and pathophysiological processes. It is challenging to target PPIs using traditional small molecule or peptide-based approaches due to frequent lack of well-defined binding pockets at the large and flat PPI interfaces. In addition, these chemical matters often suffer from poor solubility or specificity. Synthetic polymers offer an opportunity to circumvent these challenges, by providing unparalleled flexibility in tuning the size, rigidity, charge density, and other physiochemical properties to achieve the desired binding properties. In this review, we summarize the current state of the field pertaining to designing polymer-protein interactions in solution, highlighting various polyelectrolyte systems, their tunable parameters, and techniques for characterizing their interactions with proteins. We provide an outlook on how these design principles can be improved by incorporating sequence control, foldability, and machine learning for polymer architectures that can mimic proteins at the primary, secondary, and tertiary structural levels. Advances in these directions will enable the design of more specific protein-binding polymers and potentially provide an effective strategy for targeting dynamic proteins such as intrinsically disordered proteins.


## Introduction
  Chemists have reasonably mastered the art of drug design, when it involves specific binding to a well-defined, concave pocket of a protein.1–3 On the other hand, designing a competitive binder against a protein-protein interaction (PPI) is quite cumbersome, because of the large and often flat or even convex nature of the interaction interface4–7. The principles and know-hows of conventional drug design are simply not transferrable to tackle this problem. With size and number of functional groups in the same order as those of proteins, artificial macromolecules such as polymers and dendrimers, have the potential to interrupt PPIs more effectively. Although this potential has been recognized for quite some time now,8–11 practical systems have not been demonstrated (or achieved) especially when evaluated in complex environments exemplified by that of a cell. Nonetheless, the idea of designing polymers to recognize specific proteins in complex environments represents a grand challenge in polymer chemistry. In this review, we will survey the status of the field, focusing on the available chemistry explored and lessons learned in existing approaches. We first start with an overview of how nature has engineered complementary surfaces of the proteins involved in PPIs, as this sets the stage for what is needed for designer macromolecules to be competitive. We will close this review with thoughts on how advances polymer chemistry, molecular modeling and machine learning could make a concerted impact in designing artificial macromolecules for interrupting PPIs.


## Protein-protein interactions (PPIs)
  PPIs are tightly controlled in complex cellular environments. To gain some perspective on the level of complexity, we need to consider the fact that the human genome contains ~20,000 proteins. With many of these proteins as substrates for post-translational modifications at various sites, the actual proteome is thought to contain well over a million different protein products.12,13 Considering the enormous number of permutation-combinations possible, it is astounding that Nature has developed ways for highly specific PPIs, all within the confines of a micron-sized mammalian cells. This complexity highlights the difficulty in tackling numerous human diseases that are caused by aberrant PPIs.14–16 The task of engineering macromolecules that specifically interrupt the interaction between two proteins in a very crowded cellular environment, while sparing other PPIs that are critical for biological function, is clearly daunting. As such, designing an artificial macromolecule that could disrupt a specific PPI is considered a grand challenge. The first step to tackle this complex challenge is to develop an understanding of the structural basis by which nature has engineered PPI interfaces.

  The specificity of proteins towards their binding partners lies in their sequence specific functional group variations and their exquisitely precise spatial presentation at the interface as dictated by the 3D structures. Nature uses this presentation to tightly regulate interfacial complementarity through a combination of noncovalent interactions, including electrostatics, van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions.17–23 While small molecule ligand binding pockets tend to be hydrophobic, PPI interfaces have been shown to be enriched with charges.24 Additionally, a PPI interface can vary in contact area, polarity, protrusion, and flatness. These structural and physiochemical features can be modulated by solution conditions such as pH, ionic strength, redox potential, and temperature, which can further alter the strength and specificity of PPIs.

  A useful starting point in understanding the physical basis of affinity and specificity in PPI is to identify so-called hot spots at the interface.25–29 Hot spots refer to key residues that are critical for maintaining binding at the interface, which are often identified through mutagenesis experiments such as alanine-screening.30,31 These studies have revealed that most PPI hotspots are surrounded by a set of energetically less important residues, which may form an “O-ring”, the function of which is to exclude water molecules at this site (Figure 1).31–35 Solvent exclusion at the interface dramatically enhances the energetics of interactions between the complementary functional groups at the interface. For example, the stability of electrostatic interaction between a solvent-exposed pair of opposite charges is weakened by a factor of ~80 (dielectric constant of water at room temperature) as compared to that of a buried one in a hydrophobic and vacuum-like environment.36 Similarly, a typical backbone hydrogen bonding interaction (N-H….O=C) is approximately 5–8 kcal/mol when shielded from solvent, whereas it only amounts to 0.5–1.5 kcal/mol when exposed to solvent (note that kT is worth ~0.6 kcal/mol).37 The level of solvent exposure and local electrostatic environment can also dramatically shift the pKa of titratable groups and thus their protonation states to modulate the PPI specificity and strength.36

  Extensive efforts have been undertaken to inhibit or stabilize PPIs using small molecule inhibitors, peptides, and antibodies.38–43 While significant strides have been made, specificity in interrupting PPIs remains an unresolved challenge, and small molecules are generally found to be ineffective in outcompeting the nature partner and inhibiting PPIs.44 Although peptide biologics provide similarities to endogenous ligands like mimicking important secondary structures along the PPI interface, they also face challenges such as low membrane permeability, immunogenicity, and short half-lives.40,45–48 Polymeric molecules offer a unique opportunity because of their ease of synthesis and purification, dynamic properties, design tunability, ability to cover large contact areas, and potential stability in cellular environment. Moreover, their topology and size offer to make polyvalent contacts with protein surfaces which could cooperatively enhance or reduce the strength of the interaction.49


## Polymer-protein complexation
  Under different pH conditions, protein surfaces display an overall net-neutral, positive, or negative charge. When two protein interfaces bind, the pKa of ionizable groups at the interface can be altered due to the solvation or electrostatic changes triggered by the binding event, resulting in protonation and deprotonation of the residues as well as co-ion release.50–52 The binding process of negatively charged proteins to positively charged solid surfaces also displaces counter ions and water molecules from both surfaces, leading to an overall increase in entropy. Historically, polymers that have repeated units of ionizable or ionized groups, also known as polyelectrolytes, were used to complex with proteins based on the net surface charge complementarity.53 Similar to PPIs, the binding affinity of polyelectrolyte is also largely driven by entropy increase due to the counterion release when interacting with complementary polyvalent surfaces.54 A great example of polyelectrolyte-protein interaction is the DNA-histone interactions, which form the basis for regulating gene expression in cells.55,56

  Considering charge complementarity alone in polymer-protein interactions is clearly over-simplified because proteins are polyampholytes. Even when proteins display a net charge, there are patches of neutral, positively, and negatively charged units on the surface that may interact with non-complementary polymers. For example, Chymotrypsin, which carries a net positive charge, is intuitively more prone to complex with polyanions57,58; however, it has been shown that it can also interact with polycations leading to enhanced selectivity towards anionic substrates at higher charge density.59 Similarly, other studies also reported bovine serum albumin (BSA), a net negative protein, having two large patches of positive charge that can facilitate binding to polyanions at pH 8.60,61 Such interactions are referred to as occurring on the “wrong side” of the protein’s isoelectric point (pI), where both protein and polymer have the same net charge.62 From an electrostatic standpoint, there are two possible reasons for this type of complexation to occur: 1.) the charge-patch mechanism suggests that the local attractive interactions must overcome the overall repulsive interactions63 and 2.) the charge-regulation mechanism suggests that highly charged molecules on protein surface can alter the charges of weak acidic and basic amino acid residues to form strong attractive interactions.64,65 These two explanations are not mutually exclusive.66 Other possible explanations include presence of short-range non-electrostatic interactions or hydrophobic interactions.67,68 Thus, it is important to account for the complex topography of protein surfaces when designing polymers for specific recognition.

  In the subsequent sections, we will summarize key parameters that have been used to tune polymer-protein interactions (Figure 2). Lessons from these existing studies provide a foundation for developing more effective strategies for designing future recognition molecules.


## Parameters for modulating complexation

### Polymer charge density
  Charge density plays a central role in determining the strength of polymer-protein interactions.69,70 The following section summarizes several factors that modulate complexation, with predominant focus on sequence characteristics and environmental factors that affect overall electrostatic interactions between polyelectrolytes. In addition to these factors, polymer design may also consider secondary structures and tertiary structures exhibited by polymers, which is further discussed in Section 6. Experimental and theoretical models mainly use homopolymers and copolymers consisting of charged monomers due to their simplified framework.70 Generally, polymers with higher charge density result in stronger interactions and the charge densities are typically adjusted by varying the molecular weight (MW) or grafting ratio. To increase charge density, a higher MW has been utilized. For instance, the MW of poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) has been shown to affect the binding interactions with proteins. Two different MWs of PAA (100,000 Da and 240,000 Da) were studied for their interaction with α-amylase. Both MW showed no significant loss in the catalytic activity of the enzyme, however a higher binding constant was observed for higher MW PAA.71 For homopolymers comprised only one type of charged monomer, when polymer’s molecular weight increases, so does its capacity to harbor an amplified number of charged units, which potentially influences its physicochemical properties and interactions within its environment. Additionally, in the case of chitosan polymers, amino groups were deacetylated to modulate charge density and thus the overall interaction strength, because a greater number of amino groups were unmasked.72

  Other factors have also been employed to adjust charge density to influence the strength of the interaction; these include changing the grafting ratio or co-incorporating uncharged functionalities into the system. For example, the interaction strength of poly-l-lysine (PLL) and BSA decreased as amino groups in PLL were grafted with polyethylene glycol (PEG). Whereas, at a fixed grafting ratio, PLL-graft(g)-PEG with higher MW displayed more encapsulation of BSA than one with the lower MW. This suggests that a balance between MW and grafting ratio is required to obtain an optimum interaction efficiency.73 Similarly, modularity can be employed in designing polymeric systems such that nonspecific electrostatic interactions can be minimized. For instance, chitosan-based systems have been used for protein complexation and delivery; however, its cationic nature often leads to promiscuous interactions with oppositely charged plasma proteins, resulting in the formation of protein corona. Coating the surface with PEG can reduce the corona formation.74,75

  In contrast to the findings described above regarding the influence of MW on protein complexation, several alternative studies have reported an opposing effect of MW.76,77 For example, chitosan with varied MW showed anomalous interactions with human serum albumin (HSA). It was observed that the higher 200 kDa MW chitosan had weaker binding affinity compared to those of 100 kDa MW. The stability order of the complexation exhibited a trend where 100 > 200 > 15 kDa. With an increase in chitosan MW from 15 to 100 kDa, there was a rise in the number of charged sites, leading to enhanced binding of HSA to the polymer via electrostatic interaction.78 The anomalous behavior was attributed to the emergence of hydrophobic interactions in the presence of a larger chitosan (Ch-200), leading to its aggregation in solution. This would facilitate polymer–polymer hydrophobic interactions in the presence of less hydrophobic HSA and possibly weaken the interaction between HSA and chitosan.

  Even though increasing charge density should generally improve interaction, this was not always the case as discussed. Achieving optimal binding to a specific protein will likely require a balance among charge density, MW, and grafting ratio (Figure 3). At this point, predicting the “sweet spot” for specific protein binding is a challenge but imperative for rational design of optimal complexation with proteins.


### Type of charge groups
  In general, any polymer-protein pairs with complementary net charges will be complexed. Varying the type of charged groups such as amine, guanidine, carboxylic acid, phosphate, and sulfate may be beneficial for tuning polymer-protein interactions (Figure 4a). Compared to amines, arginine can form multiple types of interactions such as hydrogen bonding 36,79,80 and even π-stacking (Figure 4b).8182 In receptor heterodimerization, electrostatic interactions between arginine and phosphates of phosphorylated-serine have demonstrated “covalent-like” stability that was able to withstand fragmentation by mass spectrometric collision-induced dissociation and plasmon resonance (Figure 4c).83 This could be attributed to the guanidium group having a delocalized positive charge distributed over the entire group which provides triple resonance stabilization. Another possibility is the spatial arrangement of the guanidinium cation being flat and trigonal and the phosphate ester being tetrahedral around the phosphorus. From the perspective of the P atom exactly opposite from the P-O ester bond to serine, the three oxygens involved in the phosphate anion resonance can geometrically complement the arrangement of positively charged N atoms of guanidinium ion.

  Similarly, depending on the pH of the environment, carboxylate can harbor one negative charge, while sulfates and phosphates can support multiple interactions84, which could be beneficial in tuning polymer-protein interactions. For example, according to the re-entrant liquid condensation model85 protein condensation is driven by binding to ions of opposite charge, which results in neutralization followed by charge inversion at higher ionic strengths. Re-entrant condensation (RC) was observed for lysozyme interaction with tripolyphophates (TPP) and pyrophosphate (PP).86 Although other anionic molecules such as citrate have the same size and ζ-potentials as pyrophosphate, these do not induce RC despite being able to neutralize and invert lysozyme charge. This is attributed to the stronger electrostatic interactions arising from the more polarized P-O− bonds, relative to that of the C-O− bonds. Additionally, phosphate groups have different hybridization so their interactions with hydrogen bond donors are more flexible.87 This resultant decrease in steric hindrance causes phosphates to interact and cross-link lysozyme molecules during RC. Similarly, in a study observing RC of intrinsically disordered protein histatin (Hst5), arginine showed stronger binding to phosphate groups of PP and TPP, compared to lysine despite having similar charge.88 Here, Hst5 proteins were modified by replacing arginine with lysine at different locations and density. The results showed that the RC of Hst5 depends on the number of arginine residues rather than their sequence distribution, which was reasoned to be due to the guanidinium group forming additional hydrogen-bonding interactions with phosphate groups.

  Polyphosphates (polyP) was observed to have additional advantages such as stabilizing the complexation because it can bind weakly to proteins and have less adverse effects on protein structure compared to polysulfoanions like poly(styrenesulfonate) (PSS) and dextran sulfate.89 This is due to polysulfoanions having tighter interactions from the more electronegative sulfur compared to phosphorus. Comparison between PSS, dextran, and polyP showed that PSS was more destructive on protein conformation due to the presence of sulfate and hydrophobic groups. If hydrophobic groups were removed, as in the case with dextran, the complexation with proteins is seen to be slightly stronger, but denaturation was still observed due to the strong affinity of sulfate groups. In contrast, the presence of only phosphate groups in polyP completely suppressed protein denaturation. MD simulations further revealed different levels of counterion release between PSS and polyP, showing that polyP has lower affinity to proteins than sulfated and sulfonated polymers.90 It is important to note that although there was a trend between polyP, PSS, and dextran sulfate, direct comparison may not be appropriate since there could be other factors beyond electrostatics that could influence binding. In the case of PSS, two factors are simultaneously varied, viz., hydrophobicity and sulfation. Likewise, high density of hydroxy groups in dextran sulfate could contribute hydrogen-bonding based stabilizing forces, as observed with cellulose.91,92 Overall, although phosphates are claimed to be a better choice for anionic interactions than sulfates, because the stronger electrostatic interactions of the latter caused undesirable higher order structural changes in proteins,89 this conclusion should be considered provisional as the influence of other factors such as hydrophobicity has not been delineated.90

  Depending on the protein surface, point modification in polymer structure can also display increased affinity. This can be seen in the case of antithrombin-heparin interaction. The 3-sulfo-N-sulfo glucosamine modification contributes to ~60% of the binding free energy, where replacement of this residue resulted in 105-fold decrease in affinity.93 A library of heparan sulfate (HS) tetrasaccharides modified at N-, 2-O- and 6-O-positions from a universal HS building block (GlcN-IdoA-GlcN-IdoA) demonstrated that HS-binding relied more on specific interactions rather than overall negative charge, specifically sulfation at the 2-O-position.94 Similarly, absence of sulfation in the 2-O position in IdoA for heparin-antithrombin binding had no significant effect on binding conformation but resulted in two-fold decrease in affinity.95 Overall, the natural sequence of heparin sulfate is more selective than heparin since hydrophobicity and charge density can be synthetically varied.96 In addition, acrylamidomethylpropanesulfonate and acrylamide copolymers were observed to have the highest affinity to BSA based on sulfation patterns. These copolymers were simple enough where they could achieve random but defined distributions of charge patterns without the complications of variable bond flexibility and H-bonding. It was shown that copolymers with short runs of charged residues (25% charged) bracketed by long runs of uncharged residues achieved the highest affinity because uncharged regions minimize electrostatic repulsion from distant polyanion residues.97


### Hydrogen bonds
  Another force crucial for polymer-protein interactions is hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonds have partial covalent characteristics but are mainly electrostatic in nature since the hydrogen atom of the donor molecule is partially positive while the acceptor molecule is partially negative. As implicated by the secondary electrostatic interactions (SEI) model, the magnitude of stabilization is dependent on the polar functional groups around the binding site.98 Computationally, there are criticisms for this model such as: (1) the SEI model considers only the frontier atoms rather than all possible atom-atom interactions; (2) it is only applicable to molecules with known approximated charged densities; (3) it ignores how hydrogen bonding also carries a partial covalent character; and (4) the model does not account for long range electrostatic interactions.98 However, experimentally, the SEI model holds relatively true. For example, rather than alternating between hydrogen donor and acceptor in a sequence, when hydrogen donor atoms are grouped, there is a larger accumulation of positive charge around their frontier atoms and vice versa for hydrogen acceptor atoms where there is an accumulation of negative charge. This can result in stronger electrostatic interactions and a stronger orbital interaction because the charge accumulation decreases the HOMO-LUMO gap.99 This can also be understood by the fact that the strength of hydrogen bonds, accompanied by an ionic component, range between 10 and 45 kcal mol−1 compared to 4−15 kcal mol−1 for the stability of those between neutral molecules.100

  In terms of ligand chemistry, guanidyl groups are reported to form strong hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with amides and oxyanions in proteins.101 For example, the multivalent display of guanidyl groups on a polymer can significantly strengthen the affinity between BSA and the guanidine containing branched polyethylenimine (bPEI) polymer.102 Similarly, a polymer with the carboxylic acid groups (poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether acrylate-b-poly(carboxyethyl acrylate (PEGMEA-b-PCEA)) was studied for its ability to interact with lysozyme (pI ~ 11). Here, hydrogen bonding along with van der Waals forces were the crucial forces to hold the protein-polymer assembly even at low pHs of 4.5 and 3.0, where electrostatic interactions were unfavorable due to similar charges on the polymer and protein.103 There are a variety of polymers that exhibit hydrogen bond based interactions to form unique assemblies.104–107 Such systems can be translated in studying protein-polymer interactions for understanding H-bond driven complexation.108–110


### Non-polar associations
  Along with electrostatics and H-bonds, protein folding is highly dependent on other non-polar interactions which arise from the attractive van der Waals and hydrophobic forces between non-polar amino acids.103,111–114 Such interactions protect hydrophobic core of proteins from solvent exposure thereby providing stability. Attempt to perturb these non-polar associations among buried nonpolar residues can lead to destabilization of the native protein structure at both the secondary and tertiary levels. Nonetheless, exploiting such interactions along with electrostatics in the polymer-protein complexation has the potential to allow stronger interactions and improve binding specificity. For example, the protein Ovalbumin (OVA), which contains hydrophobic patches on its surface, has indeed been observed to show higher affinity towards a hydrophobic polymer poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm) than HSA based on binding numbers measured by static light scattering.108 Similarly, PEG polymers are well known to bind with a variety of proteins via hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and hydrogen bonding interactions.109,115 For example, anthracene-PEG, where end groups in the PEG chain are based on anthracene moiety, showed stronger binding towards BSA than corresponding PEG polymers with -CH3 as end groups110. The opposite trend was observed in the case of HSA, a less hydrophobic protein. Thus, the hydrophobic/hydrophilic balance of a polymer influences its ability to bind with the protein of interest.

  Though we have discussed polymers with neutral moieties, there are studies that incorporate hydrophobic groups in charged polymers for understanding hydrophobic interactions between polymer and protein. This creates an interesting complexation between polymers and proteins where both electrostatic and non-electrostatic interactions are at play. In these scenarios as well, hydrophobic interactions can potentially impart destabilization to proteins even with the presence of electrostatic interactions. For example, four basic proteins (chymotrypsinogen A (pI ~ 9.1), ribonuclease A (pI ~ 9.6), cytochrome c (pI ~ 10.1), and lysozyme (pI ~ 10.7)) were studied for their interaction with four polyanions with similar persistence length, but different hydrophobicity based on their partition coefficient (Nafion, poly(4-styrene-sulfonate) (PSS), heparin, and poly(vinyl sulfate) (PVS). It is suggested that between hydrophobic (Nafion and PSS) and hydrophilic polyanions (PVS and heparin), hydrophobic ones perturb protein core more and destabilize secondary and tertiary structures based on differential scanning calorimetry studies. Since hydrophobic interaction are unrelated to variations in charge, at high ionic strength, Nafion and PSS bound proteins provided still stable complexes, whereas those with PVS and heparin were weakened due to loss of electrostatic interactions at high ionic strength.114 Similarly, the α-helices of BSA are significantly lost when it binds with a strongly hydrophobic and cationic polymer, quaternized poly(chloromethyl styrene), as examined by SANS and CD studies.116 Therefore, although introducing hydrophobic interactions in proteins is an interesting way to bind with proteins irrespective of perturbations in parameter which would have negative impact on electrostatic forces (e.g. pH and ionic strength), it also comes with a potential limitation of destabilizing proteins. A balanced polymer with charged and uncharged groups could thus be a useful system.


### pH
  Extracellular and intracellular environments have different conditions ranging from pH 7.4 in blood serum to pH 6.5–5.5 in endosomes to pH 4.5–5.0 in lysosomes.117 These variations can alter the charge of both the protein and polymer and potentially their conformations, thus impacting their interaction. The pI of the protein and pKa of the polymer determines their charge in solution. When the solution pH is greater than the pI or pKa values, protein or polymer acquire a negative charge and vice versa for positive charge. Overall, the appropriate pKa of the polymer should be chosen to reflect the targeted environment for optimal interaction (Figure 5a). The effects of pH on electrostatic interactions have been extensively explored experimentally and computationally. For example, the influence of pH on protein-polymer interaction was investigated using PAA and lysozyme (pI ~11) as a model system. PAA is fully charged at alkaline pH and adopts an extended conformation, while under acidic conditions it is heavily protonated and becomes collapsed (Figure 5b).118 The study investigated the dominance of electrostatic interactions when the binding partners possess opposite charges and concluded that the binding was dictated by the ability to achieve charge neutrality at the protein−polymer interface. Different titratable groups can exhibit distinct responses to pH changes due to their different pKa values, allowing the design of polymers with desired charge polarity under a given pH environment. For instance, branched polyethylenimine (PEI) polymers containing primary, secondary, and tertiary amines were utilized to study the pH dependence when interacting with glucose oxidase (GOx).119 Amine groups underwent varying degrees of protonation depending on the surrounding pH, which resulted in a notable pH-dependent alteration in PEI’s ionization state. The pH effect on GOx activity was directly correlated with the electrostatic conditions of GOx–PEI complex formation. GOx, with a pI of 4.2, and branched PEI, with an average pKa ranging between 8.2 and 9.4 depending on its molecular weight, demonstrated altered charge states at different pH levels. At pH < 4.2, both GOx and PEI were positively charged, impeding favorable electrostatic interactions and complex formation. Conversely, at pH >4.2, GOx being negatively charged, facilitated interaction with PEI.

  With the change in pH, different modes of interactions can also be found. A recent study showed that non-covalent forces dictate the interaction between kidney-bean protein isolate (KPI; pI~5) and sodium alginate (SA) composite and vary with pH. Specifically, at pH 7.0, hydrogen bonding was the most significant force because KPI and SA have numerous negatively charged groups.120 But as pH approaches the pI, hydrophobic interactions became dominant causing formation of more insoluble aggregates of KPI and KPI-bound SA. Furthermore, below the pI, KPI’s amino groups became protonated which facilitated electrostatic interaction with SA.


### Ionic strength
  Ionic strength, as determined by the concentrations of different ionic species in a medium, both cations and anions, is a key parameter that controls the strength of electrostatic interactions in solution and affects protein structure, activity, and association. 121–123 The degree of ionization can also be changed by varying the salt concentration, which in turn will affect the interaction and complexation between the polymer and protein. At low ionic strength (Itotal= 20 mM), the complexation between BSA and NaPSS can occur on the “wrong side” at two different binding stages.60 The first stage involves formation of intrapolymer complexes which subsequently associates into larger interpolymer complexes. This complexation is possible at low ionic strength because of the presence of two positively charged domains on BSA surface at pH 8, which results in the coexistence of short-range attractive and long-range repulsive electrostatic forces between the two. Intuitively, higher ionic strength can weaken the polar interaction between polymer and protein (Figure 6a). For example, when the interaction HSA with PAA in an aqueous solution was studied as a function of added salt concentration (20–100 mM), the free energy of binding (ΔGb) decreases with increasing salt concentration, which is dominated by entropic contributions from the release of bound counterions (Figure 6c).54,124However, the influence of ionic strength on complexation is strongly dependent on the type of polymer-protein interaction. To illustrate, the binding of polyanion to the positive domain results in a combination of short-range attractive interactions coupled with longer-range repulsive interactions due to the dipolar asymmetry on the protein surface. For example, three pairs of protein-polyanions were studied: BSA with poly(acrylic acid)-grafted (g)-octyldecylamine, BSA with porcine mucosal heparin, and insulin with heparin. All three highly diverse systems display maxima or plateaus in complex formation in the range of ionic strength 5 to 30 mM.68 This indicated that long range electrostatics driven by ionic strength is responsible for protein-polymer association, although binding affinity is determined by the short-range interactions between complementary recognition motifs. The anisotropy of electrostatic domains around a protein plays a major role in determining the ionic strength dependence of its binding to a polyelectrolyte. Similarly, within polyphosphates such as tetra-P (TPP), S-polyP (10–15 phosphates), and L-polyP (60–70 phosphates), longer chains demonstrated higher affinity (ΔHbind) but induces more aggregation. When phosphate ions linked by phosphoanhydride bonds bind to lysozyme, they may prevent water molecules from forming a hydration structure at the protein surface due to their synergistic kosmotropic effect.125

  However, many studies observe non-monotonic behavior as ionic strength increases, which could be due to a complex interplay of electrostatic, non-electrostatic, hydration, conformational, and competitive effects.60,126 For example, increasing ionic strength leads to enhancement of interaction energy by preserving strong interactions between an amphiphilic polyelectrolyte poly(N,N-diallyl-N-hexyl-N-methylammonium chloride) and the surface components of a globular protein (e.g. lysozyme) even at high salt concentration.127 Interestingly, even when hydrophobic interactions coexist with other interactions, this trend is still observed in several studies. For example, in a study of the KPI-SA complex formation, hydrophobic interactions were gradually enhanced with an increase in Ca2+ concentration (20–80 mg/mL). Due to the interaction between the Ca2+ ions and KPI negative ion groups, salt bridges form between the proteins. Therefore, this resulted in an increase in agglomeration in the system, as well as an enhanced hydrophobic interaction between the KPI and SA. Overall increasing ionic strength positively affected the gel properties, because the added Ca2+ ions influenced dissolution of the components as well as intermolecular crosslinking.120

  Change in solution ionic strength also affects the morphology of protein-polymer complexes. With the increase of NaCl concentration from 0 to 300 mM, the stability and phase behavior of chitosan/casein complexes systematically changes.128 The complex diameter and polydispersity index decreased from 450 to 300 nm and 0.40 to 0.20, respectively. This could be explained by screening of electrostatic repulsion between chitosan chains at high ionic strength and diminishing of osmotic pressure difference between the solvent and the interior of complexes. However, electrostatic interaction played a major role in this reported complexation method. Depending on the component structures, the effect of ionic strength on complex formation varies as function of electrostatic attraction as well as H-bonding and hydrophobic interaction.


### Temperature
  The temperature dependence of enthalpy (ΔH), entropy (ΔS), and Gibbs free energy (ΔG) impacts the interactions between polymers and proteins.129 At moderate temperatures, hydrophobic interactions are intensified due to the liberation of confined water molecules, resulting in a positive change in entropy (ΔS) and increase in interaction (Figure 6b and 6d). For example, the interaction of polystyrene sulfonate, a stereoregular polyelectrolyte (polymers having an ordered arrangement of pendant groups along the chain) with myoglobin was studied as a function of temperature.130 In this study, turbidity measurements showed that no protein-polyion binding was detected at room temperature, but the binding became stronger at elevated temperatures due to the enhanced hydrophobic interactions. However, due to the unfolding and denaturation of proteins at prolonged high temperatures, the interaction eventually weakens.131

  The temperature dependence of salt-bridges and hydrogen bonds is complex, primarily influenced by the intricate response of hydration water.132 Importantly, the fine balance between ΔH and ΔS dictates the strength and type of interaction. The enthalpy and entropy cancellation (EEC) is a phenomenon that generally accompanies the binding of proteins to polyelectrolytes dominated by counterion release.54,133 For example, the binding of HSA to PAA-chains is accompanied by a marked cancellation of ΔS and ΔH.124 EEC is a non-electrostatic phenomenon that is related to the uptake and the release of water molecules during binding. The tighter contacts lead to a more negative enthalpy whereas it increases molecular constraints and thereby decreases disorder. Thus, uptake of water leads to a gain in enthalpy of binding but also to a concomitant loss of entropy. This results in the weak dependence of free energy of binding ΔGb on temperature. These experimentally observed results were further theoretically modeled to understand the role of EEC and the effect of uptake or release of water molecules on the ΔGb and binding affinity of protein-polymer.134 The thermodynamic analysis also provides insights into the driving forces underlying the protein-polymer interaction.


## Techniques for characterizing polymer-protein interactions
  Extensive reviews on characterizing polymer-protein interactions exist.135,136 Here, we summarize main experimental techniques that are used to quantify polymer-protein interactions and characterize their phase separation in solution. Moreover, benefiting from GPU-accelerated simulation algorithms and increasingly powerful computing hardware, computational modelling and simulation have played an expanding role in providing molecular details of polymer-protein interactions to help understand experimental observations and quantitative predictions to guide on further design and characterization (Figure 7a).53,70,137–141 Here, focusing on the widely used molecular dynamics (MD) approach, we will discuss a few computational studies using both atomistic and coarse-grained (CG) models to illustrate how computation and experiment can be integrated to obtain insights into polymer-protein interactions.

  Static and dynamic light scattering provide not only dimensional but also morphological characteristics of polymer-protein complexes. Static light scattering (SLS) is used to quantitatively determine molecular weight and dimensional parameters of these complexes, such as the radius of gyration and fractal dimension.142,143 It is particularly useful for multi-component polymer-protein systems, more specifically, protein-polyelectrolyte complexes because of providing time-dependent changes in the complexation process and also small changes in the complex morphology.144 SLS is often combined with other scattering techniques like small angle scattering, turbidity, or non-scattering methods.116,145 Dynamic light scattering (DLS) tracks the changes in scattering light intensity as a function of time and can be employed to investigate the presence of various polymer-protein systems including unbound protein, intrapolymer complexes, and interpolymer complexes (Figure 7b).146,147 It has also been used to study the impact of environmental factors including pH and temperature on the size and morphology of complex structures.54,118 Challenges in interpreting light scattering data include interference from the presence of strong scatterers, resolving free protein and PE chain dynamics from the complex, and the effects of low salt concentrations on interaction dynamics as the polyelectrolytes lose their coil-like conformations and attain augmented structures.135,148,149 Small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) can be combined with DLS or SLS analysis to investigate structural features at smaller length scales not accessible to light scattering.150,151

  Fluorescence-based techniques, including fluorescence anisotropy, fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS), and fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), are also frequently used to study polymer-protein interactions.152–154 These experiments yield information on binding, rotational freedom, protein-polymer ratio, and distance measurements within these complexes, providing insights into protein behavior in complex with polyelectrolytes. Complementing experimental methods, MD simulations can provide more detailed insights into the structural and dynamic properties of protein-polymer interactions. For instance, multi-scale simulations of the 3D structure of dendritic polyglycerol sulfate up to the sixth generation were able to characterize the effective net charges and corresponding radii, surface charge densities, and surface potentials of these polymers.155 Further simulations suggest that these dendrimers bind with lysozyme in a multivalent fashion.156 In a atomistic simulation study of PEG-BSA complex, domains I and III of BSA were identified as the hotpots for binding PEG, while the PEG chain protruded away from other domains.140 Analysis showed that lysine residues played a dominant role due to the unique ability of PEG to wrap itself around these residues and form looplike conformations.

  Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) has been extensively used to quantitatively analyze the thermodynamic parameters of polymer-protein interactions, including binding affinity, enthalpy changes, and the number of proteins bound to polymer chains.157,158 ITC can also distinguish between electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions by analyzing ΔH and ΔS signatures, shedding light on the driving forces behind these interactions.66,159 For example, protein-polyelectrolyte interactions have been shown to be dominated by counterion release based on ITC measurements along with MD simulations.156,160 It was specifically found that the binding of proteins to strongly charged polyelectrolytes is mainly derived by the release of condensed counterions on the polyelectrolytes.161 With the help of enhanced sampling methods, experimentally comparable binding free energies can be obtained, providing support for MD simulation as a powerful tool for revealing underlying driving forces.53 For example, the analysis of the binding free energy of lysozyme and dendritic polyglycerol sulfate at different temperatures and salt concentrations shows that the simulated results are in quantitative agreement with those from ITC, in both the trend and absolute values.162 The total free energy of binding calculated can be decomposed into contributions from different interactions and/or among different polymer and protein motifs, providing quantitative insights into the molecular basis of complexation. Applied to HSA-dPGS binding, the nonlinear van’t Hoff analysis of binding free energy revealed a significant heat capacity change accompanied by a strong enthalpy-entropy compensation, where the binding affinity is largely dictated by electrostatic interactions.138

  Depending on the nature of polymer-protein interactions, different physicochemical properties have been investigated experimentally. The properties of interest dictate the type of analytical techniques to be employed. To illustrate, polymer-protein interactions can lead to liquid-liquid phase separation163, which can be studied using techniques such as turbidimetry. Turbidity measurements provide insight into macroscale phase separation of protein in presence of charged polymer, viz. polyelectrolytes, emphasizing the importance of stoichiometry and the impact of solution properties like ionic strength and pH on polymer-protein interactions (Figure 7c).69,164,165 However, turbidimetric measurements cannot draw a clear distinction between coacervation and precipitation due to aggregation. Hence light scattering techniques are complementary to the turbidimetric measurements to obtain a more complete picture. Phase-separated polymer-protein solutions can undergo so-called aging and lead to gelation or coacervation, which in turn modulates the viscoelastic properties of the material.166 This has been investigated using rheology, as shown in a recent study of shear-dependent elasticity propulsion of bovine serum albumin and chitosan/poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride)-derived coacervates.167

  The impact of polymer-protein interactions at molecular scale, such as change in polymer coiling or effect on the protein structure in solution, has been assessed using biomolecular (or macromolecular) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. For instance, direct saturation compensated (DISCO) saturation transfer difference (STD) and saturation transfer double difference (STDD) NMR experiments have been employed to decipher BSA binding fingerprints on different synthetic and natural polymer surfaces. These isotopic labeling-free techniques were used to further investigate the binding parameters at atomic-level resolution (Figure 7d).168,169 In another study, Nuclear Overhauser Effect (NOE)-based NMR was employed to investigate the protein adsorption on nanoparticle surfaces.170 Isotope labeling was necessary for NOE, but not for STD.171,172 STD does not require labeling since it focuses on the ligand protons rather than the proximity of the ligand to the protein nucleus172. These interactions have also been probed using mass spectrometric (MS) techniques. For instance, conformational changes of lysozyme due to interaction with polystyrene sulfonate of various chain lengths were investigated using trapped ion mobility spectrometry-time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TIMS-TOF-MS).173 However, although ion mobility MS spectrometry is often utilized to understand protein’s tertiary structure or PPI, its application in understanding polymer-protein interactions has not been extensively explored despite the great potential as a high-throughput, label-free quantitative tool.174

  A wide range of imaging techniques have been applied to examine the structure and morphology of polymer-protein complexes. To directly visualize events associated with polymer-protein interactions, microscopic techniques are used for endpoint as well as time-dependent analysis of such complexation. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) has been applied to visualize protein distribution on polyelectrolyte surfaces, such as within capsules, gels, coacervates, or particles.175–180 CLSM also helped to visualize protein release from the formed complexes,181 as well as protein trapping and extraction in food science.177,182 The diffusion of biomolecules within coacervates stabilized by a dextran was studied through the implementation of fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments in combination with CLSM (Figure 7e).183 However, the application of CLSM often requires labeling of the components. Nonetheless, super-resolution optical microscopes are in development to overcome the diffraction limit and image such interactions with elevated clarity. For example, stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy is one such technique to investigate coacervates formed due to protein-polyelectrolyte interaction.136,184 Electron microscopy (EM) has been widely used and often considered to be label-free to study morphology and crystallinity of polymer-protein complexes. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), with cryo techniques, has provided valuable insights into these complex structures, revealing dense assembly formations within them 185,186.


## Summary and perspectives
  Numerous different PPIs occur simultaneously and specifically in the complex and crowded cellular environment. The idea of engineering molecules that can mimic or interrupt these interactions is challenging, because the PPI interface varies extensively depending on protein sequence and structure as well as the cellular environment where the interaction occurs. Many recognition molecules such as small molecules and peptides have not been able to overcome the structural complexity and topography of protein surfaces that dictate these specific interactions. Though antibodies possess the ability to recognize protein surfaces with high specificity, they face constraints due to their expensive production, poor stability at ambient conditions, transport inside the cells, and tendency to induce immune responses. Synthetic polymers offer a promising and cost-effective solution to these challenges by leveraging a wide array of chemical functionalities for design. Their design and synthesis are adaptable, the products provide stability, relatively lower immune response, and desired polyvalency to match the target protein surface. In this review, we have highlighted various polymer-protein systems and key factors that influence their interactions studied in the field thus far. The “lessons-learned” can be summarized as follows:

  While current polymer designs have demonstrated efficient complexation for their specific applications, they are often formulated based on charge complementarity and for isolated proteins. With electrostatics as the main basis for interaction, current designs are not yet sophisticated enough to differentiate between two proteins of similar size and pI (e.g., membrane proteins hydrogen peroxidase (35 kDa, pI ~6.35) and carbonic anhydrase XII (39 kDa, pI ~6.4–6.6) or among many proteins in the complex cellular environment. Additionally, current studies mainly revolve around globular proteins and do not account for intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), which make up about one-third of eukaryotic proteins.187 IDPs are dynamic proteins that do not have stable tertiary structures under physiological conditions in the unbound state.188–190 Their conformational flexibility stems from their lack of hydrophobic, aromatic, and cysteine amino acid residues that are generally required for forming a hydrophobic core for structural stability. IDPs are key components of the cellular signaling network and their conformational flexibility is believed to provide key advantages such as being adaptive to multiple specific targets, achieving large binding surface with a small interaction domain, and the ability to integrate multiple types of cellular signals.190 Mis-regulation and mis-signaling of IDPs have been implicated in many diseases, including cancers, diabetes and neurodegenerative diseases, and are considered an important but challenging new class of drug targets.191–195 It has been observed that small molecules could bind IDPs through hydrophobic regions, often through nonspecific and short-lived interactions.196–198 Nonetheless, targeting IDPs with small molecules may not be effective due to the small interaction interface achievable. Synthetic polymers may be uniquely suitable for targeting IDPs.

  Advances in diverse fields including polymer chemistry, molecular biophysics, MD simulation, and machine learning can provide new insights to guide the molecular design of PPI-targeting polymers. To take the next steps, artificial macromolecules must display controlled variations that mimic proteins at the primary, secondary, and higher order structural levels.


### Sequence-defined polymers (SDPs) and random heteropolymers (RHPs)
  Introducing sequence patterns into polymers can have an enormous impact on molecular recognition and provide an essential extra handle for functionality. This is a particularly important aspect to mimic proteins, where at the primary structural level amino acids are arranged in a particular order and elicit specific structure-function relationships.199 “Sequence-defined” implies an absolute control over monomer positioning and pattern within the linear chain; hence, chemical information can be controlled at the molecular level (Figure 8a). Though it is challenging to precisely control the sequence of the monomer in a growing polymer chain, several research groups are trying to overcome this challenge by using advanced synthetic strategies.200 Generally, sequence pattern is introduced by utilizing efficient coupling reactions and suitable chain growth strategies as described in a number of recent reviews.199–201 Synthetic SDPs are unique due to their unlimited functionalities, better environmental and biological stability, and flexible chemical and physical properties via modular functional groups. Multiple parameters can be modified to regulate macromolecule properties, including the chemical structure, average molar mass, copolymer composition, tacticity, and dispersity. Incorporating functionalities such as hydroxyl, amine and carboxylic groups in the monomer facilitates hydrogen bonding, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interactions among the SDPs to form assemblies in solution. In one study, precision controlled peptoid synthesis strategy was implemented to design a sequence defined bioinspired peptoid diblock copolymer. These 30-mer copolymers were able to form superhelices due to electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions in the hydrophilic block (Figure 8a).202

  Based on the pattern of hydrogen bonds between amino hydrogens and carboxyl oxygen atoms in the peptide backbone, the placement of amino acids in a sequence can dictate how a polypeptide folds due to the local spatial conformation. With this understanding, choosing a particular functional group in a particular sequence can promote or disrupt certain structure formation. For example, proline and glycine are sometimes known as “helix breakers” since they disrupt the helical backbone conformation.203 Conversely, their unusual conformational abilities may be incorporated in β-turns. Many PPIs involve helices in one of the two binding domains while sheet-like structures are more imbedded within proteins as they tend to aggregate and require stabilization in tertiary folds. Combined with sequence control, stereocontrol can also be employed in the polymer design to control their function and properties. Stereocontrol can be achieved in iterative polymer synthesis by implementing stereoselective reactions or using chiral building blocks.204 Stereocontrol is mainly related to the polymers’ secondary and tertiary structures and followed by the folding ability which is a characteristic of biotic macromolecules and a prerequisite to inheriting complex functionalities. Hence, incorporating both sequence and stereo control in the SDPs can build an ideal platform to mimic the protein interface and interrupt the PPIs. We note that SDPs parallel naturally occurring IDPs that nature has evolved and extensively utilized for protein binding and recognition in complex cellular environments.

  As mentioned, synthesizing sequence-defined polymers is quite challenging and despite efforts, recent strategies have increasingly focused on using random heteropolymers (RHPs) as protein mimics.205,206 Rather than matching the exact amino acid sequence, RHPs are polymers that contain two or more monomers arranged in a random sequence and have statistically similar chemical features and spatial distribution as those of protein surfaces. Utilizing reversible deactivation radical polymerization has made it feasible to synthesize RHPs with reliable control over statistical monomer distribution.207,208 These RHPs have been shown to exhibit protein-like behaviors and functions such as helping protein fold, stabilizing proteins in non-polar environments, and forming proton membrane channels.137,209 Thus, future studies that incorporate machine learning can significantly aid in the evolution and screening of random polymers.


### Foldamers
  To enhance protein recognition, it is also important to mimic protein secondary structures since numerous PPI interfaces often involve helices in one of the two binding domains. As mentioned, incorporating stereocontrol in sequenced polymers can introduce local foldability. The field of foldamers also offers control over secondary structures mainly using amino acid derived monomers (Figure 8b). To recreate protein-like secondary structures, chemists have designed oligomers from various backbones that differ from natural α-peptides, termed “foldamers”. These synthetic oligomers dynamically fold and unfold in solution based on noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatics, π-stacking, Van der Waals, and/or solvophobicity. The foldamer field has an extensive library of backbones with various modifications to choose from as described in several reviews.210–215 Many adopt helical shapes and are widely studied as PPI disruptors.216,217 Synthesis typically involves designing an appropriate monomer, and a stepwise, controlled elongation into a larger oligomer. Though difficult, recent developments in phosphorus based compounds have made it possible to synthesize high MW oligomer that fold into tertiary structures.218,219 Many sequences are designed to systematically place cyclic and aromatic rings within the oligomer strand for rigidity and π-π stacking to tune the cavity size.220,221 Others incorporate small structural modifications like N-methylation to guide folding based on steric hindrance.222 Additives such as metals, anions, and chiral molecules can induce turns in a host-guest manner based on metal-ligand binding, hydrogen bonding, and steric, respectively.223,224 Additionally, MD simulations can provide the sequence-structure relationship by finding the folding equilibria.225,226 Translating the design rules of foldamers to engineer folding polymers can subsequently expand the artificial scaffolds available for interrupting PPI interfaces with different architectures.


### Dendrimers
  Spherical dendrimers could also be ideal candidates to interrupt PPIs, as they can mimic higher order protein structures from their spherical architecture that allows functional groups to be presented in a well-defined constitution on the surface (Figure 8c). In contrast to linear polymers, dendrimers are the only class of artificial macromolecules that can be obtained in high molecular weight in a monodisperse form. Its hyperbranched structure offers many desirable features such as globular conformation and multivalency that make it an excellent choice for biomimicry.227–230 Early studies showed that the maximum interaction with proteins occurred with higher generation dendrimers due to similar interfacial areas.231,232 Similarly, multivalency was found to be crucial for effectively decreasing Aβ oligomer toxicity.231 Dendrimers can be synthesized either by convergent or divergent methods. Using the convergent approach, dendrimers can be made with tight control over chemical composition throughout each layer of the molecule (core, branching units, and peripheral).233–237 Furthermore, specific interactions can be achieved by introducing sequences in dendrimers, particularly at the periphery. By taking advantage of the different reactivities of B and B’ with A monomers, dendrons could be made to contain up to 15-mer (G-3) with different functional groups at the periphery.238 Subsequent studies also demonstrated four unsymmetrically substituted G-1 dendrons were combined to make a G-3 dendron that displayed eight different functionalities.239 The importance of sequence is apparent for both dendrimer recognition and function such that small changes to dendron primary structure can have significant changes to macroscopic properties. For example, relocation of methyl groups between two constitutional isomers dictated tertiary and quaternary structure of self-assembling dendronized perylene bisimide.240 Seemingly daunting, recent development using orthogonally complementary moieties can make synthesis of diverse and sequence-controlled hetero-layered dendrimers simpler by altering the order of monomer addition.241 Overall, the ability to control spatial disposition of functional groups in a sequence-defined manner makes dendrimers quite promising as PPI inhibitors.


### Machine Learning
  Developing more specific design guidelines can be expedited with machine-learning (ML) based approaches. In recent years, data-driven ML has emerged as a useful tool in the study of polymer-protein systems. Different from MD simulations, the inherent “black box” nature makes it hard to rationalize the physics of these systems using ML-based approaches. Instead, the structure-property relationship is directly established, which means ML can recommend specific polymer chemistry required for functions or interactions with proteins. For example, an interpretable ML framework was designed to construct structure-interaction trends from cross-polymer atomic-level behavior patterns.242 It was applied to the polymer-mucin protein system, revealing a potential structure-interaction relationship shared by various polymer ligands. Another recent study employed ML to build a protein tertiary structure-dependent reactivity model to predict the reactivities of lysines for PEG.243 A robotic platform was also established for the design of polymer-protein hybrids, where active ML was used to rapidly design copolymers to form thermostable hybrids with three chemically distinct enzymes (Figure 8d).244 Although ML is a promising tool for facilitating polymer design245, it remains a major challenge to train effective predictive machine learning models. The biggest challenge stems from data availability and quality. Obtaining high-quality data under well-defined conditions that also minimize noise, bias, or incompleteness is difficult. Additionally, it is important to account for the coverage of training data, especially for the transferability and generalization of ML models. Both proteins and polymers have dramatic diversities, which might make the essential training data too enormous to ensure the transferability of models across different types of proteins and polymers. Besides, proteins and polymers are inherently complex with nontrivial structures, which may not be adequately represented using a finite set of features such as charge, polarity, hydrophobicity, dipole moment, hydrogen bonds, and others. How to determine the most important ones and how to represent them in ML models could be particularly challenging. Although ML-aided designs are still in their nascent stages, they have demonstrated significant potential in this field. This might be the next paradigm, where simulations can curate robust databases to provide essential foundational knowledge, while machine learning operates as a high-throughput tool to expediate polymer design and exploration.
