# Structural profiles of the full phagocyte NADPH oxidase unveiled by combining computational biology and experimental knowledge


## Abstract
  The phagocyte NADPH oxidase (NOX2) is an enzyme, crucial for innate immune defense, producing reactive oxygen species necessary for pathogen destruction. Its activation requires the assembly of soluble proteins (p47phox, p40phox, p67phox, and Rac) with the membrane-bound flavocytochrome b558 (cytb558). We combined circular-dichroism analyses, with decades of experimental data, to filter structural models of the NADPH oxidase complex generated by the artificial intelligence program AlphaFold2 (AF2). The predicted patterns tend to closely resemble the active states of the proteins, as shown by the compact structure of the cytb558, whose dehydrogenase domain is stabilized closer to the membrane. The modeling of the interaction of p47phox with cytb558, which is the initial assembly and activation steps of the NADPH oxidase, enables us to describe how the C terminus of p47phox interacts with the cytb558. Combining the AF2 cytb558-p47phox model and its classical molecular dynamics simulations, we highlighted new hydrophobic lipid insertions of p47phox, particularly at residues Trp80-Phe81 of its PX domain. The AF2 models also revealed the implications of intrinsically disordered regions, such as the fragment between the PX domain and the SH3 regions of p47phox, in ensuring distant protein–protein and membrane–protein interactions. Finally, the AF2 prediction of the cytb558-Trimera model highlighted the importance of leaving Rac1 as a separate protein to reach an active state of the NADPH oxidase complex. Altogether, our step-by-step approach provides a structural model of the active complex showing how disordered regions and specific lipid and protein interactions can enable and stabilize the multisubunit assembly.


## Results

### AF2-generated cytb558 model
  We generated the AF2 models of cytb558 before any release of the experimental structures that were published in 2022 after the latest retraining of AF2 (the data cutoff for version 2.3 is in 2021) (7U8G and 8GZ3; (28, 29)) (Fig. 1, A and B). The superposition of the AF2 model with 7U8G and 8GZ3 gives RMSD’s of 0.446 Å and 0.405 Å, respectively (Fig. S1A). The same residues are found at the NOX2/p22phox interface (Fig. S1B), thus illustrating the accuracy of membrane protein complex prediction by AF2. In all five models generated by AF2, the transmembrane domains of NOX2 (6 TM helices) and of p22phox are superimposable and display a very high confidence level (pLDDT > 90) (Fig. 1A). The p22phox subunit, which was the subject of several papers disagreeing on the number of TM helices, was predicted by AF2, prior to any structural experimental release, to be organized in four transmembrane helices with the C- and N-terminal regions oriented towards the intracellular side. This concurs with a structure that was proposed first by the team of E. Pick, based on peptide walking studies performed 20 years ago (47). The C-terminal tail of p22phox displays low pLDDT confidence scores (<70).   The region at the interface between the TM and C-terminal DH domains and some parts of the DH domain are predicted by AF2 with slightly lower confidence, with pLDDT scores ranging from 70 to 90 (Fig. 1A, black frame). Often, but not systematically, low-confidence predictions match unsolved regions of the experimental structures, corresponding to more flexible and disordered regions. This is the case for the N terminus of helix I of NOX2 which is not solved experimentally. When using AF2, it was modeled as a bent helix ending parallel to the membrane close to the lipid head groups that may contribute to stabilizing the cytb558 in the membrane. This was also the case of the region Cys72-Arg96 in NOX2, also unsolved experimentally, which is organized in two small hairpin helices linked with the loop connecting Ser83 and Ser87 (Figs. 1C and S1C). This NOX2 hairpin, which is internally consolidated by a salt bridge between Asp95 and Arg80 (2.7 Å), is lipid-facing and exposes a cluster of aromatic residues (Leu75, Phe78, Leu79, Val90, Leu94) that may contribute to the protein anchoring and positioning inside the membrane. On the other side of the hairpin, a patch of positively charged residues (Arg79, Arg89, Arg91, Arg92) interacts electrostatically with the Asp396 and Glu397 residues of the NOX2 DH, thus stabilizing the position of the DH domain. Finally, in the DH domain of NOX2, the 483 to 507 fragment is unsolved in the experimental structure while in the AF2 model, this fragment displays a small alpha helix highly exposed to the solvent.

  The superimposition of the predicted AF2 model with the experimental structure of resting NOX2 (8GZ3) is shown in Figs. 2A and S2. The major differences between the AF2 model and the experimental (8GZ3) structure lie in the DH domain of NOX2 (unsolved in the 7U8G experimental structure due to its flexibility). The DH domain of NOX2 is homologous to the protein ferredoxin-NADP+ reductase and is made up of two distinct structured domains, the FAD-binding domain (residues 295–390) and the larger NADP-binding domain (residues 403–590). Both domains are connected by a linker (residues 390–395), acting as a flexible hinge. Between both models (AF2 and 8GZ3), the DH domains have a significant deviation with regard to the membrane. The DH domain of the AF2 model lies closer to the TM domain than in the experimental resting form and is pushed up towards the membrane by the above-mentioned hairpin N-terminus regions. This leads to a more compact form of AF2-cytb558 (Fig. 2B). We used DynDom (49) to calculate the deviation of the angle between the DH domain and the membrane between the resting (8GZ3) NOX2 and AF2 NOX2 models which was found to be 17.6°. Similar calculations between the orientation of the DH of the resting NOX2 (8GZ3) and of active DUOX1 in the presence of calcium (27) (PDB code: 7D3F) give an angle deviation of 49.4°. Such deviation of the DH domain suggests that AF2 generated a model that does not correspond to cytb558 in its resting state. Interestingly, the DH domain of the AF2 model closer to the membrane was found to be in a similar position to that observed in the active form of the experimental structure (8WEJ) (41), suggesting that the AF2 modeled an active configuration of the cytb558.

  The flavin and NADPH have been inserted and optimized into the AF2-cytb558 model by using Alphafill (Fig. 3A). When both structural models are superimposed based on the FAD-binding domain, a deviation of the DH domain is observed in the experimental resting (8GZ3) NOX2 compared to the AF2 model (Fig. 3B). Since no comparison was possible with the experimental structure of the resting cytb558 due to the absence of NADPH, we focus on the distance between Cα of Thr341 belonging to the FAD-binding site and of Cys537 part of the NADP-binding pocket (Fig. 3C). In the experimental structure, this distance is increased by about 3 Å. In our AF2 model, the edge-to-edge distance between flavin N5 and NADPH is estimated of about 7.1 Å (Fig. 3D). We compared this distance with the experimental structures of the NOX2 homologs, csNOX and DUOX1 (25, 26). We estimated this distance by considering the C-terminus Trp695 proposed to mimic the NADPH nicotinamide ring. The distance between Trp695 and flavin N5 in the DH domain of csNOX was found to be about 4 Å. In the experimental 3D structure of DUOX1, this distance was found to be about 10 Å, too far for hydride transfer. Thus, the distance between FAD and NADPH in AF2-cytb558 is intermediate between csNOX and DUOX1 but is still slightly too far to expect efficient electron transfers between NADPH and FAD. This suggests that the two pockets should move closer upon activation. Finally, in the predicted AF2-cytb558, the C-terminal arm of NOX2 shields the isoalloxazine ring from the bulk solvent and brings the aromatic side chain of Phe570, at the potential nicotinamide ring–binding site, facing the pyrazine ring of FAD (parallel π-stacking) at a distance of 3.9 Å, compatible with van der Waals contacts (Fig. 3D). The C-terminal Phe570 is unresolved in the experimental resting NOX2 structure. #### AF2-generated model of cytb558 with p47phox
  p47phox plays a central role in conducting the initial assembly and activation steps of the NADPH oxidase via regions involved in protein–protein interactions (such as its SH3 domains), protein–lipids interactions (such as its PX domain), and unstructured regions. The structure of the full-length p47phox alone or in interaction with its other oxidase partners is still unsolved. AF2 was used to predict models of the full-length p47phox in interaction with the cytb558. Five different models of the multimer were generated, featuring again a structurally stable transmembrane portion for NOX2 and p22phox (as illustrated by the pLDDT scores). In the five cytb558-p47phox models, while the domain positions of p47phox vary (Fig. S3), the cytb558 structure is similar to the above-described AF2-cytb558 model. Thus, introducing p47phox in the predictive process of AF2 modeling of the full-length p47phox with the cytb558 did not modify the overall conformation of the cytb558 which displays the same DH domain rearrangements. The selection of the most appropriate model from the five models generated by AF2 was based on the two consensual criteria described in the literature for p47phox and cytb558 interacting regions, that is, (i) the presence of an interaction of the PRR region of p22phox with the SH3 of p47phox, as well as (ii) the PX domain of p47phox oriented towards the membrane (Table 1). The models lacking these two well-known interactions were not further considered. However, we cannot exclude that they could represent transitional/primarily interacting forms. Thus, the model, that best fits the current state of knowledge regarding the activation way of cytb558 by p47phox, is the rank2 model (Figs. 4 and S3). Notably, it displays interactions between the proline-rich region (residues 151–160) in the cytosolic C terminus of p22phox and the SH3 domains of p47phox (Fig. 5A). Spatial positions of cytb558 with respect to the lipid bilayer were optimized by the PPM 2.0 method that accounts for the hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions (described by the dielectric constant and hydrogen-bonding profiles) of the proteins with the anisotropic water-lipid environment described by the dielectric constant and hydrogen-bond profiles.   In the literature, four putative p47phox-binding sites have been proposed in NOX2 (50). The 491 to 500 fragment (“HFAVHHDEEKD” residues), located in the DH domain of NOX2, has been suggested to be involved in the oxidase assembly, supported by impaired p47phox translocation in CGD point mutation (Asp500) of p47phox (31). This fragment, with several negatively charged residues (Asp496, Glu497, Glu498, Asp500), is part of the NIS (NADPH-domain Insertion Sequence; 484–504 residues) described as a flexible region that influences the oxidase activity (51, 52). In our AF2 model, this fragment is shifted, by about 10 Å compared to the experimental (8GZ3) structure, close to the C-terminal of p47phox (Fig. 5B). Thus, this 491 to 500 fragment of NOX2 lies at distances compatible with salt bridges with p47phox, that is, between Glu498 from NOX2 and Arg377 from p47phox. This proximity may explain the role of this sequence during NADPH oxidase assembly for the binding of p47phox (31). Also, the 451 to 458 fragment was demonstrated to be important for the complex assembly (30). However, these authors noticed this fragment is not crucial for its activity. In our model, the 451 to 458 fragment belongs to the DH domain of NOX2 and is located close to the PRR region of p47phox (∼7 Å away), which may suggest a possible interaction with NOX2 (Fig. 5C). One can note that our filtering and selection processes based on the two above-mentioned specific interactions (i.e. the interaction of PRR of p22phox with the p47phox SH3 tandems and the membrane orientation of p47phox PX) lead to a reliable model compatible with other experimental results.

  Two other regions of NOX2 in interaction with p47phox have been suggested experimentally to be important in oxidase activation but are not identified in our AF2 model. The 86 to 94 fragment, located in the B loop of the NOX2 protein, was proposed to interact with p47phox through electrostatic interactions (30). However, in our AF2 cytb558-p47phox (Fig. S4B), this NOX2 loop is part of the hairpin sandwiched between the membrane and the DH domain, similarly as observed in the AF2 model of the cytb558 (Fig. 1C) and is far from p47phox. Our result does not support the hypothesis of an interaction between this NOX2 fragment with p47phox, but rather that this NOX2 region may be involved in the stability of the cytb558 and the DH domain reorganization occurring during its activation. Lastly, the C-terminus of NOX2 was identified to interact with p47phox (53). More specifically, the 559 to 565 fragment at the terminal region of NOX2 has been proposed to interact with p47phox (30). Unfortunately, this fragment is found in the AF2 model distant from p47phox (Fig. S4B). Further studies in the light of current knowledge and techniques are needed to clarify the role of these NOX2 fragments in p47phox binding or the DH domain structural changes.

  Other important regions proposed in the literature need to be reconsidered from the perspective of our model. The 149 to 154 region (in particular Ile152 and Thr153) of p47phox was proposed to be important for the phagocyte NADPH oxidase function but after oxidase assembly (54). The Ala substitution of Ile152 results in the loss of the ability to activate the oxidase but without affecting its interaction with p22phox or with p67phox. In our AF2 cytb558-p47phox model (also in our AF2 cytb558-Trimera model in Fig. 9A), this region of p47phox was not found in close interaction with the cytb558, the closest NOX2 residue being located 20 Å away (Phe376). Considering the AF2 model, this region may act as a linker that allows the extension of the PX domain of p47phox to reach the membrane (Fig. S4A). Therefore, this flexible region may play an important role in the stability of the PX domain versus the SH3 region of p47phox and the consolidation of the complex. In the active experimental 8WEJ structure, this region (149–158) is the sole part of p47phox resolved by cryo-EM. It was found in close interaction with Rac1, but we cannot exclude that this specific position may be correlated to the use of a chimeric construct where p47phox is fused to Rac1.

  Finally, in the AF2 model, the PX domain is not anchored in the membrane via the Ile65 residue as expected (35) but via a hydrophobic fragment (Trp80-Phe81) and a positively charged residue (Lys79) (Figs. 5D and S5). Although the Ile65 residue is part of the (55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80) disordered region that could easily approach the membrane, it is too far to interact with the lipid bilayer. The anchoring of the Trp80-Phe81 fragment may contribute to bringing closer to the membrane the critical residues that interact with the canonical phosphoinositide-binding pocket that includes Arg43 and Arg90 (33, 36, 81), and the noncanonical (secondary) phosphoinositide-binding pocket formed by His51, Lys52, Lys55, Arg70 (33, 82). In addition, in our model, p47phox appears to be stabilized, facing the lipid bilayer and close to the DH domain of NOX2, by a putative methionine-aromatic interaction between F58-M57 of p47phox and W380 of NOX2. It would be interesting to investigate the role of these residues in the complex assembly and membrane stability by site-directed mutagenesis approaches.


#### Molecular dynamic simulations of the membrane-embedded cytb558-p47phox complex
  To take a closer look at the dynamics of the structure and explore lipid–protein interactions, thus improving our understanding of the complex behavior within the membrane environment, we carried out molecular dynamic (MD) simulations.

  The protein–lipid interaction analyses of the cytb558-p47phox complex in membrane MD simulations were conducted for 2 × 500 ns replicas of both unphosphorylated and phosphorylated p47phox. In the phosphorylated runs, lipid-binding sites exhibited higher lipid occupancy and longer residence times compared to the unphosphorylated runs, indicating that phosphorylation enhances the anchoring of both NOX2 and p47phox to the membrane. This more stable interaction could in turn facilitate a more stable conformation, potentially supporting the activation and sustained activity of the NOX2 complex.

  Throughout the simulations, we observed several key lipid–protein interactions. In phosphorylated MD simulation replica 1, we identified PI(3,4)P2 bound to NOX2 residues Pro558 and Arg559, while POPS was bound to NOX2 residues Cys85 and Cys86 (Fig. 6A). In phosphorylated replica 2, PI(3)P interacted with p47phox residues Val19, Pro20, Ser21, Lys52, Pro78, Lys79, and Trp80 at 256 ns (Fig. 6B). Additionally, at 503 ns in the same replica, PI(3)P interacted with p22phox tail residues Asn186, Pro187, and Ile188, alongside p47phox residues Val19, Pro20, Ser21, Arg121, and Asp123 (Fig. 6C). We also observed the p22phox tail embedded in the lipid bilayer in this phosphorylated replica 2 simulations, which was an emphasized interaction mentioned in the “active” complex cryo-EM structure paper (PDB ID: 8WEJ) (41).   Throughout the simulations, we observe the anchoring of the hydrophobic Trp80-Phe81 residues and Lys79 of the p47phox-PX domain within the membrane bilayer as identified by the AF2 model of the NOX2-p22phox-p47phox complex (Fig. 5D). This anchoring is persistent in our MD simulations but with time, Ile65 gets closer to the bilayer and interacts with NOX2 through the lipid-embedded TM6 and the FAD domain (via Val296, Lys313, Lys315) in all runs. Key residues involved in canonical phosphoinositide recognition, such as those in the PXXP motif (Pro73-Pro76) and the Lys79-Arg85 region of the p47phox-PX domain, along with Phe18-Gln22 and Pro128-Lys134 of the linker connecting PX and SH3, engaged with the bilayer. However, due to the random positioning of lipids (PI(3)P, PI(3,4)P2, POPS, etc.) at the start of the simulations and the limited simulation timescale, we cannot fully quantify these specific lipid–protein interactions across all replicas. To reinforce our analysis of the MD, we therefore opted for confronting the MD simulations with cryoEM data.

  In this context, our MD simulations uncovered two important lipid interaction sites. In replica 2, we identified a highly occupied lipid-binding site at the NOX2-p22phox interface (Fig. S6A), which was emphasized in the NOX2-p22phox complex resting state cryo-EM study (PDB ID: 7U8G) (29). At this site, NOX2 residues Ala113, Ala109, Trp106, Phe100, Thr99, Arg96, and Phe570 interacted with various lipid types, including POPC, PLPC, DOPE, and POPS. The LipIDens and PyLipID analyses revealed significant lipid residence times, with strong R2 values indicating reliable fits for the residence times across the trajectory.

  Additionally, a distinct lipid interaction involving the conserved Phe100 residue on the NOX2 TM3 helix was observed (Fig. S6B). This interaction featured DOPE/PLPE lipids in close contact with Phe100, corresponding to a distinct density feature in the NOX2-p22phox complex resting state cryo-EM map (PDB ID: 7U8G). The high occupancy rate and residence time for this site throughout the trajectory suggest that Phe100 contributes to the stability of the NADPH-binding pocket, possibly by forming part of a lipid coordination site. This role of Phe100 is conserved across all NOX proteins, further emphasizing its importance in the complex's structural integrity (29).

  The observed persistent interactions, particularly between the p22phox PRR and the SH3 domains of p47phox, as well as the interaction of p47phox C-terminal with the 491 to 500 loop of NOX2, in phosphorylated runs, provide support to the structural models and highlight the importance of these regions in maintaining the structural integrity and activation of the NADPH oxidase complex. We do not observe a stable interaction between the p47phox C-terminal and the 451 to 458 loop of NOX2, but we see the interactions of the phosphorylation sites Ser359 and Ser 370, which neighbors p47phox PRR, with the residues from 491 to 500 loop of NOX2 in the phosphorylated state runs. Also, like in the starting AF2 model, we observe NOX2 Cys86-Leu94 and NOX2 Arg559-Phe565 regions staying far away from p22phox and p47phox interfaces and staying very close to the bilayer mostly involving in the lipid-binding sites throughout the simulations (Fig. 6A).

  These interactions, while concurring with the AF2-generated structural predictions, also highlight the dynamic nature of the NOX2-p22phox-p47phox complex and its regulation by phosphorylation states. Our findings emphasize the importance of considering both static models and dynamic simulations to fully understand the molecular mechanisms governing the assembly and activity of the NADPH oxidase complex.


#### AF2-generated model of cytb558 with p47phox, p67phox, and Rac1
  We attempted to predict the assembly of the full-length p47phox, p67phox, and Rac proteins with cytb558 but unfortunately, this was unsuccessful. The resulting models proved unsatisfactory as the proteins were in the "spaghetti" forms, with low levels of pLDDT scores. This is likely due to the many flexible and unstructured regions present in these proteins. Therefore, to bypass the number of flexible regions and, thus, facilitate the AF2 modeling, we used a chimeric protein, the so-called Trimera, fusing the protein of Rac1 and the essential functional domains of p47phox and p67phox (Figs. 7A and S7). The Trimera is extensively used for in vitro experiments to reconstitute the active complex (28, 46, 83). It requires only the addition of an activator, the arachidonic acid (AA).   To date, no structural data are available for the Trimera. We used AF2 to predict its structure in solution. Despite a globally consistent superposition with preserved individually structured domains, the five AF2-generated models differ by the relative orientation of the subunits depending on the positioning of the linkers and the unstructured regions (Fig. S7). The selection of the optimal model was based on criteria that ensure structural compatibility with already known structural features: (i) same orientation of the PB domain of Rac1 and the PX domain of p47phox for membrane interaction; (ii) accessibility of the SH3 domains of p47phox and the activation domain (AD) region of the p67phox protein for interaction with the membrane proteins. A theoretical CD spectrum of the Trimera AF2 model was calculated using SESCA software and was compared to the spectra of the purified protein in the absence (inactive form) and the presence (active form) of AA obtained on the synchrotron radiation CD facility. Surprisingly, as observed in Fig. 7B, the theoretical and experimental CD spectra obtained in the presence of AA display significant similarities, suggesting that the conformation of the selected model of Trimera generated by AF2 adopts an active conformation.

The five models generated by AF2 are similar and superimposable, with acceptable pLDDT scores. This interacting region containing the 369Cys-Gly-Cys371 triad was described as essential for p67phox-binding stabilization on NOX2 (57); (iii) the PX domain of p47phox positioned on the membrane side. ## Discussion
  Despite extensive knowledge about the biological function of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase complex, its structural features that regulate activation and activity remain less understood due to the challenges in capturing them for study. The role of the various subunits in NADPH oxidase activation depends on a series of intricate protein–protein interactions, particularly between p47phox and p22phox, p67phox and NOX2, as well as Rac1 and NOX2 (reviewed in (2, 58, 59)). Additionally, lipid–protein interactions are crucial, especially those involving Rac1 and p47phox with the membrane (36, 39). All these interactions need to be synchronized for optimal enzyme activity. The p47phox protein plays a central role in this synchronization, yet its structural details remain elusive due to its highly flexible and unstructured regions. By integrating computational methods such as AF2 and MD simulation and experimental data, we have meticulously constructed macromolecular models, concentrating on the p47phox interactions to approach the structure of the active complex. It is important to note that the AF2 algorithm still faces challenges when modeling proteins, with high flexibility and numerous unstructured regions, such as p47phox, p67phox, and the C-terminal region of p22phox. In these cases, literature data and existing structural information, even if partial, are essential for selecting the most accurate model from those proposed by the software and ensuring the final model's quality and reliability.


### Rearrangement and compaction of the DH domain of NOX2 in the cytb558
  A distinctive feature of phagocyte NADPH oxidase is its dependence on cytosolic protein interactions for activity. The mechanisms of activation of the catalytic unit, cytb558, by the cytosolic proteins, remain a topic of ongoing debate with many aspects still unresolved. One of them concerns electron transfers, which begin at the level of the DH domain. Whatever the absence or presence of the associated subunits (with p47phox or Trimera) in our AF2 models, the DH domain always adopts the same conformation: (i) the entire DH domain is close to the membrane, stabilized by electrostatic interactions between amino acid of the DH (390–400) and the hairpin helices (Cys72-Arg96) localized at the N-terminal end of NOX2; (ii) the NADPH-binding domain is closer to the FAD-binding domain than in the experimental resting structure; (iii) the flexible C-terminus of NOX2 brings the aromatic side chain of Phe570, highly conserved residue in the NADPH oxidase family, at van der Waals distance of the isoalloxazine ring of FAD.

  The position and the compaction of NOX2 are close to what is observed in the active SpNOX structure (60) and the recent experimental structure of the active form of cytb558 (41). These rearrangements of the DH domain correspond to (at least) two structural transition steps necessary for the cytb558 activation, aiming to facilitate the electron transfer pathway from NADPH to FAD and from FAD to Heme1. Interestingly, AF2 generated a cytb558 structure close to the active form of our proteins (interestingly, it was also the case for the Trimera generated by AF2 in its active conformation).

  Also, we pay attention to the C-terminus end of NOX2, including the Phe570 residue. It is known that a conserved aromatic residue at or near the C-terminus of the DH domain is present among members of the ferredoxin-NADP reductase–like proteins. In these different flavoenzymes, we and others proposed that associated with the rearrangement of the NADPH-binding domain, the flexibility of the C-terminus region may participate in controlling the NAD(P)H binding and activities (61, 62, 63). Similarly, in the phagocyte NADPH oxidase, the C-terminal part of NOX2, through the interaction of the C-terminal Phe570 with the isoalloxazine ring, could contribute to the modulation of substrate binding and/or a control of the electron transfer. This is supported by similar hypotheses described for the prokaryotic NADPH oxidase SpNOX (64). Interestingly, in the recent cryo-EM structure of the active state of NADPH oxidase, Phe570 also faces FAD and is sandwiched between FAD and NADPH, as in our AF2-cytb558 model (41). Among the structurally related NOX homologs (NOX1-5), only NOX4, the sole constitutively active NOX enzyme, has a serine (after Phe570), as the C-terminal residue, while the others, which all require an activation step, end with Phe570. However, the role of Phe570 and the C-terminus part of NOX2 in the NADPH oxidase complex activity may appear controversial, since observations in one study using a cellular approach, the Phe570 mutation does not significantly impact the oxidase activity (65). The constraints in the cellular approach may mask the effects on the electron transfer or substrate affinity. More extended studies of the C-ter part of NOX2 are needed to solve this question at a molecular level using transient absorption approaches coupled with site-directed mutagenesis as it was performed for the other flavoproteins.


### The membrane-embedded p47phox-cytb558 model

#### The hanging of the p47phox protein on the cytochrome b558
  Our model (Fig. 4) highlights the very high flexibility of p47phox. Besides its interaction through its SH3 domains with the PRR region of p22phox (including Pro152, Pro156, and Arg158 as described in (66, 85), p47phox extends up to interact via its C-terminal end with the 491–500 loop and with the 451–458 region of NOX2 and harpooning the membrane via its N-terminal PX domain. Based on biochemical studies, such as cell-free interactions, mainly four NOX2-p47phox interacting regions have been proposed to be important for NADPH oxidase activation. Two of them, in good agreement with our model, are the 491–500 and 451–458 fragments of NOX2 that are found to interact with the C-terminus (363–390) of p47phox in the AF2-cytb558-p47phox model and are seen partly in the simulations. These results support their role in the constitution of the complex. These interactions involving the C-terminus of p47phox could not be visualized in the AF2 cytb558/Trimera model since p47phox is truncated from residue 286 and in the active structure 8WEJ due to unsolved regions. In these structural models, the 491 to 500 region of NOX2 is at the crossroads of the tetratricopeptide repeat region of p67phox and Rac1. In the active form, the region 491 to 500 of NOX2 does not interact anymore with p47phox but with p67phox. Therefore, one can suggest that once p47phox has anchored via its C-terminus to the region 491 to 500 of NOX2 (which is solvent accessible in the cytb558), important conformational rearrangements of p47phox enable the p67phox AD to interact with NOX2. The methionine-aromatic interaction between Phe58-Met57 of p47phox and Trp380 of NOX2 observed in our models may be important to stabilize the complex formation since Met-aromatic bridges were proposed to constitute motifs for molecular recognition or redox properties (67).

  On the other hand, the 86 to 94 (hairpin structure) and 559 to 565 fragments of NOX2, which lie close to each other and are mentioned in the literature as areas of interaction with p47phox, do not appear to be interacting with p47phox in any of our AF2 models and simulations, as they are too distant from each other. This 86 to 94 fragment is lipid-facing on one side through lipid-aromatic interactions and DH-facing on the other side, through electrostatic interactions. It may participate in stabilizing the DH in its “active” state close to the membrane. Mutations in some of these amino acids, in particular the charged residues, could have a major impact on the DH domain positioning. Overall, these results offer a promising starting point for future experimental studies aimed at gaining a better understanding of the interaction of cytb558 with p47phox.


#### The interactions of the p47phox-PX domain with the membrane bilayer
  The PX domains, originally identified in the phagocyte NADPH oxidase (68), are known to target membranes through specific interactions with phosphoinositides. In the NADPH oxidase, the p47phox-PX domain has been shown to bind to a variety of phosphoinositides and lipids (69, 70). The NMR experiments revealed the existence of an 8-Å deep cavity that was proposed to be involved in interactions with negatively charged phospholipids (71), such as PI(3,4)P2 and phosphatidic acid (81). Studies based on site-directed mutations and calculations of electrostatic potentials have identified the key amino acids for the membrane interaction of the PX domain of p47phox: (i) Arg43, and Arg90, essential for PX interaction with the canonical phosphoinositide (33, 36, 81); (ii) Ile65, adjacent to canonical and secondary phosphoinositide-binding sites, involved in nonspecific penetration of neutral lipid monolayers (35). Here, we show that alternative regions of p47phox are involved in its interaction with the lipid bilayer: the region K79-R85 and the hydrophobic lipid insertion through W80-F81. Due to the dynamic nature of specific lipids in the bilayer, MD simulations did not capture stable interactions with the canonical residues; however, we consistently observed the hydrophobic W80-F81 residues of the p47phox-PX domain anchored within the lipid bilayer. The K79-R85 and W80-F81 regions in the p47phox PX-domain, through their interaction with the membrane, might be involved in the protein translocation of the cytosolic proteins to the membrane or the complex stability. The observation from a previous report (70) that point mutation of Arg42 or mutations in the PX region do not abolish the membrane association of p47phox further reinforces this idea. While their precise functional roles remain unclear, it is plausible that one event may exert a subtle allosteric role on p47phox membrane binding and possibly affect the orientation of the PX domain, while the other may enhance overall membrane avidity and contribute to the complex stability.


### Interactions within the fully active complex
  To design the assembled (“active”) model, we used the Trimera either as a single polypeptide or split into two parts, with Rac1 as a separate protein. The aim was to map the interactions involved in an active complex, with the Trimera specifically engineered to include only the regions of the cytosolic proteins, essential for the enzyme activation (46). The modeling of cytb558 interacting with the Trimera, as a single polypeptide, has shown that it is impossible to have simultaneous interactions of the AD of p67phox with NOX2 and of the PB of Rac with the membrane. These spatial constraints may explain why, experimentally, the addition of AA to the cell-free assay is still necessary to obtain the active complex (32, 44, 45). By splitting the Trimera and leaving Rac1 as a separate protein in the AF2 modeling, we achieved a significantly improved model (Fig. 8B). In this configuration, Rac1 can interact effectively with the membrane by anchoring its C-terminus into the lipid bilayer. We consider this model to be the most accurate for a structure of the complex in its active form, closely resembling the native enzyme composition. The results generated from the AF2 predictions are particularly impressive, as the positions of p67phox and Rac1 relative to the membrane and cytb558 align well with the recently published cryo-EM structure 8WEJ (Fig. 9) (41), which was not included in the AF learning database at the time of our modeling. These results suggest a potential improvement of the Trimera engineering by introducing a linker between the two proteins, p67phox, and Rac1 providing Rac1 with sufficient flexibility to reach and interact with the membrane more effectively.

  Nevertheless, we notice one difference in our active model (Fig. 8B) with the recently published cryo-EM structure 8WEJ. In the experimental structure, only the small peptide 149 to 158 of p47phox was solved and is located on the external face of Rac1 (Fig. 9, A and C). In our model, this region that links the PX to the SH3 region of p47phox is located further away, leaving potentially enough place for other proteins such as p40phox to bind to the complex. This difference between our model and the cryo-EM structure could be attributed to the different fused proteins used in both approaches. In the 8WEJ structure, p47phox is fused to Rac. This could keep artificially p47phox close to Rac, and thus to p67phox, while to the best of our knowledge, no interaction between p47phox and Rac has been described for now. However, the p47phox protein is a modular highly flexible protein that could, in the end, adopt different orientations.

  To further explore those possibilities by taking advantage of the recent access to AlphaFold3 (AF3), we prepared a model with full-length regulatory proteins that also include p40phox. The model is shown in Fig. S9A and is one of the five models proposed by AF3 and is very similar to the AF2 model except for the positioning of p47phox. On p22phox, the fragments Gly177-Gly182 and Pro156-Pro160 from C-ter interact with p47phox. On p47phox, Ile152 is close (<4 Å) from Rac1 (Ile173) and is now centrally located in the heart of the complex formed by Rac1 and p40phox N-ter. AF3 allows to include cofactors (hemes, FAD, NADPH) in the structure modeling. As shown in Fig. S9B, in this model, FAD and NADPH are closer to each other and Phe570 is not found between FAD and NADPH, but next to them. All those observations highlight that contacts between proteins are different when more partners are added. This could illustrate how the contacts evolve in the course of activation. In turn, it suggests that apparent discrepancies between intermediate models and experiments should be considered as an incentive to undertake a more exhaustive exploration of the conformational landscape accessible to this complex. An additional intriguing observation is that p67phox SH3A (233–298) is found at the level of the membrane. This is not a known role of this SH3 domain. It could either be an artifact from AF3 or an interesting prediction. Therefore, this model, although interesting, still needs experimental testing.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we provide evidence that AF2 models of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase structures, which were not part of the AF2 training set, offer accuracies comparable to those of experimental X-ray structures deposited in the PDB. Our results show that the AF2 structural predictions take into consideration the membrane and the interactions that membrane and cytosolic proteins can develop with their environment during the assembly process. The AF2 model and the MD simulation provide valuable insights into the structure of the full-length p47phox assembled with the cytb558, which remains unresolved experimentally, to the cytb558 and the membrane. While the first steps involve an assembly, additional organizational processes are likely required to consolidate the complex and achieve a fully functional enzyme. The lipid–protein interactions remain crucial for stabilizing and maintaining the enzyme activity. It is questionable whether the knowledge gained from AF2 models can be validated experimentally, particularly for those components of the complex that are difficult to solve using current techniques, but these results demonstrate the potential use of AF2 as a guidance tool for the analysis of experimental data that could contribute to the generation of hypotheses in the biological search for inhibitory molecules.


## Experimental procedures

### Materials
  Chemicals (cis-AA, PBS) came from Sigma Aldrich. Resins Ni-sepharose and SP-sepharose HP were purchased from Cytiva. cis-AA was solubilized at a final concentration of 20 mg/ml in ethanol, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.


### In vitro studies

#### Expression and purification of trimera
  The Trimera plasmid was generously donated by Prof. E. Pick (Tel Aviv University, Israel). Trimera is a chimeric protein consisting of the fusion of human N-part of p47phox (aa 1–286), N-part of p67phox (1–212), and full-length Rac1Q61L (1–192) (46). Expression and purification of trimers were carried out as previously described in (46). Purification chromatography including Ni-NTA-Sepharose-FF resins (Cytiva) was carried out using ÄKTAprime system. The purity of protein solutions was assessed by migration in 10% SDS BisTris-NuPAGE gels (Biorad), stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Protein concentrations were estimated with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer at 280 nm using the absorption coefficient calculated for the Trimera (ExPASy, ProtParam free software).


#### CD spectroscopy
  CD was used to explore the secondary structure of the proteins in solution and the structural effects of AA on proteins. To avoid any undesirable absorption due to phosphate buffer, the protein samples were dialyzed overnight in very low concentration of sodium phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.0) and 100 mM NaF to maintain the ionic strength. The dialysate buffer was retained for CD control analyses. Before data collection, samples were briefly centrifuged at 10,000 × g to remove protein aggregates.

  CD spectra using synchrotron radiation were collected on the DISCO beamline at the SOLEIL synchrotron radiation facility (72, 73). The use of synchrotron radiation enables us to extend the spectral range to lower wavelengths than would be possible with classical laboratory equipment. All spectra were calibrated for wavelengths and amplitudes with camphor sulphonic acid. Protein samples (≈3.6 mg/ml) were loaded in CaF2 cells using 3 μl volumes and optical pathlengths of 50 μm (73). When indicated, AA was added as an ethanolic solution as it is not soluble in water. An identical volume of ethanol was added in the reference, in order to subtract its CD contribution. Ethanol at such concentration did not perturb the secondary structure of the proteins (data not shown).

  Spectra were measured from 280 to 170 nm, using the HT (high tension) mid-eight as the cut-off at 175 nm. Three consecutive scans were recorded and averaged for the samples and the baseline. The mean baseline was subtracted from the mean sample spectra. The temperature was kept constant at 25 °C.

  Spectra are expressed in molar CD (delta epsilon) units, calculated using mean residue weight of Trimera (pending for 730 amino acid length). The secondary structure composition was determined using the BestSel software. The fitting range was 180 to 250 nm.


### Modelling approaches

#### In silico protein modeling
  The upgraded version of the AlphaFold 2 (AF 2.3) algorithm was used to predict the three-dimensional structures of NADPH oxidase protein complexes from their amino acid sequences (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) (74, 75). It was used online or implemented locally to increase the number of cycles. The number of cycles was set at 18, as no further improvements were observed in the models generated beyond this number.

  In silico–predicted protein CD spectra were performed using the SESCA software (76). A method for Positioning of Proteins in Membrane (PPM2.0) was used to model the cytb558 orientation in a lipid bilayer (https://opm.phar.umich.edu/ppm_server) (48). #### In silico protein domain movement analysis
  The Dyndom server (http://dyndom.cmp.uea.ac.uk/dyndom/) was used to determine the angle of deviation of protein domains from the membrane by displaying domain movements relative to an axis of rotation (49). The coordinates of the different structural models were used as input data to obtain the angle of deviation of the DH domain from the membrane.


#### In silico cofactor insertion
  AlphaFill algorithm (https://alphafill.eu/) was used to insert the cofactors into the predicted structures (77). The prediction is based on at least 25% sequence identity with the reference protein, and RMSD metrics are calculated on the basis of residues within 6 Å around the cofactor.


#### Molecular dynamics
  In this study, MD simulations of the NOX2-p22phox-p47phox complex embedded within a membrane bilayer were conducted using GROMACS version 2021.3 to explore the dynamics (78, 79). The initial structure, modeled as described in the In silico protein complex modeling section, was further processed with Phenix software (80) to minimize clashes and strains. The model was then oriented for membrane embedding using the PPM server (86).

  Glycans were added to the NOX2 apoprotein, at residues N132, N149, and N240, so was a disulfide bond between C244 and C257, and heme coordination centers at the outer heme/Heme2 (H115-H222) and inner heme/Heme1 (H101-H209). The membrane system, assembled using CHARMM-GUI (49, 87, 88), mimicked the plasma membrane with a lipid composition of POPC, PLPC, DOPC, POPE, PLPE, DOPE, POPS, cholesterol, PI(3)P, and PI(3,4)P2 (Table S1). Simulations were performed in an explicit TIP3P water model (89), within a hexagonal periodic box (Table S2). The systems underwent energy minimization and equilibration steps, in the NVT and NPT ensembles, thus ensuring stabilization before the production phase. We conducted 2 μs simulations of the NOX2-p22phox-p47phox complex, comprising two 500 ns replicas with unphosphorylated p47phox and two 500 ns replicas with phosphorylated p47phox, with phosphorylations at residues S303, S304, S328, S359, S370, and S379. The simulations were performed using the CHARMM36m force field, recognized for its accuracy in modeling both ordered and disordered fragments of proteins and protein–lipid interactions (90), with a time step of 0.002 ps. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method (91), with van der Waals interactions managed using a cut-off and force-switch modifier at 1.2 and 1.0 nm, respectively.

  Temperature and pressure were regulated using the Nose-Hoover thermostat and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat, set at 310.15 K and 1.0 bar. Hydrogen bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm. The model included hemes and FAD cofactors, with FAD parameters sourced from (92). The placement of the hemes was taken from the cryo-EM structure 7U8G (29) and FAD was modeled based on the DUOX1-DUOXA1 cryo-EM structure 7D3E (27). The DH domain of the model was adjusted to match the orientation of the DH domain in 7D3E and to refine the distance between the inner heme and FAD, providing a physically relevant starting point for MD simulations that would further refine the structure.


#### Lipid–protein interactions analysis
  Protein–lipid interactions were characterized using PyLipID (93) with a dual cut-off scheme of 0.35/0.50 nm applied for all proteins. All other input parameters in PyLipID were kept at default settings. LipIDens (94) was then used to rank the lipid-binding sites generated by PyLipID based on occupancy and residence time. This ranking allowed us to identify key binding sites with extended residence times and high lipid occupancy. The R2 values for the predicted residence times, as presented in the plots, were used to assess the quality and reliability of the PyLipID outputs. Using the LipIDens tool allowed us to extract lipid residence times, binding site occupancy, and duration of interactions from our simulations, enabling a comparison of lipid interactions between the phosphorylated and unphosphorylated states.
