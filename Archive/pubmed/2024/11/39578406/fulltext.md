# Formation of multicompartment structures through aging of protein-RNA condensates


## Abstract
  Cells can dynamically organize reactions through the formation of biomolecular condensates. These viscoelastic networks exhibit complex material properties and mesoscale architectures, including the ability to form multiphase assemblies. It was shown previously that condensates with complex architectures may arise at equilibrium in multicomponent systems or in condensates that were driven out of equilibrium by changes in external parameters such as temperature. In this study, we demonstrate that the aging of initially homogeneous protein-RNA condensates can spontaneously lead to the formation of kinetically arrested double-emulsion and core-shell structures without changes in external variables such as temperature or solution conditions. By combining time-resolved fluorescence-based experimental techniques with simulations based on the Cahn-Hilliard theory, we show that, as the protein-RNA condensates age, the decrease of the relative strength of protein-RNA interactions induces the release of RNA molecules from the dense phase. In condensates exceeding a critical size, aging combined with slow diffusion of the macromolecules trigger nucleation of dilute phase inside the condensates, which leads to the formation of double-emulsion structures. These findings illustrate a new mechanism of formation of multicompartment condensates.


## Significance
  Biomolecular condensates formed by phase separation of proteins and nucleic acids play an important role in cellular organization. These condensates exhibit complex material properties and architectures, including multiphase condensates. Mechanisms regulating spatial organization of molecules in the absence of physical barriers remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate that spatial organization can be modulated by aging of condensates, i.e., change of interactions and material properties over time. Specifically, we show that the net strength of protein-RNA interactions decreases over time in condensates formed by RNA and a helicase, leading to RNA release and formation of multiphase condensates. These findings show a new mechanism to form multiphase condensates.


## Introduction
  Increasing evidence suggests that cells can orchestrate reactions in space and time by forming membraneless organelles known as biomolecular condensates (1,2,3,4). Biomolecular condensates in cells are often multilayered, consisting of subcompartments with different composition and material properties (5,6,7). A well-studied example of a multilayered biocondensate is the nucleolus. Its complex architecture arises from its two main protein components, nucleophosmin (NPM1) and fibrillarin (FIB1), phase separating into mutually immiscible liquid-like phases where differences in the interfacial tensions of the different phases give rise to the core-shell architecture (8). Another example of biocondensates with a complex architecture are P-granules. In vitro, one of the two main protein components of P-granules, PGL-3, forms liquid-like droplets, which are stabilized by gel-like condensates of the second main protein component, MEG-3, adsorbing to their surface (9). The formation of multilayered condensates was also observed in multicomponent systems of elastin-like polypeptides where tuning the amino acid sequence of the peptides affected the mutual miscibility of the liquid-like phases formed upon phase separation and thus the architecture of the condensates (10). It was also shown that condensates with complex architectures form in the systems of RNA-binding proteins and RNA depending on their ratio, which is ascribed to the reentrant phase behavior (11,12,13,14). Thus, condensates with complex architectures may arise at equilibrium in multicomponent systems depending on the relative interaction strengths between the different components and/or their stoichiometry.

  It has also been shown that formation of condensates with a complex architecture may result from kinetic rather than purely thermodynamic driving forces. Erkamp et al. (15) demonstrated that double-emulsion condensates form in a poly(A)-PEG system due to a rapid change in temperature, resulting in a change in the equilibrium concentrations of the dense and dilute phases. Slow diffusion of macromolecules combined with long diffusion paths within the condensates led to nucleation of the dilute phase within the dense phase (15). This nucleation allows the system to achieve the new equilibrium concentrations more rapidly than relying solely on macromolecular diffusion. Thus, complex architectures can be formed in out of equilibrium condensates (15).

  In this study, we demonstrate that the aging of initially homogeneous protein-RNA condensates can spontaneously lead to the formation of kinetically arrested double-emulsion and core-shell structures without changes in external variables such as temperature or solution conditions. During aging, intermolecular forces among the components of the condensates exhibit gradual nonconservative changes, which may be linked to conformational transitions of the macromolecules. This results in changes of the material properties of the condensates from more liquid-like to more arrested, which reflects the slowed-down macromolecular diffusion due to establishing longer-lived physical contacts between the components. The molecular mechanisms behind aging of biocondensates and their physiological and pathological significance are a field of active studies (14,16,17,18,19,20,21).

  Here, we analyze aging of heterotypic condensates composed of the DEAD-box protein Dhh1 (22) and an RNA model, poly(U). We find that initially homogeneous Dhh1-poly(U) condensates transition into double-emulsion structures upon aging, with subsequent fusion of the entrapped droplets leading to formation of kinetically arrested core-shell condensates. We use a combination of fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS), fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM), and theoretical modeling based on the Cahn-Hilliard theory to show that our observations are consistent with the decrease of the relative strength of protein-RNA interactions in the condensates, leading to a decrease of the equilibrium poly(U) concentration in the dense phase and therefore to the release of poly(U) molecules into the dilute phase. We observe a size-dependent morphology of the aged condensates, where only condensates larger than a threshold size transition into double-emulsion/core-shell structures upon aging, while smaller condensates remain homogeneous.

  Our findings provide an example of formation of multicompartment condensates under kinetic control, as recently observed for heterotypic condensates formed by RNA molecules and the RNA-binding protein Whi3 (23).


## Materials and methods

### Protein purification
  Recombinant Dhh1 with an N-terminal 6xHis tag and C-terminal mCherry tag were expressed in E. coli BL21 DE3 cells as described previously (24). The protein was first purified by affinity chromatography using a Ni2+-charged Fast Flow Chelating Sepharose matrix (Cytiva Sweden AB, Uppsala, Sweden) and by size exclusion with a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva Sweden AB). The elution buffer at pH 7.5 contained 300 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, 2 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10% glycerol. Selected fractions from size-exclusion chromatography were concentrated to ∼400 μM using Amicon 10,000 MWCO spin filters and flash-frozen in liquid N2 in 5–10 μL aliquots.


### Poly(U)
  Lyophilized polyuridilic acid (poly(U)) containing 20 and 50 nucleotides (polyU-20 and polyU-50) and polyU-20 labeled at the 3′ end with carboxyfluorescein (FAM) were purchased from Microsynth AG (Baglach, Switzerland). High-molecular-weight poly(U) (average degree of polymerization of 2860) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland).


### Sample preparation and imaging
  Condensate formation was induced by dissolving the protein stock solution in a low-salt buffer (18 mM HEPES, 90 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgCl2) in the presence or absence of 0.1 mg/mL poly(U). All samples were prepared and analyzed in glass bottom 384-well plates (Azenta Life Sciences, Burlington, VT, USA) at a final volume of 20 μL. To prevent evaporation during long-term incubation, the wells adjacent to the ones containing the samples were filled with 150 mM NaCl solution and the plate was closed with aluminum tape. Samples were imaged by wide-field microscopy performed on an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti-E; MicroManager software, version 2.0-gamma) equipped with a Plan Apo 60× 1.4 NA oil immersion objective (Nikon Europe B.V., Amstelveen, the Netherlands), an LED light source (Omicron LedHUB Light engine; Omicron Software, version 3.9.28) and an Andor Zyla sCMOS camera (Andor Technology, Belfast, Northern Ireland).


### Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching
  Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments were performed on a Nikon Spinning Disk SoRa confocal microscope equipped with sCMOS Hamamatsu (Hamamatsu Photonics Europe GmbH, Ammersee, Germany) Orca Fusion BT camera. A 100× 1.45 CFI Plan Apo oil immersion objective was used. Samples were bleached and imaged using a 561 nm laser. For each experiment, a region of interest within a protein condensate with a size of ∼10% of its diameter was chosen. For each measurement a region of interest of the same size was placed in another condensate for background correction. Fluorescence recovery was measured over 120 s post bleaching. Data were analyzed using Nikon NIS Elements software. ### FCS
  FCS experiments were performed on an inverted confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8 STED, Leica Application Suite X [LAS X] software, version 1.0) equipped with an HC PL APO CS2 63× 1.2 NA water immersion objective with a software-controlled correction collar (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and a hybrid detector for single-molecule detection (HyD SMD). The confocal volume was calibrated using Atto 488 NHS Ester (Dcoeff = 400 μm2/s), which yielded an effective volume, Veff, of 0.6 ± 0.1 fL and a focal volume height/width ratio, κ = 9. The samples were excited with a 488 nm laser (from a white Light Laser at 80 MHz) and the fluorescence emission collected between 500 and 530 nm.


### FLIM
  FLIM experiments were performed on an inverted confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8 STED, Leica Application Suite X [LAS X] software, version 1.0) equipped with an HC PL APO CS2 63× 1.2 NA water immersion objective with a software-controlled correction collar (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and a hybrid detector for single-molecule detection (HyD SMD). FLIM images of the condensates with poly(U)-FAM were acquired until 1000 photons per pixel were collected in the brightest channel. The samples were excited with a 488 nm laser (from a White Light Laser at 20 MHz) and the fluorescence emission collected between 500 and 530 nm. Data analysis was performed using SymPhoTime 64 2.1 software (PicoQuant, Berlin, Germany). Images were fitted pixel by pixel with a one-component reconvolution model. The images and data shown in the text display the intensity and the intensity-weighted average fluorescence lifetime.

We considered the total concentrations of 20 μM Dhh1-mCh and 0.1 mg/mL poly(U) used in experiments, and protein and poly(U) densities of 1.35 g/mL (26) and 1.5 g/mL (27), respectively. Np, Nu, and Ns in the Flory-Huggins free energy model (Eq. 7) were set equal to the number of residues (protein, 748; poly(U), 20/50; solvent, 1).

  The values of the binary interaction coefficients were set to χpu = −2, χps = 0.7, and χus = −0.7 to simulate phase separation in a range of poly(U) and protein concentrations consistent with experimental data. The change in the strength of protein-protein interactions during aging process was modeled by a linear increase of the value of χpu from −2 to −1.7 and of χps from 0.7 to 1, over 48 h.

  In addition to the thermodynamic parameters that determine the chemical potential, the Cahn-Hilliard model (Eq. 5) requires the definition of the mobility M(ϕ). ## Results and discussion

### Condensates of Dhh1-mCh with and without poly(U) age over time
  We first employed fluorescence microscopy to investigate the morphological changes of condensates composed of 20 μM mCherry-tagged Dhh1 (Dhh1-mCh) on the timescale of several days. Phase separation was induced by diluting the stock solution at high salt (200 mM NaCl) in 18 mM HEPES-KOH, 90 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgCl2 buffer (pH 6.0). Samples were incubated for 1 h before imaging. Fig. 1 A shows fluorescence images of Dhh1-mCh condensates collected at different time points after inducing phase separation. Initially, all condensates showed spherical morphology and relatively rapid fusion events (within seconds) indicative of their liquid-like character. After 24 h, the condensates developed nonspherical morphologies, indicative of arrested coalescence, with no further changes observed after 48 h. These morphological changes point to the kinetic competition between aging of the condensates, likely due to establishing longer-lived protein-protein interactions, and condensate growth due to coalescence (30). The transition toward stronger protein-protein interactions is expected to slow down protein diffusivity within the condensate and prevent it from relaxing into the thermodynamically favored spherical shape. We attempted to measure changes in the diffusivity of Dhh1-mCh within the condensates using FCS, but the diffusivity was too low to produce an autocorrelation curve. Changes of the material properties of the Dhh1-mCh condensates over time were confirmed using FRAP, which showed a 50% decrease in recovery after 48 h compared with freshly prepared condensates (imaged within 2 h after preparation) (Fig. 1 B). This aging of homotypic Dhh1-mCh condensates indicates an increase of protein-protein interaction strength over time.

  Next, we followed the aging of Dhh1-mCh condensates formed in the presence of 0.1 mg/mL poly(U) with a degree of polymerization of 20 (polyU-20). As in the case of homotypic Dhh1-mCh condensates, fresh heterotypic Dhh1-mCh-polyU-20 condensates display spherical morphology and fuse rapidly (within seconds). Upon incubation, we observed size-dependent morphology changes (Figs. S1 and S2). The largest condensates (diameter above 25 μm) developed a double-emulsion structure within 24 h. Within 48 h, the entrapped droplets underwent coalescence, which resulted in the formation of a core-shell structure (Fig. 1 C). In contrast, smaller condensates remained spherical and did not show any signs of double emulsion formation. Similarly to homotypic Dhh1-mCh condensates, FRAP experiments showed a 50% decrease in protein fluorescence recovery in condensates imaged 48 h after preparation as opposed to condensates imaged 2 h after preparation (Fig. S3).

  In addition, we followed the morphological changes of the Dhh1-mCh condensates with longer RNA mimics, polyU-50 (Figs. 1 C and S4) and polyU-3000 (Fig. 1 D). In the case of condensates with polyU-50, we observed similar morphological changes as in the case of polyU-20 but on a longer timescale: the core-shell morphologies were observed after 72 h as compared with 48 h for condensates with polyU-20. For the Dhh1-mCh condensates with polyU-3000, no morphological changes were observed for up to 8 days.


### Poly(U) migrates from the dense to the dilute phase as Dhh1-mCh condensates age
  To get insights about the mechanism of the formation of the double-emulsion condensates in the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) samples, we investigated how condensate aging affects the partitioning of poly(U) in the dilute and dense phases. First, we measured poly(U) concentration in the dilute phase by FCS. We prepared condensates of 20 μM Dhh1-mCh and 0.1 mg/mL polyU-20 (corresponding to 15 μM) including 1% polyU-20 labeled at the 3′ end with carboxyfluorescein (polyU-FAM). We acquired FCS data in the dilute phase of fresh samples (t = 2 h) and after 48 h (Fig. 2 A). The decay and amplitude of the FCS autocorrelation curve contain information regarding the diffusion coefficient and the concentration of fluorescently labeled molecules, respectively (31). The autocorrelation curves at both time points were fitted using a model considering the presence of a single diffusing component, yielding a Dcoeff= 147 ± 4 μm2/s (mean ± standard deviation, n = 4), which is equal to the value measured in a homogeneous dilute solution of monomeric polyU-FAM (Dcoeff= 151 ± 5 μm2/s, n = 4, Fig. S5). Based on the amplitude of the autocorrelation curve, the total poly(U) concentration in the dilute phase of fresh samples was calculated to be equal to 2.1 ± 0.2 μM (mean ± SD, n = 4). The decrease in the amplitude of the autocorrelation curve over time indicates an increase in the poly(U) concentration in the dilute phase, which after 48 h was 3.6 ± 0.4 μM (mean ± SD, n = 4). Importantly, in the aged samples, polyU-FAM concentration was measured to be the same in the dilute phase and in the protein-poor core of the double-emulsion condensates.Figure 2Migration of poly(U) from the dense phase into the dilute phase upon Dhh1-mCh condensate aging.   PolyU-FAM concentration within the condensates could not be measured by FCS due to too low diffusivity. However, the increase in the polyU-FAM concentration in the dilute phase shows that, as aging proceeds, poly(U) migrates from the dense into the dilute phase.

  To further characterize the release of poly(U) from the condensates, we performed FLIM on the same samples previously analyzed by FCS by monitoring the fluorescence lifetime of carboxyfluorescein conjugated to polyU-20 (Fig. 2, B and C). Fresh Dhh1-mCh-pU20 condensates showed FAM lifetime of 2.3 ± 0.1 ns (Fig. 2 B). This value was homogeneous within and in between different condensates. Due to the low poly(U) concentration in the dilute phase, the fluorescence intensity was below the threshold chosen for the analysis. Therefore, the lifetime histogram reconstructed for fresh condensates is dominated by a single peak centered at ∼2 ns (Fig. 2 C), corresponding to the polyU-FAM within the condensates. After 48 h, the FLIM image showed detectable fluorescence signal of polyU-FAM both in the dilute phase outside the condensate and within the protein-poor core of the condensate (Fig. 2 D). This signal was characterized by a longer fluorescence lifetime, and therefore the lifetime histogram for the aged samples shows two peaks: one centered around 2.3 ns, corresponding to polyU-FAM inside the condensates, and the second centered around 3.6 ns, corresponding to the poly(U) in the dilute phase and in the protein-poor core of the double-emulsion condensate (Fig. 2 E). This second peak is consistent with the fluorescence lifetime of monomeric polyU-FAM measured at low concentration in the same buffer used in our experiments (3.65 ± 0.02 ns) (Fig. S6). The observed decrease in the fluorescence lifetime of polyU-FAM upon incorporation in the dense phase may be due to many factors, including changes in the refractive index, viscosity, or pH between the two phases as well as possible FRET between FAM and mCherry (see detailed discussion in the supporting material). Regardless of the exact mechanism, our FLIM data corroborate the FCS results and confirm that, as the condensates age, poly(U) migrates from the dense phase into the dilute phase and that the poly(U) concentration and interaction properties in the core of the core-shell condensates are the same as in the dilute phase outside of the condensates.

  Overall, the data described in this section indicate that poly(U) is released from the condensates upon aging. This process can be explained by a decrease in the relative strength of the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) interactions over time, in agreement with the increase of protein-protein interactions shown by aging of homotypic Dhh1-mCh condensates (Fig. 1 A and B).


### Decrease of net Dhh1-poly(U) interactions upon aging
  To support the decrease of the net Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) interaction strength over time, we designed an experiment in which we prepared homotypic condensates of Dhh1-mCh and added polyU-FAM after incubating the samples for 1 or 24 h. As shown in Fig. 3 A and B, when polyU-FAM was added to homotypic Dhh1-mCh condensates incubated for 1 h, within 20 min most of the condensates in the sample became homogeneously fluorescent in the FAM channel. In addition to the recruitment of poly(U) into existing Dhh1-mCh condensates, the introduction of poly(U) promoted the formation of new condensates containing both Dhh1-mCh and polyU-FAM due to the decrease of the saturation concentration required for phase separation, driven by the additional contribution of attractive protein-poly(U) interactions to homotypic protein-protein interactions. The fluorescence intensity within the condensates was relatively homogeneous and similar among different condensates (the average FAM intensity measured within 60 condensates was 40 ± 9 a.u., Fig. 3 B). The diffusion of polyU-FAM within the condensates occurred over a period of 20 min. Considering a typical diffusion distance inside the condensates of 3.5 μm, this timescale corresponds to a diffusion coefficient on the order of 0.01 μm2/s, i.e., more than four orders of magnitude lower than the diffusivity in the dilute phase (150 μm2/s as determined with FCS, Fig. S5).   As observed with fresh Dhh1-mCh condensates, the addition of polyU-FAM to Dhh1-mCh samples incubated for 24 h led to the formation of new condensates (Fig. 3 C). However, the partitioning and diffusion of poly(U) within aged condensates proceeded over a longer timescale. We could observe low polyU-FAM intensity in the center of aged condensates within 30 min. From this timescale and the typical diffusion distance from the rim to the center of the condensates (3 μm), we estimated the upper bound of the poly(U) diffusivity in the aged condensates to be ∼0.001 μm2/s (∼5 orders of magnitude lower than in buffer).

  Importantly, at t = 30 min, fresh and aged condensates coexisting in the sample displayed significantly different fluorescence intensities of polyU-FAM (Fig. 3 D). The fluorescence intensity of polyU-FAM in the center of aged condensates remained lower (I = 25 ± 10 a.u. [n = 30 condensates]) compared with fresh condensates (I = 52 ± 8 a.u. [n = 30]). Although we cannot ensure that poly(U) reached its equilibrium concentration in the dense phase within the time frame of the experiment, the fact that fluorescence intensity of polyU-FAM is lower in the aged as compared with the fresh condensates combined with the fact that poly(U) migrates from the dense to the dilute phase over time as shown with FCS and FLIM (Fig. 2), together suggest that the equilibrium concentration of poly(U) in the aged condensate is lower than in the fresh condensate. This in turn supports the conclusion that that the relative strength of the protein-RNA interaction in the condensate decreases upon aging.


### Cahn-Hilliard theory describes the formation of double-emulsion condensates
  The experimentally observed nucleation of a dilute phase within the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) condensates (Figs. 1 C, D, S1, and S4) can be explained by a decrease in the relative strength of protein-poly(U) interactions with time combined with the slow diffusion of poly(U) and protein inside the dense phase. The observed size-dependent morphology (Figs. S1 and S2) further supports this mechanism, as nucleation of the dilute phase is observed only in large condensates, suggesting a kinetic rather than a thermodynamic effect. Large condensates exhibit a slower response to changes in equilibrium compositions due to longer diffusion paths, which can lead to metastable or unstable compositions. Our experimental results can be quantitatively rationalized using the Cahn-Hilliard phase field model (29). The governing equation that describes the evolution of the order parameter ϕ in time and space is given by (28):(5)∂ϕ∂t=∇·M(ϕ)∇μwhere(6)μ=∂F∂ϕ−ϵ2Δϕ.

  Here, μ represents the chemical potential, M(ϕ) the composition-dependent mobility, and t the time. The chemical potential μ consists of a bulk term (related to the free energy of the system F, first term on right hand side of Eq. 6) as well as a surface term (second term on right hand side of Eq. 6), where the parameter ϵ is related to the surface tension. At equilibrium, the chemical potential must be identical everywhere, resulting in all time derivatives being zero.

  Applying this model to a ternary mixture of protein, poly(U) and solvent, we obtain coupled partial differential equations (Eq. 5) with the volume fractions of protein (ϕp) and poly(U) (ϕu) as order parameters. The free energy of the system F, according to the Flory-Huggins model, is given by (32):(7)F(ϕp,ϕu)=ϕpNpln(ϕp)+ϕuNuln(ϕu)+ϕsNsln(ϕs)+χpuϕpϕu+χpsϕpϕs+χusϕuϕs

  Here, the solvent volume fraction is given by ϕs=1−ϕp−ϕu, χpu, χps, and χus are binary interaction coefficients for protein-poly(U), protein-solvent, and poly(U)-solvent, while Np, Nu, and Ns are the number of monomers for protein, poly(U) and solvent. Under the experimental conditions used in this work, Dhh1-mCh undergoes phase separation in the absence of poly(U) (Fig. 1 A), indicating that the interaction parameter χps is positive. We chose negative values for χpu and χus that lead to phase separation in a range of poly(U) and protein concentrations consistent with experimental data. All model parameters are shown in Table S1, and additional details concerning their choice are reported in the materials and methods.

  The starting point of all simulations is a condensate rich in protein and poly(U) at equilibrium with the dilute phase. The concentrations in the dilute and dense phase were calculated based on the interaction parameters defined above and the total concentrations of protein and poly(U) used in the experiments. To model aging, we decreased the net strength of the protein-RNA interactions by linearly increasing the strength of homotypic protein-protein interactions over time (χpu and χps were increased simultaneously).

  Fig. 4A shows the simulation of a 30 μm condensate obtained from a solution with 20 μM Dhh1-mCh and 0.1 mg/mL polyU-20 (same conditions of the experiments shown in Fig. 1 C). The results of the simulations show that the increasing strength of protein-protein interaction over time leads to slight condensate shrinking and an increase of protein concentration in the dense phase, but no change in the condensate morphology. Condensate shrinking initially increases the concentration of poly(U) in the dense phase, which later decreases due to the decrease of the net protein-poly(U) interactions. The release of poly(U) from the dense phase is consistent with the increase in concentration of poly(U) in the dilute phase measured by FCS (Fig. 2 A). This may result from the balance of interactions in the aged system, with protein-poly(U) interactions being stronger than protein-solvent interactions, and poly(U)-solvent and poly(U)-protein interactions being of comparable strength.   We next applied the model to rationalize the effect of condensate size on the observed formation of double-emulsion condensates over time. While simulations with the 30 μm diameter condensate showed that the properties of the condensates changed over time but no double-emulsion was formed (Fig. 4 A), simulations of a condensate with a larger initial diameter (60 μm) indicated that aging led to nucleation of a dilute phase inside the dense phase (Fig. 4 B). This is consistent with the experimental observation that double-emulsions were formed only in the largest condensates (Figs. 1 C, D, S1, and S2). Indeed, although poly(U) diffusivity inside the condensates is low, poly(U) can still diffuse out of the dense phase due to the relatively short diffusion paths in smaller condensates. In these conditions, aging does not affect the morphology of the condensates (they remain spherical). However, in larger condensates the low diffusivity combined with longer diffusion paths hinders migration of poly(U) to the dilute phase. This leads to the nucleation of a dilute phase inside the condensates to reach the lower equilibrium concentration of poly(U) within the dense phase upon aging. A similar process was recently observed by Erkamp et al. (15) in a poly(A)-PEG system where slow macromolecular diffusion was found to limit the rate at which the condensates respond to environmental changes that affect the equilibrium concentrations of the dense and dilute phases. Double-emulsion condensate formation was observed during rapid temperature changes in condensates exceeding a critical size, which can be explained by deviation of the system from the binodal concentrations due to slow diffusion of the macromolecules and thus to the condensates being out of equilibrium (15). A key difference between the poly(A)-PEG system and the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) system analyzed in this work is the trigger for changes in the equilibrium concentrations of the dense and dilute phases. In the poly(A)-PEG system, changes are driven by a rapid modification of an external parameter (temperature), whereas in the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) system they are driven by changes in intermolecular interactions over time, possibly related to conformational changes during aging. Independently of the trigger, in both systems a double emulsion is formed above a critical condensate size.

  We also probed the effect of poly(U) length on the condensate aging by performing the same simulations with polyU-50 and observed an increase in the threshold condensate diameter and of the time required to form the double emulsion (Fig. S9), which is consistent with experimental results (Fig. 1 D). The larger critical condensate diameter observed with polyU-50 may seem counterintuitive, since the slower diffusion of polyU-50 compared with polyU-20 is expected to facilitate nucleation of a dilute phase inside the dense phase. However, due to the larger molecular size and thus greater number of protein interactions sites per poly(U) molecule, polyU-50 displays overall stronger interaction with the protein and therefore a lower tendency to migrate toward the dilute phase.

  All together, our model is capable of rationalizing the observed experimental trends for protein-polyU-20 and protein-polyU-50 condensates, further confirming the proposed mechanism underlying the formation of double-emulsion and core-shell structures upon aging of the Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) condensates.


## Conclusions
  We have shown that heterotypic Dhh1-mCh-poly(U) condensates undergo aging over time, which results in the formation of multicompartment architectures depending on the condensate size. By a combination of experimental and theoretical approaches, we have demonstrated that a net decrease in the strength of protein-poly(U) interactions triggers release of poly(U) molecules into the dilute phase. For condensates smaller than a critical size, the diffusion of poly(U) from the dense to the dilute phase, as aging proceeds, does not change condensate morphology. In contrast, for condensates larger than a critical size, dilute phase nucleates inside the dense phase, leading to the formation of a double-emulsion structure. In analogy to previous work (15), this process is ascribed to the slow diffusion of macromolecules inside the dense phase, which constitutes a limiting step to attaining new equilibrium concentrations in the system during aging. This work shows that multicompartment architectures can originate in nonequilibrium condensates undergoing aging over time, leading to kinetically arrested double emulsions.

  This result, together with recent findings of demixing of heterotypic condensates formed by RNA and the RNA-binding protein Whi3 (23), show how the formation of multicompartment condensates can be under kinetic control, in addition to thermodynamic driving forces. The implications may have significance in biology, since aging of biocondensates has been linked to numerous pathological states (33,34,35,36), and in the material sciences for the development of kinetically arrested double emulsions based on substructured condensates. These could potentially lead, e.g., to microcapsules (37), with applications in protein or RNA release.
