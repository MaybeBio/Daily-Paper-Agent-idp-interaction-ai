# Regulating transport efficiency through the nuclear pore complex: The role of binding affinity with FG-Nups


## Abstract
  Macromolecules are transported through the nuclear pore complex (NPC) via a series of transient binding and unbinding events with FG-Nups, which are intrinsically disordered proteins anchored to the pore's inner wall. Prior studies suggest that the weak and transient nature of this binding is crucial for maintaining the transported molecules’ diffusivity. In this study, we explored the relationship between binding kinetics and transport efficiency using Brownian dynamics simulations. Our results indicate that the duration of binding is a critical factor in regulating transport efficiency. Specifically, excessively short binding durations insufficiently facilitate transport, while overly long durations impede molecular movement. We calculated the optimal binding duration for efficient molecular transport and found that it aligns with other theoretical predictions. Additionally, the calculated value is comparable to experimental measurements of the association timescale between nuclear transport receptors and FG-Nups at a single binding site. Our study provides a quantitative framework that bridges local molecular interactions with overall transport dynamics through the NPC, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms governing selective molecular transport.


## INTRODUCTION
  Eukaryotic cells compartmentalize their subcellular space into two primary regions: the nucleus and the cytoplasm. Communication between these regions is facilitated by the nuclear pore complex (NPC), a channel embedded in the nuclear envelope (Hoogenboom et al., 2021; Stewart, 2022; Cowburn and Rout, 2023; Rush et al., 2023). The NPC is composed of around 30 distinct types of proteins known as nucleoporins (Nups). Each NPC contains multiple copies of these Nups, resulting in a total of 500 to 1000 Nups incorporated into its structure. Some of these Nups form the scaffold of the pore, integrating the structure into the nuclear membrane (NE), while others, known as FG-Nups, create a polymer mesh-like structure within the pore (Alber et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2018; Mosalaganti et al., 2022). FG-Nups are intrinsically disordered proteins that are rich in phenylalanine and glycine motifs (FG-motifs), enabling them to form weak and transient hydrophobic connections (Denning and Rexach, 2007; Yamada et al., 2010; Ando et al., 2013; Peyro et al., 2021b). One end of each FG-Nup is anchored to the inner channel of the NPC, and the other end extends freely within the pore.

  The NPC plays an essential role in selective molecular transport, crucial for safeguarding the genetic material inside the nucleus (Jamali et al., 2011; Soheilypour and Mofrad, 2018; Bensidoun et al., 2021). It allows free passage for small molecules under 40 kDa, while larger molecules require nuclear transport receptors (NTRs) to facilitate their transport. This selectivity is primarily enforced by the FG-Nups, whose mesh structure imposes a size constraint on molecules passing through the pore. NTRs feature multiple hydrophobic spots on their surfaces, which interact with FG-motifs. These interactions facilitate the incorporation of the cargo complex attached to NTRs into the mesh structure, thus enabling the transport of larger cargoes across the NPC.

  To elucidate the detailed mechanism for the selective transport, extensive research has focused on the dynamic interactions between NTRs and FG-Nups (Aramburu and Lemke, 2017). Studies highlight that the hydrophobic interactions between these entities are weak and transient, facilitating rapid molecule passage through the pore. Quantitative analyses of the interactions between NTR binding spots and FG-repeats reveal a dissociation constant, Kd, ranging from µM to mM (Hough et al., 2015; Milles et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2018). The binding rate, kon, is ∼109 M−1s−1 (Milles et al., 2015), leading to a dissociation time, τ = (Kdkon)−1, of microseconds to milliseconds. This time frame is comparable or shorter to the transit time of molecules through the NPC, which is about 1–10 ms (Ribbeck and Görlich, 2001; Yang et al., 2004; Kubitscheck et al., 2005). Additionally, NTRs typically possess multiple binding sites, enhancing their collective interaction with FG-repeats. This significantly reduces the effective dissociation constant to the nM range (Ben-Efraim and Gerace, 2001; Bednenko et al., 2003; Tetenbaum-Novatt et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2018). This difference between individual binding affinity and overall binding avidity is considered crucial, as it allows for both rapid and specific transport of molecules through the NPC.

  While the binding kinetics between NTRs and FG-Nups are increasingly understood, their impact on transport dynamics within the NPC remains less explored. In general, the diffusive dynamics within the polymer network exhibit diverse behaviors, with the binding kinetics acting as a critical regulator (Cao et al., 2017; Yamamoto et al., 2018; Rasid et al., 2021; Rao et al., 2021). For instance, if the binding affinity is too weak, the effect on transport is negligible, and it does not facilitate molecular movement. Conversely, if the affinity is too strong, it can hinder diffusion. It is hypothesized that there exists an optimal binding affinity that facilitates the incorporation of NTRs into the mesh structure as well as maintains the high diffusivity of the molecules.

  In this study, we explored how the binding kinetics between the cargo complex and FG-Nups influence their diffusive dynamics and transport efficiency across the NPC. We utilized a coarse-grained Brownian dynamics simulation model (Moussavi-Baygi et al., 2011a, 2011b; Moussavi-Baygi and Mofrad, 2016; Peyro et al., 2021a) to delve into the nanoscale diffusive dynamics, a realm challenging to probe with experimental methods. Our approach involved a simplified model system comprising sticker and spacer beads, allowing us to isolate and examine the pure effects of binding kinetics on the dynamics. Our findings revealed a distinct monomodal relationship between the dissociation time and transport efficiency, where the optimal value closely matched the single binding spot affinity observed experimentally. This result implies that the binding kinetics within the natural NPC system are finely tuned to optimize transport efficiency.


## RESULTS

### Soft-core potential effectively reproduced the binding and unbinding dynamics
  We simulated the transport dynamics of the cargo through the NPC using the coarse-grained Brownian dynamics model. Given the intrinsically disordered nature of FG-Nups (Aramburu and Lemke, 2017; Hoogenboom et al., 2021; Matsuda et al., 2024), which we consider a key factor in regulating transport dynamics, we designed a simulation system in which flexible polymers are anchored to the inner surface of a cylindrical pore (Figure 1). FG-Nups were modeled as a series of beads connected by springs, with each bead having a diameter of σ = 0.86 nm, corresponding to the Kuhn length of FG-Nups (Lim et al., 2006). The beads were categorized as either stickers or spacers: sticker beads represented amino acid residues that promote hydrophobic interactions, such as FG-motifs in FG-Nups or hydrophobic regions on the cargo surface, while spacer beads represented residues without specific attractive properties. In FG-Nups, stickers were placed every four beads, reflecting the ∼25% hydrophobic residue coverage (Yamada et al., 2010). Stickers were also placed on the cargo, with their quantity determined by surface coverage parameters, S. We defined the potential energy of the bead-spring system as outlined in the Materials and Methods section and calculated their dynamics using the overdamped Langevin equation, also described in detail in the Materials and Methods section. Using this Brownian dynamics model, our goal was to investigate the fundamental mechanism by which flexible polymers regulate molecular transport—insights that are essential for understanding NPC function and have broader applications in biomimetics constructs (Ying et al., 2022).

  We aimed to reproduce the transient and repetitive bindings between the cargo and FG-Nups, which is the key feature for the selective transport (Aramburu and Lemke, 2017). To achieve this, we implemented a soft-core potential for the interactions between beads, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section. This potential is characterized by its absence of singularities at close bead distances, effectively preventing drastic changes in the force field and ensuring smooth transitions between the bound and unbound states. Supplemental Movie S1 presents a brief segment of the sample simulation (parameters: ε/ε0 = 2.0, rcut/σ = 1.8, d = 6.0 nm and S/S0 = 1.0). In the movie, the red and blue color indicates the bound and unbound states of individual beads, respectively. A bead was classified as “bound” if 1) it was a sticker bead and 2) another sticker bead was within the cut-off distance, rcut. In all other scenarios, the bead was considered “unbound” (see the Materials and Methods section for the detailed definition). The proportion of the cargo's surface in the bound state continuously fluctuated, showcasing the frequent binding and unbinding events. Despite the rapid interactions between beads, the system remained stable without exhibiting unreasonably large displacements, demonstrating the advantage of using the soft-core potential.

  Using the soft-core potential, we studied how the binding and unbinding dynamics of individual beads affect the overall transport behavior of the cargo through the NPC. The dynamics of each bead were modulated by two physical parameters: the binding energy, ε, and the cut-off distance, rcut, which governs the bead-bead attraction (refer to the Materials and Methods section for the exact formula of the pairwise potential energy). As expected, increasing ε and rcut prolonged the duration that individual beads remained bound to each other. Given that the cohesion between FG-repeats and the attractions between FG-repeats and the cargo are both driven by hydrophobic forces (Naim et al., 2009; Yoshimura et al., 2014; Aramburu and Lemke, 2017; Opakua et al., 2022), we applied the same pairwise potential to all attractive beads, regardless of whether the bead belongs to FG-Nups or the cargo. We selected the parameter range, ε/ε0 = 1.0−3.0 and rcut/σ = 1.6−2.0, to ensure that the overall morphology of the FG-Nups assembly remained relatively unchanged (Figure 2A). This approach contrasts with other simulation studies that investigating the global transition of FG-Nups from extended to aggregated states (Mishra et al., 2019; Zheng and Zilman, 2023). By avoiding such global structure changes, we aimed to isolate and specifically examine the effect of local binding interactions on transport dynamics.


### Transport efficiency reached its peak at intermediate levels of binding interactions
  To explore how local binding interactions influence overall transport behavior, we simulated the system dynamics across various parameter configurations. The simulation was conducted in a Lx × Ly × Lz box, where a membrane containing a modeled NPC divided the space into two regions (Figure 1). We placed the cargo on one side of the membrane and examined whether it would be transported to the opposite side. Each trial began by positioning the cargo at the initial location, (x, y, z) = (0, 0, z0), followed by equilibrating the FG-Nups for teq nanoseconds. After equilibration, the system's dynamics were simulated for tsim nanoseconds. We continuously monitored the cargo's center position and considered it successfully transported if its axial position crossed the membrane boundary. A transport event was deemed successful if the cargo crossed the boundary at least once within the simulation time of tsim nanoseconds, even if it later moved back across the boundary (see the Materials and Methods section for parameter values and detailed process descriptions).

  Supplemental Movie S2–S4 and Figure 2A illustrate how the system evolved under different binding energies: ε/ε0 = 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0. The other parameters were held constant at rcut/σ = 1.8, d = 6.0 nm, and S/S0 = 1.0. When the binding energy was ε/ε0 = 1.0, the interaction between the cargo and FG-Nups was too weak to draw the cargo into the pore region. Increasing the binding energy to ε/ε0 = 2.0 strengthened the interaction, enabling successful cargo transport. However, when the binding energy was further increased to ε/ε0 = 3.0, the cargo became trapped within the FG-Nups network and failed to move to the other side of the pore. This trend is more clearly depicted in Figure 2B, which shows the trajectory of the cargo's center, color-coded according to its bound or unbound status. Here, the bound/unbound status was assessed for the entire cargo, rather than for individual beads. The cargo was classified as bound if at least one of its constituent beads was in the bound state (see the Materials and Methods section for the detailed definition of cargo bound/unbound states). Please note that in Figure 2A, the configurations of FG-Nups at each time step appear similar across different ε values, due to the use of the same random number seed. However, despite using the same seed for calculating the cargo's dynamics, its trajectory varied significantly between trials. This difference likely stems from the fact that the cargo's motion is less constrained compared with that of FG-Nups. While FG-Nups are tethered to the wall and interconnected at multiple points within the pore, the cargo lacks such configurational restrictions, allowing it greater freedom to explore different pathways during transport.

  We repeated the simulation ntrial times for each parameter set to further investigate the statistic behavior. Figure 3A shows the collective positions of the cargo's center from all trials. The trend observed in Figure 2 persisted across different trials; increasing the binding energy from ε/ε0 = 1.0 to 2.0 led to a higher flux through the pore, while further increasing it to ε/ε0 = 3.0 reduced the flux due to the cargo becoming trapped within the FG-Nups structure. Quantitatively, the fraction of successful transport events relative to the total number of trials, ntrial, indicates that the intermediate binding energy, ε/ε0 = 2.0, facilitates the transport more than twice as effectively as other cases, ε/ε0 = 1.0 and 3.0 (Figure 3C). Hereafter, we refer to this fraction as “transport efficiency,” as a measure of the system's ability to successfully complete transport events.

  We then calculated the transport efficiency across different values of ε and rcut, generating a heat map in Figure 3D. The results revealed a consistent monomodal trend, with the highest transport efficiency occurring at intermediate levels of binding energy and cut-off distance. The optimal values of ε for peak flux shifted with changes in rcut. A larger cut-off distance, which effectively strengthens the sticker-sticker interactions, reduced the energy value needed for optimal molecular transport. In contrast, a smaller cut-off distance required a higher energy parameter to achieve optimal transport. This suggests the importance of precisely tuning these two parameters, highlighting their critical role in the efficiency of the process.


### Characteristic binding time is the key determinant in controlling the transport dynamics
  Our results indicate that the parameters regulating local affinity between beads, such as binding energy, ε, and cut-off distance, rcut, significantly influence the global motion of the cargo and its transport efficiency. To better understand this relationship, we aimed to identify a metric that could comprehensively capture the effects of these local parameters. Specifically, we hypothesized that the timescale during which a cargo remains within the potential energy well formed by surrounding FG-Nups could serve as an effective measure. Accordingly, we defined the binding time, τ, as outlined below, and analyzed its relationship with transport efficiency.

  To define the binding time, τ, we first measured the duration for which the cargo remains in its “bound” state. The bound state of the cargo is defined by the condition that at least one of its stickers is within the cut-off distance of an FG-Nup's sticker (see the Materials and Methods section). Figure 4A illustrates how the bound state of the cargo evolves over time for different binding energy, ε. The cargo oscillated between bound and unbound states, and the time spent in the bound state increased as the binding energy, ε, increased. When the binding energy was ε/ε0 = 3.0, the cargo remained in the bound state for the entire simulation duration in most cases. For these instances, we defined the binding duration to be equal to the total simulation time, 200 µs.

  The measured binding durations were binned for each trial, and this process was repeated across all trials. We then analyzed the resulting histogram by calculating the complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) (see the Materials and Methods section). Figure 4B presents an example CCDF for different binding energy, ε. As the binding energy increased, the timescale over which the CCDF decayed became longer. We modeled the CCDF using the exponential function exp(−t/τ) to determine the characteristic binding time, τ. For the case when most binding duration match the total simulation time, such as when ε/ε0 = 3.0 and rcut/σ = 1.8 as shown in Figure 4B, the actual characteristic binding time likely exceeds the total simulation time. Nonetheless, we applied the same formula to estimate the binding time, τ, establishing an accuracy limit for τ at ∼200 µs.

  In Figure 4C, we illustrate the characteristic binding times across various binding energy, ε, and cut-off distances, rcut. Both larger binding energy and cut-off distances led to increased binding times. By plotting the transport efficiency (Figure 3D) against the binding times (Figure 4C) as shown in Figure 5A, we observed a clear correlation between these variables. This suggests that binding time, τ, serves as a comprehensive parameter that captures the effects of various local parameters, such as binding energy, ε, and cut-off distance, rcut. We also observed that the most efficient transport occurs at intermediate binding times. The bell-shaped trend in the Figure 5A indicates that transport efficiency reaches its peak at an intermediate binding time, around τ ∼ 101–102 µs.


### Optimal binding time scale for efficient transport remained constant across various conditions
  To evaluate the robustness of our measurement of the optimal binding time, we conducted two additional control simulations. For these simulations, we reduced the number of trials, ntrial = 20, from 40 to save computational cost. Other parameters, such as the cargo size (d = 6.0 nm) and binding surface area (S/S0 = 1.0), were kept the same. First, we extended the simulation time, tsim, from 2 × 105 ns to 4 × 105 ns. The results (Figure 5B) showed an overall increase in transport efficiency, likely because the longer simulation time allowed for more transport events to occur. Second, we increased the size of the simulation box, (Lx, Ly, Lz), from (100, 100, 108) to (200, 200, 208) while maintaining the same boundary conditions. As shown in Figure 5C, transport efficiency decreased slightly, likely due to the larger volume reducing the probability of cargoes reaching the pore. Despite these changes, in both cases, the characteristic bell-shaped relationship between transport efficiency and binding time persisted, with the optimal binding time remaining consistent at τ ∼ 10–100 µs. These control simulations demonstrate the robustness of our findings, supporting our decision to retain the original parameter values for the subsequent analysis.

  To further investigate the relationship between the transport efficiency and characteristic binding time, we conducted additional simulations using cargoes with a larger attraction surface, S/S0 = 2.0, and an increased diameter of d = 8.0 nm (compared with the previous conditions of S/S0 = 1.0 and d = 6.0 nm). The variations in surface area and cargo size are visually represented in Figure 1C. We repeated the same simulations and analyses as conducted in the previous sections to investigate whether the relationship between transport efficiency and binding time, τ, persists under these new conditions.

  Increasing the binding surface area from S/S0 = 1.0 to S/S0 = 2.0 led to a shift in the optimal parameters for achieving the most efficient molecular transport, favoring lower energy scales, ε, and shorter cut-off distances, rcut (Figure 6A, top). Additionally, there was an increase in the characteristic binding time for each parameter set (Figure 6A, bottom), which is logical considering that cargoes with larger binding surfaces are more likely to form bonds with FG-Nups, thereby extending the duration of binding. In Figure 6B, we plotted the fraction of transported molecules against the characteristic binding time. Despite these shifts, the correlation between them remained consistent, peaking at a binding time scale of approximately τ ∼ 101–102 µs.

  Increasing the cargo diameter from d = 6.0 nm to d = 8.0 nm resulted in a decrease in the number of cargoes successfully passing through the NPC (Figure 7A, top). This outcome is expected as larger cargoes face greater difficulty navigating through the pore populated with FG-Nups. Despite a lower baseline in transport efficiency, the parameter sets that yielded peak efficiencies remained similar to those in the reference case. The characteristic binding times showed minimal variation between the smaller and larger cargoes (Figure 7A, bottom). When plotting the fraction of transported molecules against the binding time, the general trend appeared consistent with other scenarios, although there was a slight shift toward a longer optimal binding time for achieving the best transport efficiency, that is, τ ∼ 101–102 µs (Figure 7B).

  These data collectively suggest that the optimal time scale for achieving the best transport efficiency, τ ∼ 101–102 µs, remains constant across various cargo conditions. This supports our hypothesis that the characteristic binding time between stickers is the primary determinant of the cargo's dynamics and its transport efficiency.


### Cargoes with scattered binding sites exhibit reduced transport efficiency
  In our previous simulations, we modeled cargoes with a clustered sticker distribution, which effectively forms a single patch of attractive surface (Figure 1C). To explore how a different arrangement of stickers might impact the transport dynamics, we carried out simulations where the stickers were scattered randomly across the cargo surface. We prepared cargoes with a diameter of d = 6.0 nm and randomly assigned the stickers to individual beads, creating a scattered distribution (Figure 8A). For this analysis, we examined three cases where the ratio of stickers to total beads, representing the relative attractive surface area, was set to S/S0 = 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0. Simulations were conducted for each of these cases to calculate the transport efficiency and characteristic binding times. Due to the constraints in our computational resources, the number of trials in this section was reduced to ntrial = 20, compared with the 40 trials used in other sections.

  The simulation results showed a significant decrease in the characteristic binding time, τ, when comparing the scattered sticker distribution (Figure 8C) to the clustered distribution cases (Figure 6). For the cases where S/S0 = 1.0 and 2.0, the binding time was reduced by several orders of magnitude, solely due to changing the sticker distribution from clustered to scattered. To recover binding times comparable to those in our previous simulations, it was necessary to increase the surface area to S/S0 = 3.0. The reduction in binding time can be explained by the following: when stickers are clustered, they collectively form a larger potential well, making it more difficult for FG-Nups to escape, thereby increasing the binding time. This observation aligns with findings from other simulation studies (Davis et al., 2021; Matsuda and Mofrad, 2021), where similar decreased binding profiles were observed for scattered sticker distributions, and increased binding times for clustered distributions.

  For the case of S/S0 = 1.0, the reduction in binding time led to a low fraction of successful transport events across all data (Figure 8B). In contrast, for S/S0 = 2.0, the transport fraction was higher for larger ε and rcutoff values, corresponding to a binding time of approximately τ ∼ 10–100 µs (Figure 8B). In both cases, however, the data did not reach the higher range of binding times, τ, preventing us from observing its optimal value (Figure 8D). On the other hand, for S/S0 = 3.0, the binding time was explored over a wider range, revealing a clear peak in the transport fraction as a function of binding time (Figure 8D). This peak again occurred at τ ∼ 10–100 µs. These results confirm that the characteristic binding time, τ, is the key parameter controlling cargo transport dynamics, and its optimal value remains conserved across different conditions.


## DISCUSSION
  In this study, we investigated the role of attractive interactions between the cargo and FG-Nups in regulating transport efficiency through the NPC. Our simulations reveal that a binding duration that is too short does not effectively support cargo transport, while excessively long binding times can trap the cargo within the FG-Nups network, hindering its passage through the pore. We identified an optimal binding duration, calculated to be τ ∼ 101–102 µs.

  Our results align with the reaction-diffusion model (Yang et al., 2018; Maguire et al., 2019), which also predicted the existence of an optimal binding affinity. Maguire and colleagues modeled the concentration profiles of FG-Nups, free cargo (unbound to FG-Nups), and FG-Nup–cargo complexes as a function of the axial distance within the pore (Maguire et al., 2019). Under the assumptions that 1) FG-Nups and cargo undergo repeated binding and unbinding reactions with given association and dissociation rate constants, and 2) both free cargo and FG-Nup–cargo complexes move through the pore with defined diffusion coefficients, they formulated a reaction-diffusion equation describing these dynamics. The steady-state solution of the model showed that the flux of FG-Nup–cargo complexes, normalized by that of free cargo, reaches a peak at an optimal dissociation constant of Kd ∼1–10 µM. Yang and colleagues also modeled the system dynamics using a set of reaction-diffusion equations (Yang et al., 2018). Instead of relying on a single diffusion coefficient, they assumed that the multivalent binding and unbinding events between the cargo and FG-Nups define the cargo's motion, effectively incorporating them into the diffusion coefficient. Their results similarly indicated the presence of an optimal dissociation constant, Kd ∼ 10–100 µM. Collectively, these models suggest that the efficiency of cargo transport through the NPC hinges on precisely adjusting local binding affinities.

  To compare our results with experimental measurements, we calculated the corresponding dissociation constants using Kd = (τ kon)−1. In our simulation, the association of the two stickers is diffusion-controlled, so we adopted a diffusion-limited association rate of kon ∼ 109 M−1s−1 (Berg, 1993). This rate is consistent with experimental observations, where stopped-flow spectroscopy and molecular dynamics simulations also reported a fast association rate of kon ∼ 109 M−1s−1 between FG-Nups and NTRs (Milles et al., 2015). Given a binding time of τ = 10–100 µs, the corresponding dissociation constant is estimated to be Kd = 10–100 µM. This value aligns with previous modeling studies, where the optimal dissociation constant was found to be 10–100 µM in Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2018) and 1–10 µM in Maguire et al. (Maguire et al., 2019).

  Experimentally, the dissociation constant between FG-Nups and NTRs has been measured using various techniques (Table 1). When considering a single binding site, the affinity is typically low, with Kd values ranging from the µM to mM scale. However, NTRs typically possess multiple binding sites, and when their collective interaction with FG-Nups is assessed, they exhibit significantly higher binding avidity, typically on the order of nM. In comparison to these experimental studies, the dissociation constant from our simulation (Kd = 10–100 µM) is closer to the affinity of individual binding sites, rather than the avidity observed with multivalent interactions. Given that each sticker is on the scale of 1–2 amino acids, several stickers together likely form a single functional binding spot, making this result reasonable. In simulations where cargoes had scattered sticker distributions, individual stickers did not function as stable binding spots, further supporting this interpretation. On the other hand, our simulation system should also exhibit some characteristics of collective binding, as multiple FG-Nups stickers can simultaneously bind to a “single” binding surface represented by a collective set of stickers. This makes it somewhat surprising that the dissociation constant from our simulation deviated significantly from the experimental avidity data. Future studies should explore key questions, such as how large the binding spot needs to be to effectively capture multivalent binding characteristics, and whether the resulting dissociation constant approaches experimentally measured values.

  In conclusion, our study highlights the pivotal role of the cargo–FG-Nups interaction timescale in regulating transport efficiency through the NPC. We identified an optimal binding duration that aligns with predictions from other theoretical models, providing deeper insights into the molecular transport through the NPC. If experimentally validated, these findings could pave the way for advancements in protein engineering, such as designing molecules that traverse the NPC more efficiently by fine-tuning their surface properties. Furthermore, the expanding field of biomimetic nanopore technology (Fisher et al., 2018; Ketterer et al., 2018; Ying et al., 2022) could leverage this knowledge to improve molecular filtration systems. Ongoing experimental efforts will be crucial to confirm this hypothesis and explore its broader applications in both biological research and engineering innovation.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Coarse-grained model
  Our simulation was conducted within a spatial domain of dimensions Lx × Ly × Lz. The space was divided into two regions, z > h/2 and z < −h/2, by a partition with thickness h, representing the NE with a computationally reduced thickness. Within this partition, we positioned a cylindrical pore of diameter D to model the NPC.

  FG-Nups were modeled as bead-springs tethered to the inner surface of the NPC. We arranged nFG FG-Nups on the pore wall in an octagonally symmetrical pattern. Each FG-Nup consisted of N beads, each represented as a sphere with a diameter of σ. We used two types of beads: stickers, representing FG-motifs, and spacers, representing other amino acid residues. In calculating the pairwise potential among beads, stickers exhibited attractive interactions with each other, whereas spacers did not. Reflecting the average proportion of FG-motifs in FG-Nups, we placed one sticker for every three spacers.

  The cargo was modeled as a sphere with a diameter d, with its surface discretized into beads identical to those used for FG-Nups. Stickers were placed on a portion of the cargo, while the remainder of the surface was covered with spacers.

### Boundary conditions
  At the edge of the simulation domain, the faces in the x and y directions were modeled as periodic boundaries, while the face in the z direction was modeled as a reflecting boundary. Similarly, the surfaces of the NE and NPC were also treated as reflecting boundaries. When the center of a bead approached within σ/2 of a reflecting boundary, a constant force Fwall was applied in the direction opposite to the normal of the boundary surface.

### Bound and unbound state
  A bead was defined as “bound” when one of its pairwise potential terms followed the first equation in Eq. 4. Specifically, a bead was considered bound if both it and the interacting bead were stickers, and their distance was within the cut-off distance, rcut. If these conditions were not met, the bead was classified as “unbound.” Notably, if the bead was part of the FG-Nups, pairwise interactions were evaluated with both FG-Nup and cargo beads, whereas for cargo beads, it was evaluated only with FG-Nups.

  The entire cargo was classified as “bound” when at least one of its constituent beads was in its bound state. The bound state of the entire cargo was used for the visualization in Figure 2B and for the calculation of the binding time, τ, between the cargo and FG-Nups.


### Characteristic binding time for the cargo
  The calculation of binding time, τ, was based on the bound state of the entire cargo, instead of the individual bead. We measured the duration for which the cargo remained bound to FG-Nups across ntrial trials and compiled the data into a histogram. Next, we calculated the cumulative sum of the data, which was then normalized to produce the Cumulative Distribution Function, CDF(t). The CDF(t) represents the probability that the binding duration time is less than or equal to a specific time t. Following this, we calculated the Complementary Cumulative Distribution Function such that CCDF(t) = 1−CDF(t). By fitting the CCDF(t) to an exponential decay model exp(−t/τ), we determined the characteristic binding duration time, τ. This time τ quantitatively describes the typical persistence of binding interactions under the conditions tested.


### Calculation of the mesh size
  The effective mesh size of the FG-Nups system, ξ, was calculated using the scaling relation ξ ≈ bϕ−ν/(3ν−1), where b = σ = 0.86 nm is the Kuhn length, ϕ is the volume fraction of the polymers, and ν = 0.566 is the Flory exponent (De Gennes, 1979; Rubinstein and Colby, 2003). We determined the volume fraction ϕ as ϕ = VFG/Vpore, where VFG = nFGN × πb3/6 is the total FG-Nups volume and Vpore = πD2h/4+πD3/6 is the effective volume that FG-Nups dynamically occupy in space. Using the values presented in Table 2, we calculated the mesh size as ξ = 24 nm.
