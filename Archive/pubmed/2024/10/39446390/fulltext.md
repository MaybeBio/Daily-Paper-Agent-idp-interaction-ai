# AlphaFold-Multimer accurately captures interactions and dynamics of intrinsically disordered protein regions


## Significance
  The contribution of intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDRs) to cellular processes often hinges on their exquisite interaction versatility. The question arises whether complexity of IDR interactions can be captured by AI-based structure prediction methods primarily trained on independently folding domains. We demonstrate the capability of AlphaFold-Multimer to accurately predict structures adopted by IDRs upon binding. Although predictions are less successful for IDRs that interact in more heterogeneous manners, AlphaFold-Multimer is able to identify segments within IDRs that exhibit greater dynamic behavior. We introduce a metric that pinpoints IDR residues that most likely bind a given partner, which may make AlphaFold-Multimer-based screens for interactions more efficient. Importantly, we provide clear guidelines for using AlphaFold-Multimer to predict various types of IDR-mediated interactions.


## Results

### Dataset Characteristics.
  For our analysis, we assembled multiple datasets of protein complexes in which one interaction partner is intrinsically disordered in isolation. We focused on dimeric protein complexes that comprise an intrinsically disordered protein (IDR chain) and a structured globular protein (receptor chain). The first dataset contains a collection of complexes that we previously and carefully curated (32). This “high-confidence” set (IDRBind) contains 73 complexes for which experimental data indicate intrinsic disorder in isolation and folding upon binding of one interaction partner. However, all complexes in IDRBind predate 2018 April 30, the AlphaFold-Multimer training set cutoff date. It is likely that at least some of these complexes were used in the training of AlphaFold-Multimer, and begs for a nonseen (blind) dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Therefore we assembled an additional dataset, using the methodology initially proposed in ref. 33 that exploits the fact that IDRs often adopt expanded conformations in the complex structure, i.e., they have a large radius of gyration (Rg) for their sequence length (see Materials and Methods for details). Applying this approach on PDB structures deposited after 2018 April 30 and excluding structures similar to those in the AlphaFold-Multimer training set allowed us to identify 210 complexes (Rg set). We compared interface size and predicted disorder content (Materials and Methods) of the IDRs from the IDRBind and Rg sets with 42 complexes from a recent study by Bret et al. (31) that assessed AlphaFold-Multimer on protein–peptide interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). The Rg set IDRs have significantly larger interfaces than those in the Bret et al. set, despite similar disorder levels predicted with different methods. These results demonstrate that the Rg set complements the set of Bret et al. by providing IDRs with larger interaction interfaces. The “high-confidence” IDRBind set shows a higher predicted disorder content than the Rg and Bret et al. sets (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). To refine the Rg set and reduce potential false positives, we, therefore, selected the half with the highest predicted disorder, defining the Rg+ID set, which includes 105 complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C).

  In order to assess AlphaFold-Multimer’s ability to predict dynamic and multisite binding of IDRs, we also assembled a “Fuzzy” dataset by utilizing annotations available in FuzDB (34). This is a database of experimentally observed fuzzy protein complexes. Briefly, we queried FuzDB for protein complex structures that had been determined through nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and for which “fuzziness” is defined by the structural variability of the IDR and not the receptor. The IDRs in these 37 Fuzzy complexes form interfaces that are similar in size to the ones present in the IDRBind set (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), but the predicted disorder of these IDRs is significantly lower than the one calculated for IDRBind (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). IDRs in the fuzzy dataset display significantly higher heterogeneity as inferred from root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSFs) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Overall, we collected datasets of protein complexes that cover a large spectrum of interface sizes, contain IDRs that are likely to have different levels of structural content in isolation, and have unique or multiple binding modes of the IDRs. Also, we gathered protein complexes that are blind to AlphaFold-Multimer, as well as high-confidence complexes. This diversity of features allows for an extensive assessment of AlphaFold-Multimer’s ability to predict the structure and dynamics or protein interactions involving IDRs.


### AlphaFold-Multimer Predicts Different IDR-Containing Complexes with High Success Rate but Varying Quality.
  We first assessed the prediction performance of AlphaFold-Multimer v2.2 (Materials and Methods) using the sequences of the receptor and the IDR as provided in the PDB SEQRES records, i.e., only taking the sequence segments that were used to determine the structures. If multiple experimental structures were available, we compared AlphaFold-Multimer predictions with the first deposited model, as done previously (26, 28–31). To measure success in structure prediction, we defined critical assessment of prediction of interactions (CAPRI) classification metrics based on DockQ (35) (Materials and Methods) that group predictions into inaccurate structures and structures with acceptable, medium, or high quality based on a combination of several quality measures including the fraction of correct native interface contacts and differently calculated RMSDs. We found that AlphaFold-Multimer generates correct complex structures for over 76% of the samples present in each of the datasets we assembled (Fig. 1A). This performance is comparable to AlphaFold-Multimer’s performance on the peptide-protein dataset assembled by Bret et al. (Fig. 1A). However, there are noticeable differences in the quality of the correctly predicted complex structures. While about 25% of the predicted structures from the Rg and Rg+ID sets are of high quality, this quality class drops to about 3% for the Fuzzy set. Differences in prediction qualities for the various sets are also reflected in the distribution of DockQ scores (Fig. 1B). While the median DockQ scores are close to 0.65 for the complexes in the Rg/Rg+ID sets, respectively, the median scores are significantly lower for complexes in the IDRBind and Fuzzy datasets (P-values: 2.6 × 10−2 and 1.8 × 10−4, Mann–Whitney U test). Overall, the assessment revealed that AlphaFold-Multimer predicts the structures of IDR-containing protein complexes with a high success rate, but the quality of the predictions varies significantly between sets.

  All structures in the Fuzzy and many in the IDRBind set were determined via NMR spectroscopy, meaning that multiple structure models are available for each complex. The AlphaFold-Multimer structure with highest Combined score (see below for definition) may be closer to any other than the first deposited NMR model. Moreover, any of the structures generated by AlphaFold-Multimer may be the most accurate prediction of any of the deposited NMR models. Therefore, we compared all predicted structures with all NMR models and extracted two new metrics. One where we took the maximum DockQ for each predicted structure and one where we took the maximum DockQ for each NMR model. The first one measures how precise the predictions are and the second one measures how well the population of NMR conformations are covered by AlphaFold-Multimer predictions. As can be seen from SI Appendix, Fig. S2, this approach did not lead to a drastic change in the overall success rate across the two datasets, nor did it have a major impact on the quality of the predictions.


### AlphaFold-Multimer Intrinsic Scores Allow Separating Decoy and Inaccurate Structures from High-Quality Ones.
  The inherent self-explanatory nature of AlphaFold-Multimer is one of its most advantageous features, as it can generate intrinsic scores to indicate the quality and reliability of its predicted structures. The predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT), the Predicted Aligned Error (PAE), and the predicted template modeling (pTM) scores are prime examples of this capability and provide assessments of the local prediction confidence at the residue level, the confidence in the relative position of two residues and the accuracy of the entire structure, respectively. For protein complex predictions, specific metrics such as the interface-pTM (ipTM) and the Combined score (calculated as 0.8·ipTM+0.2·pTM) have been developed to capture and include the accuracy of the predicted relative positions of the complex members. The Combined score is particularly valuable because it is commonly used to rank AlphaFold-Multimer predictions, with the highest-ranked structure often being selected for further analysis. We tested to which extent the Combined score reflects the quality of the predictions and plotted the distribution of the Combined score for each dataset (Fig. 1C). We found that differences in the distribution of the Combined score across datasets are consistent with the distribution differences in prediction qualities as measured by DockQ scores. Indeed, the Combined score and the DockQ score are highly correlated for each dataset, though the correlation is smaller for the Fuzzy than other datasets (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

  Next, we assessed whether AlphaFold-Multimer scores separate true from fake complexes. To this end, we compared the distribution of the Combined score for true and decoy complexes. We created decoy complexes by substituting in receptor-IDR pairs the disordered binder by other IDRs from the same protein the cognate binder originates from (see Materials and Methods for details). Most true complexes have a Combined score above 0.5, in contrast to decoys that score below (Fig. 1C). To quantify AlphaFold-Multimer’s ability to correctly classify true and decoy complexes, we calculated Areas Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) for all common AlphaFold-Multimer intrinsic scores (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4) (26, 28). We also determined the thresholds for which the difference between true positive and false positive rates (TPR−FPR) is at its maximum. In the classification of true and decoy complexes, Combined score, ipTM and IDR pLDDT all show great performance with IDR pLDDT standing out (AUROC =0.91, threshold =42.9, Fig. 1D first column). As Combined scores for complexes in the Fuzzy dataset are the lowest for all true complexes, we specifically assessed how well AlphaFold-Multimer can separate decoys from complexes in the Fuzzy set. We found similar performances in this classification task, with IDR pLDDT being an excellent classifier (AUROC =0.91, threshold =42.4, Fig. 1D second column). Finally, we tested the ability of the different AlphaFold-Multimer scores to separate inaccurate from accurate complex structure predictions (CAPRI classes). We tried multiple scores and found that the Combined score has the best performance in this task (AUROC =0.81, threshold =0.62, SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Overall, these tests revealed that AlphaFold-Multimer predictions of interactions between an IDR and a receptor with a Combined score above 0.62 are not only likely to be true but also accurate.


### AlphaFold-Multimer Structures Are Mostly Consistent with Nuclear Overhauser Effect (NOE)-Derived Distances and Capture Binding Diversity.
  Assessments of prediction qualities with DockQ and CAPRI have limitations, which may explain why the complexes in the Fuzzy and IDRBind datasets are categorized as of lower quality. By design, DockQ and CAPRI metrics take one specific tertiary arrangement of atoms as the ground truth, hence failing to properly assess the quality of structures predicted for dynamic binding modes such as fuzzy complexes (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Therefore, we took additional steps to measure AlphaFold-Multimer’s performance on such structures. Considering that the Fuzzy and a big portion of IDRBind datasets are composed of NMR structures, we decided to analyze violations of NMR restraints, specifically NOE-derived interproton distances. Briefly, after adding hydrogens to the AlphaFold-Multimer structures, we used PdbStat (36) to analyze restraint violations (Materials and Methods). This analysis revealed that the structure ensembles deposited to PDB have a slightly lower median number of violations than the models predicted by AlphaFold-Multimer (Fig. 2C). However, calculating the NOE RMS violations revealed that AlphaFold-Multimer generates structures with overall higher distance deviations. The median NOE RMS violations for AlphaFold-Multimer are around 1.5 Å for both datasets, while the RMS violations are 0.2 Å and 0.3 Å for the NMR models in the Fuzzy and IDRBind datasets, respectively. This said, there are several complexes in both datasets for which violations calculated for deposited NMR models and the different AlphaFold-Multimer predictions are on par.

  Given the fact that fuzzy complexes contain regions that remain highly dynamic following binding, we studied how AlphaFold-Multimer recapitulates these features in its outputs. To this end, we calculated RMSFs and distance variations (DVs) for each sample in the Fuzzy dataset and the AlphaFold-Multimer models (Materials and Methods). The former metric measures per-residue fluctuations, while the latter monitors pair-wise fluctuations within a protein or complex, i.e., how dynamic a region is with respect to other parts of the protein/complex. We treated predicted structures as different conformations of the same underlying protein complex and directly extracted RMSFs and DVs from these conformations. Spearman’s correlation calculated for DVs of the IDR chains in AlphaFold-Multimer predictions and structures in the Fuzzy dataset is high (0.84) (Fig. 2D, Top). However, the correlation is much lower for interchain DVs. The correlations are also low for the IDR’s RMSFs (Fig. 2D, Bottom). A closer look at RMSFs calculated from AlphaFold-Multimer predictions and PDB models revealed that fluctuations in the predictions are generally much smaller in magnitude, i.e., AlphaFold-Multimer predicted structures are more homogeneous and differ less in overall topology, in contrast to the deposited models (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Notably though, AlphaFold-Multimer’s intrinsic scores such as ripTM (the per-residue ipTM score) and PAE display high correlations with RMSFs and DVs (Fig. 2D). In other words, parts of the IDRs with high RMSFs or DVs are assigned low ripTM and high PAE scores. Most importantly, segments of the IDRs that have been identified as dynamic for functional reasons, i.e., those segments that justify the inclusion of these interactions in the Fuzzy dataset, have significantly lower ripTM scores than IDRs from the IDRBind dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These results reveal that IDRs with high structural variability in protein complexes are assigned lower confidence scores by AlphaFold-Multimer, suggesting that AlphaFold-Multimer has an intrinsic knowledge of more dynamic parts of IDR-containing complexes.

  These findings indicate that AlphaFold-Multimer scores may be able to distinguish homogeneous from nonhomogeneous binding. Indeed, pTM is able to discriminate fuzzy complexes from the homogeneous binding modes of the Rg set (AUROC =0.71, threshold =0.8, Fig. 1D third column). Moreover, projection of the predicted structures from the different datasets on the pTM versus ipTM space clearly shows that fuzzy complexes have lower pTM scores than other complexes (Fig. 2E). The ipTM score further allows separating true complexes from the decoys, which have much lower values in this dimension. Thus, projections in the pTM versus ipTM space provide an easy way to visualize how AlphaFold-Multimer separates heterogeneous IDR binding modes from homogeneous ones, as well as decoys from true interactions.


### Determinants of AlphaFold-Multimer Precision in Predicting Structures of IDR-Containing Complexes.
  Given the substantial differences in prediction qualities that we observed across the datasets, we searched for properties that individual datasets are enriched in and correlate with AlphaFold-Multimer’s prediction quality. Consistent with previous results (37), we found that IDR-containing complexes with small interface sizes have significantly lower DockQ scores than those with large interfaces (Fig. 3A, P-value: 9.6 × 10−8, Mann–Whitney U test). However, interface size does not seem to be a key factor contributing to prediction quality differences across the sets we analyzed, because the Fuzzy, IDRBind, and Bret et al. datasets have very similar distributions of interface sizes (Fig. 3D).

  We have shown previously that interfaces involving IDRs are enriched in hydrophobic residues and that the many charged residues present in IDRs play an important role in determining interaction specificity (33). Therefore, we tested how hydrophobicity of the interface and charge content in the IDR and receptor impact AlphaFold-Multimer prediction quality. The most striking effect was observed for interface hydrophobicity. Complexes having interfaces with high and medium hydrophobicity are predicted more accurately, as inferred from DockQ scores, than complexes with interfaces that have a low hydrophobicity (Fig. 3B, P-values: 8.5 × 10−15 and 3.6 × 10−8, Mann–Whitney U test). Consistent with this result, we found that complex interfaces with high content of polar and charged residues are generally of lower prediction quality (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Mirroring the gradual increase in the quality of the prediction made by AlphaFold-Multimer going from the Fuzzy to the Bret et al. dataset (Fig. 1B), hydrophobicity increases, with the exception of the Fuzzy dataset, also in the interface of the complexes in these datasets (Fig. 3E).

  Secondary structure content has also been linked to differences in the quality of AlphaFold2 predictions (25). Similarly, we found that complexes containing IDRs are more accurately predicted when folding upon binding leads to a high α-helical content in the IDR (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). In line with this finding, and most notable in the given context, the lack of secondary structure has a dramatic effect on the quality of the predicted structures. We found that IDRs with low coil contents, when interacting with the receptors, are predicted with significantly higher quality than IDRs that have an intermediate or high coil content after binding (Fig. 3C, P-values: 4.9 × 10−9 and 7.3 × 10−43, Mann–Whitney U test). Interestingly, IDRs in the Fuzzy and IDRBind datasets have the highest coil content of all datasets (Fig. 3D). Taken together, these findings suggest that the interactions of IDRs with high coil content and low hydrophobicity, features often found in segments that interact transiently in a multimodal way (38), may be harder for AlphaFold-Multimer to model accurately.


### AlphaFold-Multimer Is Capable of Identifying the Binding IDR in a Full-Length Protein.
  The benchmarks that we carried out thus far only used the sequences of IDRs as found in PDB structures, and not the entire sequence of the protein the IDRs originate from. To investigate whether AlphaFold-Multimer can simultaneously identify the correct IDR in a protein that binds a specific receptor and predict the complex structure, we substituted IDR sequences from the Rg and Bret et al. datasets with the full-length protein sequences they originate from and used them alongside their cognate receptor sequences for AlphaFold-Multimer predictions. Evaluating the predicted structures according to DockQ revealed a marked decline in prediction quality when compared to the prediction results using the appropriate IDR sequence stretches only (Fig. 4 A and B). This decline in prediction quality is particularly pronounced for the Bret et al. dataset. These results demonstrate that AlphaFold-Multimer’s prediction accuracy is sensitive to the defined boundaries of the interacting partners.

  It is worth noting though that AlphaFold-Multimer is capable of producing correct complex structures for the majority of samples in Rg and Rg+ID sets when using the full-length protein. This result points out that AlphaFold-Multimer is frequently able to pinpoint the IDR in a full-length protein that binds a given receptor. To assess how frequent, we tested different AlphaFold-Multimer metrics for their ability to identify the region (IDR residues) in a full-length protein that binds a given receptor. It is important to note that we tested here the ability to pinpoint the binding region when we pair sequences of proteins that are known to interact somewhere. Of the different metrics that we tested, min-iPAE and minD showed best performances in this prediction task (Materials and Methods). For the Rg dataset, for instance, min-iPAE, which is the minimum PAE value between each residue in the IDR-containing full-length protein and all receptor residues (Materials and Methods), achieves an AUROC of 0.91 (Fig. 4C) and Average Precision (AP) of 0.5 (Fig. 4D). An even higher performance in identifying the IDR residues that are involved in binding a given receptor is achieved when using Distogram data. Distogram head is a module in AlphaFold-Multimer that linearly projects the symmetrized pair representations into 64 bins from 2 Å to 22 Å. This module is trained to guess the true distance between each pair of residues, hence can directly predict protein interfaces when used on two different protein chains. We computed the minimum expected distance score (minD, see Materials and Methods) between each residue in the full-length IDR-containing protein and all residues of the receptor. It turned out that minD is highly accurate (AUROC =0.94 and 0.92, AP =0.61 and =0.59 for the Rg datasets) in identifying the IDR in a protein that binds to a specific receptor (Fig. 4 C and D). Although with lower precision, minD is also performing well in this task for proteins of the Bret et al. dataset. The ability to do so is notable, given the lower number of residues at the interface of these peptide complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) and the lower performance of AlphaFold-Multimer when using full-length proteins for this dataset (Fig. 4 A and B).

  This high performance of the minD metric motivated us to investigate the Distogram head output further. A close inspection of the Distogram head data revealed that multiple peaks are often seen for a given protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), suggesting multiple potential interaction sites. We carried out a systematic peak analysis of normalized minD to identify potential interaction sites and rank them (see Materials and Methods for technical details). Sorting all peaks for each prediction revealed that for 95%, 93%, and 84% of the samples in the Rg, Rg+ID and Bret et al. datasets, one of the top-5 peaks was located within the region that truly harbors the binding site (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, the top peak identified the correct binding region in 77% of the Rg samples, while for only 65.3% of the samples AlphaFold-Multimer also generated a correct structure (Fig. 4A). In SI Appendix, Fig S10. we provide an example of how AlphaFold-Multimer has accurately found the correct binding region within a full-length (≈1,400 amino acids long) protein, while generating a seemingly incorrect complex (DockQ =0.009).

  It has been shown that AlphaFold-Multimer predictions can be used to identify thus far unknown interaction partners (30, 31, 39). To do so, sequences of potential interaction partners are fragmented, guided by domain boundaries, and AlphaFold-Multimer run on all possible pairings. Our results suggest that min-iPAE and minD should help identifying fragments with potential interaction sites, reducing computational costs. The usefulness of these metrics is further increased if they do not identify false positive interaction sites in proteins that do not interact with a receptor. To test for this, we created a balanced negative test set of full-length proteins randomly paired with receptors and calculated the AUROC with the additional negative residues from nonbinders. We observed only a small drop in performance in identifying the correct interaction sites for all three tested datasets (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Moreover, we found that true and nonbinders have similar numbers of minD peaks, but top ranked peaks in true binders have significantly higher scores than those in nonbinders (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).


## Discussion
  The remarkable accuracy of AlphaFold-Multimer in folding protein complexes has opened novel ways to investigate protein–protein interactions. In the realm of IDR-protein interactions, AlphaFold-Multimer has only been applied to a limited set of interactions, specifically those mediated by short linear motifs (30, 31). In this paper, we examined the possibilities for extending the use of AlphaFold-Multimer to a broad spectrum of IDR binding modes, from homogeneous binding modes to the dynamic interactions of fuzzy complexes. Our analysis revealed that AlphaFold-Multimer consistently produces correct structures across the spectrum of IDR complexes with a high success rate of at least 76%. Yet, the quality and reliability of the structures significantly varies for different datasets, such that the high-quality portion is much smaller for fuzzy complexes. However, AlphaFold-Multimer scores are clearly able to separate predictions for true complexes (receptor-IDR pairs that are known interactors) from predictions for decoys (receptors paired with random IDRs not known to interact). Using a simple threshold on the Combined score of about 0.5 achieves an AUROC of over 0.8 in distinguishing decoy interactions from true IDR interactions, independent on whether the interaction of the IDR is homogeneous or fuzzy. Furthermore, a Combined score of 0.62 sets apart a large fraction of the high accuracy predictions from those with lower quality. It is important to note that the prediction quality is likely to improve when using some of the pipelines recently introduced to improve the prediction quality of AlphaFold-Multimer (31, 40).

  A key aspect of this study is the assessment of AlphaFold-Multimer’s performance on complexes with high heterogeneity. Specifically, we assessed to which extent multiple models predicted by AlphaFold-Multimer recapitulate the structural heterogeneity present among the NMR models of fuzzy complexes. Notably, we found high correlations between the RMSF and DV calculated for fuzzy complexes and the intrinsic AlphaFold-Multimer metrics of ripTM and PAE (note that these metrics are extracted from only the top-rank predictions). Thus, AlphaFold-Multimer assigns to parts of IDRs that are structurally very diverse in complex metrics indicating low confidence in prediction accuracy. One caveat to consider is that some parts of the IDRs in the fuzzy complexes may be more heterogeneous because of missing or more ambiguous restraints, and not an inherent and functionally important dynamic nature. Therefore, we specifically analyzed parts of the IDRs with a “fuzzy” annotation, those parts with experimental evidence for dynamic behavior and functional relevance. We found that these parts have significantly lower ripTM scores than the IDRs of the IDRBind dataset. Taken together, these findings suggest that running AlphaFold-Multimer in its default settings is enough to get an accurate identification of segments in IDRs that engage in more heterogeneous interactions. Importantly, this identification strategy should only be applied under the condition that the overall complex prediction is considered accurate, meaning that the Combined score is higher than 0.62 (see above). In that case, a pTM threshold of 0.8 could be used for the discrimination of fuzzy from homogeneous binding modes.

  Despite the ability to identify more heterogeneous IDR interactions, several findings demonstrate that the magnitude of heterogeneity seen in NMR models is not fully captured. AlphaFold-Multimer produces structures with similar numbers of violations than the NMR models, but NOE violations are overall larger. Models predicted with the standard protocol are not as diverse as those deposited in the PDB. These findings are consistent with recent studies showing that AlphaFold2 produces structures that are in good agreement with NMR data of unbound proteins but that it finds challenges in predicting heterogeneous structural ensembles accurately (41–44). The fact that AlphaFold-Multimer struggles more with interactions that are more heterogeneous may not be so surprising given that more homogeneous, high-affinity interactions dominate in the PDB and, therefore bias training of AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold-Multimer. Notably though for our analysis, NOE violations are similarly high for the predictions of complexes in the IDRBind set, which have significantly lower heterogeneity. It needs to be seen whether alternative methods (40, 45, 46) that produce a larger number of diverse conformations predict the magnitude of structural fluctuations with even higher accuracy.

  In order to reveal why certain IDR complexes are more challenging to predict, we investigated which sequence and structure features correlate with poorer prediction performance. We found that high coil content and reduced hydrophobicity at the interfaces are likely impacting prediction quality for certain complexes involving IDRs. Of particular interest in the context of IDR-mediated interactions is our finding that reduced hydrophobicity at the interfaces is associated with lower prediction quality. Although segments of IDRs that are directly involved in binding are enriched in hydrophobic residues, which contribute significantly to binding affinity (33), it has been shown that IDR interfaces are, overall, less hydrophobic than interfaces between globular proteins (32). Moreover, polar interactions play an important role in establishing specificity in IDR-mediated interactions (33), and are therefore an abundant element of their interaction interfaces. It may well be that AlphaFold-Multimer struggles more with interactions that are driven primarily by electrostatic interactions, but more research is required to further substantiate this hypothesis.

  The high prediction success of AlphaFold-Multimer on IDR-mediated complexes may open avenues to predict new IDR interactions. However, such task requires prediction accuracy in the context of full-length proteins. We tested the ability of AlphaFold-Multimer to predict the structure of IDR-mediated complexes (Rg dataset) in the case where the full-length protein, from which the IDR originates, is paired with the correct receptor, i.e., a receptor known to bind the provided protein somewhere. Consistent with previous results (30, 31), we found that the overall prediction accuracy drops. However, over 60% of predicted complex structures are still classified as accurate, which is probably due to the fact that AlphaFold-Multimer signals the correct interaction sites through its Distogram head with great accuracy. Indeed, the introduced minD score, which is extracted from the Distogram head, identifies the interaction sites of receptors within disordered regions of full-length proteins with high accuracy. A close inspection of minD scores revealed that they peak at multiple places along the protein sequence, suggesting alternative binding sites. We found that the correct binding site can be found in the top-5 peaks for over 80% of all samples.

  Previously, it has been shown that new protein interactions can be identified with the help of AlphaFold-Multimer. Specifically, the sequences of potential interaction partners are fragmented, guided by domain boundaries, and AlphaFold-Multimer run on all possible pairings (30, 31, 39). However, testing all possible combinations to discover interactions requires extensive computational resources. Using minD to identify fragment location may constitute an alternative method that circumvents the need for the brute scanning of fragments, unless minD identifies many false-positive interaction sites in nonbinders of a given receptor domain. However, our analyses using balanced negative sets of nonbinders do not suggest that. One can certainly not exclude the possibility of the existence of some sequences in an entire proteome that are not supposed to bind a given receptor domain for which minD identifies many false interaction sites, but analyses with larger negatives sets are required to get an estimate of their abundance. In any case, interaction identification via AlphaFold-Multimer relies on the correct distinction between true and false complexes using metrics like the Combined score (see above). It has been highlighted that using these metrics in the fragmentation approach results in an estimated false discovery rate (FDR of 20% for AFM-based interaction identifications (30). Based on our results, one expects that the FDR for heterogeneous complexes involving IDRs is slightly higher than for more homogeneous complexes. Large numbers of fragments that need to be screened to identify novel interactions will lead to many falsely identified ones with these FDR levels. However, using minD may reduce the number of false positives because fewer fragments need to be tested, keeping prediction specificity at a higher level. More research is required to establish the efficiency gains made when using minD and to investigate whether minD can be combined with the ipTM or other scores to reduce FDRs.


## Materials and Methods

### Curating a Blind Dataset Based on Radius of Gyration (Rg).
  To compile a nonredundant set of IDR-protein dimers, we followed a modified version of the pipeline introduced in ref. 33. The methodology exploits the fact that IDRs bury more solvent-accessible surface area relative to their length than structured proteins when they interact with partners. In other words, IDRs often adopt expanded conformations in the complex structure, which can be used for their identification. We and others have previously shown that the radius of gyration (Rg) of protein chains provides a means to exploit this idea and have successfully used it in the identification of protein interactions involving IDRs (33, 47). Specifically, Rg/N can be used as an effective classifier separating classical complexes between folded domains from complexes that contain a MoRF. Flory has shown that for heteropolymers Rg=R0Nv, where the scaling factor v is 0.5 for IDRs and 0.33 for globular domains. We tested Rg/Nv with different scaling factors v in the classification. Out of all the scaling factors that we tested, v of 1 resulted in a classifier that had the highest AUC and a Rg/N<0.26 provided the highest Matthew’s correlation coefficient. Following our previous procedures, we first sent a request to the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) PDB Data Application Programming Interface (API) (48), asking for PDB IDs of the structures released after 2018 April 30, having the experimental resolution of 3.5 Å and lower (or be an NMR structure), and comprising at least 2 chains. Structures having nonprotein chains (DNA or RNA) were omitted. Moreover, structures with all chains shorter than 20 residues were omitted. 43,725 PDB IDs were found at this stage. After downloading the structure files, a series of filters were applied to find IDR-protein interactions. First, the IDR chain should possess an extended form, meaning that it should have a high radius of gyration (Rg) relative to its length. A Rg/N≥0.26 was used to identify the IDR chain. On the other hand, there should be a globular protein (Rg/N<0.26) in contact with the IDR chain. We also made sure that the two chains are in contact by validating whether they have any two heavy atoms in 5 Å proximity. Also, IDR and receptor chains should be longer than 20 and 70 residues, respectively. Note that the IDR chain should have only one receptor and the receptor chain should be in contact with only one IDR chain. As noted in the original pipeline, omitting disulfide-rich structures is crucial as these often fall into the false-positives of the Rg/N classifier. Therefore, any structure with two or more disulfide bonds were ignored. We ended up with 1,329 interactions after these filters.

  To avoid any further overlap with the training set of AlphaFold-Multimer, MMSeqs2 (49) was used to search for sequence similarities between the dataset and PDB SEQRES sequences released on or before 2018 April 30. 30% sequence similarity with 80% coverage was set for the MMSeqs2 search pipeline. Interactions already available in AlphaFold-Multimer’s training set were dropped then, resulting in 440 complexes. Finally, to improve the quality of the dataset, we clustered the IDR sequences and the receptor sequences separately using MMSeqs2 (30% sequence similarity with 80% coverage) and chose one sample per cluster: the sample with the best resolution, if there was at least one structure with resolution information recorded in the cluster. Otherwise, we chose the most recently released structure from the cluster. Together, these steps led to 210 IDR-receptor complexes in the Rg set. This dataset and its prediction results are provided in Dataset S1.


### IDRBind Dataset.
  PDB IDs of the structures previously used to train an IDR interface predictor (32) were obtained from the supplementary files of the study. 84 structures were originally used in the IDRBind study, 73 of which have their IDR chains interacting with only one receptor chain. We used these 73 complexes in our study. This dataset and its prediction results are provided in Dataset S2.


### Fuzzy Dataset.
  In order to create a dataset of fuzzy complexes, we first downloaded the entire FuzDB (34) directory for which at least one NMR experiment is available in the PDB. FuzDB is a manually curated database of fuzzy complexes. For all complexes included, certain regions must have experimental evidence for dynamics in the bound state as well as evidence that these structurally heterogeneous regions are critical for activity. Out of 296 structures, 37 interactions were extracted by a series of filters: The structure had to have at least 2 models in it; the IDR and receptor chains had to be longer (or equal) to 15 and 60 residues, respectively. For each complex, the RMSD of the receptor in all models with respect to the receptor in the first model was calculated, and interactions with a mean RMSD above 3.5 Å were dropped. This dataset and its prediction results are provided in Dataset S3.


### Bret et al. Dataset.
  We downloaded the supplementary data of 42 dimeric complexes used in the previous study by Bret et al. (31). These complexes contain receptors interacting with short linear motifs. As the authors produced 25 predictions per experiment setting with 5 different random seeds, we extracted the results of the first seed (iteration = 1). This way, we ensured a valid comparison with our results.


### Decoys Dataset.
  We constructed a decoy dataset as a control set. To create this set, we paired receptors from the Rg dataset with fragments of intrinsically disordered regions from the same protein as the cognate IDR binding partner. We used UniProt to extract the full-length sequence from which the IDR originates (50), then used a disorder predictor to identify IDRs on the entire protein. We then divided each UniProt sequence into disordered regions and split each region using a sliding window of the size of the cognate binding IDR. Decoy fragments are defined as windows containing no part of the original dataset fragment. To predict the structure of the decoy complexes, we fed sequences of the receptor and decoy fragments to AlphaFold-Multimer resulting in 73 decoy complexes for the dataset.


### Running AlphaFold-Multimer.
  We used ColabFold’s implementation of AlphaFold-Multimer (51), which is shown to have a performance similar to the original implementation of AlphaFold-Multimer while using MMSeqs2 to search genomic databases orders of magnitude faster. ColabFold v1.5.2 is grabbed with model_type set to alphafold_multimer_v2, which uses the parameter weights of AlphaFold-Multimer v2.2. The rest of the configurations are set to its default. Therefore, num_recycles is equal to 3 and num_seeds is 1.


### Folding IDR Fragments.
  We extracted SEQRES from the PDBs and submitted the gained sequences of IDR and receptor chain sequences to AlphaFold-Multimer. Thus, only sequence parts used in the experimental determination of the structures were used for prediction.


### Folding Full-Length Proteins.
  For the experiments carried out on full-length proteins, we first mapped each IDR fragment to its UniProt parent protein by probing UniProt’s API (50). We then used AlphaFold-Multimer to fold the full-length protein and its corresponding receptor domain (only the domain sequence) alongside each other.


### Rapid Intrinsic Disorder Analysis Online (RIDAO).
  We utilized the sequence-based disorder predictor RIDAO to determine a disorder score for each dataset in our study. RIDAO combines six established disorder predictors into a unified platform (52). To calculate a disorder score for each protein fragment, we first used RIDAO to generate a per-residue score for the entire protein containing the fragment. We then averaged the scores of the fragment’s residues to obtain the final disorder score for that fragment.


### IUPred3.
  We used the sequence-based disordered predictor IUPred3 on all the studied datasets. This method computes a per-residue score for being part of a disordered region (53).


### DockQ and CAPRI.
  DockQ is widely used in the field to assess the quality of molecular docking (35). DockQ is the average of three metrics:DockQFnat,LRMS,iRMS,d1,d2=13Fnat+f(LRMS,d1)+f(iRMS,d2),

  where d1= 8.5 Å, d2= 1.5 Å, and f is the inverse square scaling function defined asf(r,d)=11+rd2.

  To calculate DockQ, we first aligned the prediction and the ground truth structures and did the calculations only on the aligned fragments.

  CAPRI classes are defined based on DockQ scores: DockQ ≥0.8 means high quality, DockQ ≥0.49 means medium quality, DockQ ≥0.23 is of acceptable quality, and values lower than 0.23 are considered as incorrect predictions.


### PAE and min-iPAE.
  All AlphaFold-Multimer predictive scores were obtained from the JSON files containing scores that ColabFold provides alongside the PDB formatted structures. PAEi,j is the expected distance between the predicted and true coordinates of Cα atom in residue j, when both predicted and true structures are aligned on residue i (21). Note that PAEi,j≠PAEj,i. For multichain predictions, PAEi,j is called “inter-PAE” if residues i and j come from different chains. Suppose we have a dimeric predicted structure with chains A and B, min-iPAE(j) (minimum inter-PAE) for residue j from chain A is defined as[1]min-iPAE(j)= mini∈BPAE(i,j).


### pLDDT, pTM, ipTM, and ripTM.
  pLDDT is a per-residue metric provided by AlphaFold-Multimer to estimate lDDT-Cα (54). pTM is a predicition of TM score (21). ipTM is a modified version of pTM which predicts TM score for all chains except the chain that the predicted and ground truth structures are aligned on. This metric measures the quality and position of chains with respect to each other. Combined score is defined as a weighted average of global and interface pTMs (0.2·pTM+0.8·ipTM) (27). We used the Combined score to select the top-ranked predictions for most analyses. Each row of the PAE matrix is consumed to derive a per-residue ipTM score. We call this ripTM. Note that[2]ipTM= maxj  ripTM(j).


### Distogram Distribution and minD.
  One part of the objectives for AlphaFold-Multimer during training is to predict the true distances between pairs of residues. This is achieved by linearly transforming the pair representation of each pair of residues into 64 equal-width bins from 2 Å to 22 Å, thus producing a distribution over pairwise residue distances. Here, the distance is defined as the distance between Cβ atoms (Cα for glycine) in the ground truth structure. This linear Distogram head is trained alongside other AlphaFold-Multimer modules (21). Then, we can derive an “expected” distance for each pair of residues:[3]E[Dij]=∑b=164pijbcb,

  where pijb is the probability derived by applying softmax on the logits for residue pair (i,j) produced by the Distogram head. cb is the center of bin b. Then, minD(j) is the minimum expected distance of residue j from chain A with any residue of other chains, considering a multichain structure. Technically,[4]minD(j)= mini∉A E[Dij].

  The code to calculate minD(j) is available at https://github.com/alirezaomidi/AFM-IDR (65).


### Analysis of minD for Binding-Site Prediction.
  We proposed minD as a per-residue measurement of binding sites within a full-length protein. The experiment we carried out to measure its capability on such a task is as follows. We first chose Rg, Rg+ID, and Bret et al. datasets for this experiment, as they only contain post-training-cutoff-date samples. Then, we mapped the IDR chains in these datasets to their UniProt parent protein and extracted the full-length sequences (Materials and Methods). This procedure results in sets of two-chain complexes of full-length proteins alongside their corresponding receptor domains. In each sample, the residues residing in the original ID region are marked as positive and the rest of the residues are marked as negative in ground-truth. To test whether minD also works when nonbinder proteins are included as negatives, we also paired each receptor with a random full-length protein from the same dataset. All residues in all full-length proteins of this negative set are marked as negative. We then used AlphaFold-Multimer to fold all these pairs, whether true or randomly paired and measured the capacity of minD in classifying positive and negative residues leveraging ROC and precision–recall curves.


### Analysis of NOE-Derived Distance Violations.
  We obtained the NMR restraints files from PDB (48) and used PdbStat for our NOE-derived distance violation analysis (36). We analyzed both the NMR and AlphaFold-Multimer structures. To perform the analysis, we used NMR ensembles as they were provided in the PDB. On the other hand, we added hydrogens to AlphaFold-Multimer structures as these atoms are not natively predicted and the analysis involves interproton distances. To add them, we used an open-source Python library called “hydride” which uses the known bond lengths and angles to add hydrogens to models (55).


### RMSF and DV.
  As the RMSF values depend on the superimposition used, we employed two different alignment strategies. One where we aligned all models on the first NMR model using the entire structure. In the other approach, we only aligned the receptor on the first NMR model and calculated the RMSF on the IDR chain.

  The DV was calculated by computing the Cα–Cα distances in all models and then taking a SD of the distances between each pair of Cα atoms. The unit for DV is Å.


### Hydrophobicity.
  We used the Kyte–Doolittle scale to assign hydrophobicities to each amino acid (56). Interface hydrophobicity was calculated as the average hydrophobicity values over the interface regions.


### Peak Analysis.
  Finding local maxima (peaks) of minD was done by a simple comparison of neighboring values through the use of the scipy.signal.find_peaks function. Specifically, we first normalized minD to map its range from [2,22] to [0,1]:[5]minDnorm(j)=1−minD(j)−220

  Then, the scipy.signal.find_peaks function was called with prominence = 0.02 (to avoid small and invalid peaks) and distance = 30 to have no more than one peak in a 30 residues window.


### Other Software Suites Used.
  We extensively used BioPython, NumPy, SciPy, pandas, Matplotlib, and seaborn (57–62) to develop the code and plot the figures for this work. Protein visualizations were done in PyMOL (63). Statannotations was used to compute the statistical test and annotations on the figures with the specific test of two-sided Mann–Whitney U test (64).
