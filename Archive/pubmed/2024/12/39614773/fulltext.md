# The Myotubularin Related Proteins and the Untapped Interaction Potential of Their Disordered C‐Terminal Regions


## ABSTRACT
  Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) of proteins remain understudied with enigmatic sequence features relevant to their functions. Members of the myotubularin‐related protein (MTMR) family contain uncharacterized IDRs. After decades of research on their phosphatase activity, recent work on the C‐terminal IDRs of MTMR7 revealed new interactions and important new functions beyond the phosphatase function. Here we take a broader look at the C‐terminal domains (CTDs) of 14 human MTMRs and use bioinformatic tools and biophysical methods to ask which other functions may be probable in this protein family. The predictions show that the CTDs are disordered and carry short linear motifs (SLiMs) important for targeting of MTMRs to defined subcellular compartments and implicating them in signaling, phase separation, interaction with diverse proteins, including transcription factors and are of relevance for cancer research and neuroscience. We also present experimental methods to study the CTDs and use them to characterize the coiled coil (CC) domains of MTMR7 and MTMR9. We show homo‐ and hetero‐oligomerization with preference for MTMR7‐CC to form dimers, while MTMR9‐CC forms trimers. We relate the results to sequence features and make predictions for the structural landscape of other MTMRs. Our work gives a broad insight into the so far unrecognized features and SLiMs in MTMR‐CTDs, and provides the basis for more in‐depth experimental research on this diverse protein family and understudied IDRs in proteins in general.


## Introduction
  Myotubularin‐related proteins are a large protein family that is named after myotubular myopathy (MTM1) and their original member myotubularin, also called MTM1 [1]. The family consists of 16 members named MTM1, and MTMR1 up to MTMR15, collectively referred to as MTMRs [2, 3, 4]. MTMRs are ubiquitously expressed in the human body, notably in the peripheral nervous system and in the gastrointestinal tract, inhabiting diverse locations within the cell. While MTMR4 and MTMR7 colocalize with endosomes, MTMR6 and MTMR8 are found at the nuclear envelope, and MTMR2 inside the nucleus [5, 6]. MTMRs are typically associated with the plasma membrane or intracellular membranes and exert their function at these specific subcellular locations, but they are also functional in the cytosol [7, 8, 9]. Despite their shared family tree (Figure S2), MTMRs are very diverse both in length and in domain composition; the common denominator being a phosphatase and a pleckstrin homology‐glucosyltransferase, rab‐like GTPase activator and myotubularins (PH‐GRAM) domain [2, 10], here referred to as the MTMR core domains.

  The main function of MTMRs has been described to be the dephosphorylation of phosphoinositides (PIs) like phosphatidylinositol‐3‐monophosphate (PtdIns3P) and phosphatidylinositol‐3,5‐biphosphate (PtdIns(3,5)P2) [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The substrates PtdIns3P and PtdIns(3,5)P2 are required for selective sorting of target proteins such as proteins harboring Fab1/YOTB/Vac1p/EEA1 (FYVE) or PH domains [16, 17, 18], implicating MTMRs in the regulation of protein trafficking. Also proteins whose membrane localization relies on a change in cellular PI concentration have been shown to be regulated by MTMRs, like Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) [19]. MTMR4 was also shown to dephosphorylate proteins involved in signaling processes [20, 21].

  MTMRs are of medical interest. Loss of MTMR2 or MTMR13 is connected to the development of Charcot–Marie‐Tooth neuropathy [22, 23, 24, 25], while absence of MTMR7 has been shown to be an important factor for malignant progression of colorectal cancers [6, 9, 26]. Moreover, in the prevention of for example, cardiovascular diseases and colorectal cancer, MTMRs have been found to be promising drug targets [3, 6, 26, 27].

  Despite the potential medical relevance of this large protein family, biophysical studies elucidating the structure and function of MTMRs are still limited [2]. The only published three‐dimensional structures of human MTMRs are of the globular core domains involved in PI binding and dephosphorylation, which are shared between MTM1 and MTMR1‐13 [2]. These structures show that the core domains are conserved, and AlphaFold3 (AF3) predictions of the 14 core domains of MTM1 and MTMR1‐13 support this finding (Figure 1A). The majority of the eight available crystal structures of human MTMRs are of MTMR2 (pdb codes: 1lw3, 1zvr, 1zsq, 1m7r, 5gnh), and these have been used to understand the phosphatase activity in molecular detail [14, 28]. There is a single crystal structure of the core domains each of MTMR1 (pdb code: 5c16) [29] and MTMR8 (phosphatase domain only, pdb code: 4y7i) [30], published nearly a decade after the MTMR2 structures, and one structure of the core domains MTMR6, on‐hold since 2011 (pdb: 2yf0).

  Not all MTMRs have an active phosphatase domain required for the dephosphorylation of lipid substrates. Therefore, MTMRs are categorized as either catalytically active or inactive: MTM1, MTMR1–4, MTMR6–8, and MTMR14 are catalytically active, while MTMR5, MTMR9–MTMR13, and MTMR15 are catalytically inactive [2, 3]. The catalytic activity of active MTMRs is enhanced by homo‐ and heterodimerization with other MTMRs—including catalytically inactive ones—facilitated via a coiled coil (CC) domain located in the C‐terminal tail of all MTMRs, except for MTMR10, which lacks a CC domain (Figure 1B,C). The CC domains are usually located within the first 100 residues C‐terminal of the core domains, regardless of the overall length of the protein (Figure 1B). Thus, for example, active MTMR7 heterodimerizes with catalytically inactive MTMR9, which in itself can homodimerize [5, 7] (Figure 1D). The complexes form parallel dimers, and dimerization may enhance membrane binding by providing a second PH‐GRAM domain for PI binding [8]. In addition, the CC domains can direct MTMRs to specific targets, regulated by the cellular PI‐levels. This was shown for MTMR6, which takes part in CC interactions with the K‐channel KCa3.1 to dephosphorylate PIs in its proximity [31]. Biophysical data characterizing MTMR‐CC domains are rare. Recent data show that the CC domain of MTMR7 is disordered in its monomeric form, which may explain why these domains have eluded structural characterization with methods like crystallography [2, 6].

  Beyond the core and CC domains, MTMRs have N‐ and C‐terminal domains or tails (NTDs and CTDs) of varying lengths and thought to be largely disordered [2]. All MTMRs have a CTD, but they vary greatly in length: The average length is 204 ± 184 residues, with the longest CTD being 620 residues (MTMR4) and the shortest 49 residues (MTMR9). Some MTMRs (MTMR3‐5 and MTMR13) contain additional folded domains in their CTDs, such as FYVE and PH domains, which also bind PIs (Figure 1B) [2, 16, 17, 18]. The only structure of any MTMR that is not of the core domains, is a solution structure of the PH domain located at the very C‐terminus of murine MTMR5/Sbf1, on hold since 2004 (pdb code: 1v5u). As the only members, MTMR5 and MTMR13 also have prominent NTDs, with lengths of more than 800 residues [2], containing folded DENN domains, which function as guanine nucleotide exchange factors for RAB GTPases [32, 33, 34].

  Thus, MTMRs are structurally diverse. Therefore, relating the function of MTMRs only to how they contribute to dephosphorylation of PIs, seems reductive. The DENN domains of MTMR5 and MTMR13 already suggest a broader functionality of MTMRs, and the emerging understanding of the functions of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in protein–protein interactions [35] makes a functional relevance of the MTMR‐IDRs very likely. At least the CC domain of MTMR7 has been shown to have functions beyond facilitating dimerization with MTMR9 for enhanced dephosphorylation. Through direct protein–protein interactions, MTMR7 was shown to activate the nuclear transcription factor peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐gamma (PPARγ) [9] as well as to inactivate RAS proteins in colorectal cancer cells [6]. This contrasts the MTMR6 interaction with and inhibition of KCa3.1 [31]. While MTMR6 is guided to a specific membrane location to dephosphorylate PIs by its binding to KCa3.1, the new functions of MTMR7 with non‐MTMR‐partners are independent of the dephosphorylation activity [6, 9]. The interaction sites of MTMR7 for K‐RAS are bivalent and the interaction areas 1 (IA1) and 2 (IA2) shown to lie in and around the CC domain [6] (Figure 1E), whose main function was thought to be well understood [2, 7]. These novel interaction partners of MTMR7 and the deeper understanding of the corresponding functional mechanisms was illuminated through experimental work and go beyond what could be currently predicted by freely available computational tools like for example AF3. This conclusion is illustrated in the AF3‐predicted complexes of MTMR7‐CC, where a helix is predicted in the entire length despite the CC domain of MTMR7 being disordered outside of CC interactions (Figure 1F) [6, 36]. Thus, current predicted structural models do not correlate with experimental data and do not give meaningful insights into the structures of MTMR CC domains and their complexes.

  These recent findings, showing functions of MTMRs beyond lipid dephosphorylation, suggest an untapped functional potential in the IDRs of MTMRs and led us to raise the question of which other discoveries lie hidden in the uncharacterized and diverse CC domains and the IDRs of MTMRs (Figure 1B). To address this question, we have performed bioinformatical analyses of the MTMR family focusing on their CTDs, and biophysical and biochemical experiments on MTMR7 and MTMR9. We have compared and characterized the sequential grammar and properties of the CC domains of MTM1 and MTMR1‐13 in detail and predicted sites for interactions and post‐translational modifications (PTMs). In addition, we provide the first biophysical and biochemical data characterizing the structures and interactions of the CC domains of MTMR7 and MTMR9 (MTMR7‐CC and MTMR9‐CC). With the aim to inspire a deeper understanding of the IDRs of the large and diverse MTMR family, we cross‐compared the biophysical and bioinformatic data, thereby revealing new directions for more experimental research that may promote further insight into this intriguing protein family.


## Material and Methods

### SLiM Predictions Using the ELM Resource
  MTMR‐CTD sequences were submitted to the ELM server (May 2024), specialized to predict eukaryotic linear motifs [37]. Cell compartments where specified based on UniProt [38] predicted localization (Table 1), species was Homo sapiens, and motif probability cutoff was set to 100. Only SLiMs were included, that were suitable for the full‐length protein, for example, excluded if the SLiM required a free N‐terminus. UniProt entry numbers were Q13496 (MTM1), Q13613 (MTMR1), Q13614 (MTMR2), Q13615 (MTMR3), Q9NYA4 (MTMR4), O95248 (MTMR5), Q9Y217 (MTMR6), Q9Y216 (MTMR7), Q96EF0 (MTMR8), Q96QG7 (MTMR9), Q9NXD2 (MTMR10), A4FU01 (MTMR11), Q9C0I1 (MTMR12), Q86WG5 (MTMR13).


### Computational Protein Models
  Protein models were generated using AF2 and ‐3 [39, 40] and analyzed in PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 2.4.1 Schrödinger LLC).


### Protein Production
  Human MTMR7‐CC (residues 514–554) and MTMR9‐CC (residues 502–549) cDNA was cloned into modified pET24a vectors, encoding an N‐terminal hexahistidine‐ and SUMO‐tag (H6‐SUMO). A tryptophan at position 517 of MTMR7‐CC was added for quantification, as described [6]. Both proteins, stable isotope labeled with 15N and unlabelled, were produced in  E. coli  BL21(‐DE3) cells (New England BioLabs, Frankfurt, Germany) and purified as described [6].


### Peptides
  The MTMR7‐CC used for cross‐linking experiments corresponded to the CC domain of human MTMR7 (aa 521–550; SwissProt ID: Q9Y216.3: LMAVKEETQQLEEELEALEERLEKIQKVQL). The peptide was synthesized with a myristidic acid at the N‐terminus and an amide at the C‐terminus at ETH Zürich, Switzerland as published [6, 9].


### Circular Dichroism Spectropolarimetry
  Far‐UV circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed on a Jasco‐J‐815 suited with a Peltier controlled cuvette holder set to 10°C recording ellipticity between 260 and 195 nm (scan speed: 10 nm/s), with a data pitch of 0.5 nm and a digital integration time of 2 s. The light path length was 0.1 cm. Each spectrum is an accumulation of five scans. Only measurements at a diode high tension voltage below 600 V were included, and identical settings were used to record a spectrum of the buffer, which was then subtracted from the protein spectrum. The freeze‐dried peptides were dissolved in PBS buffer, pH 7.3, 137 mM NaF at a concentration of 28 or 40 μM. The ellipticity was converted to mean residue weight ellipticity (MRE, [θ]MRE) with Equation (1). (1) θMRE=MWn−1mdeg10 c d where c is the concentration (g/L), n the number of residues, d the path length (cm), and MW the molecular weight (Da).

  To calculate the percentage of helicity, Equation (2) was used [41]. (2) %Helicity=100θ222−40,0001−2.5n where [θ]222 is the MRE at 222 nm and n, the number of peptide bonds.


### Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy
  Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) samples containing 40–160 μM 15N‐MTMR7‐CC or 15N‐MTMR9‐CC in the same buffer as the CD samples were supplemented with 10% D2O (v/v), and 0.7 mM 4,4‐dimethyl‐4‐silapentane‐1‐sulfonic acid (DSS) as chemical shift reference. For interaction studies between MTMR7‐CC and MTMR9‐CC, non‐isotope‐labeled MTMR9‐CC was added to 40 μM of 15N‐MTMR7‐CC, first at equimolar ratios and then with an MTMR9‐excess at 100 μM. All NMR spectra were acquired at 283 K on a Bruker AVANCE III 750‐MHz (1H) spectrometer equipped with a cryogenic probe. Proton chemical shifts were internally referenced to DSS at 0.00 ppm and 15N according to its gyromagnetic ratio. Free induction decays were transformed and visualized in NMRPipe [42] or Topspin (Bruker Biospin), and subsequently analyzed using the CcpNmr Analysis software [43].


### Multiple Sequence Alignment
  All data on the protein sequences were downloaded from UniProt [38]. The sequences of the protein family were aligned to identify conserved regions and residues. Using the Clustal Omega [44] alignment algorithm, available on the EMBL‐EBI webserver [www.ebi.ac.uk] [45]. The resulting multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was visualized using the r package ggmsa [46]. For comparison of MTMR protein composition, the average amino acid composition was calculated using all human proteins from the UniProt database. The database included 20 435 reviewed sequences from Swiss‐Prot and 183 987 unreviewed from TrEMBL, with both their canonical and isoform sequence [38]. The average compositions were calculated by finding the average of each of the amino acids in all proteins from the database.


### Phylogenetic Tree
  The phylogenetic tree of the CTDs was made using the output from Clustal Omega [44] MSA of the CTD regions. The tree was visualized using BioPython [47].


### Disorder Prediction
  Disorder was predicted with two approaches. The disordered regions were predicted using IUPred2A [48] as well as AF2 [40]. From the AlphaFold structures, the model confidence (pLDDT) was used as an indication of accurately predicted protein structure, as shown useful in [49]. The model confidence was assigned with difference thresholds very high (pLDDT > 90), confident (90 > pLDDT > 70), low (70 > pLDDT > 50) and very low (pLDDT < 50).


### Phosphorylation Sites
  Experimentally determined phosphorylation sites in MTMRs were downloaded from PhosphoSitePlus webserver [www.phosphosite.org] [50].


## Results

### The CTDs of MTMRs Are Diverse but Can Be Grouped by Similarities
  The CTDs of MTMRs are very diverse, but not equally different from each other (Figure 1B and Figure S1). From the length of the CTDs and their domain composition, the catalytically active MTMR3 and ‐4 as well as the catalytically inactive MTMR5 and ‐13 form closely related protein pairs, respectively. Based on these similarities, and a phylogenetic tree (Figure S2), we divided the MTMRs into four groups, numbered G1 to G4 in order of their average CTD length. G1 contains the MTMRs with the shortest and G4 those with the longest CTDs. For simplicity, only one group representative is shown in Figure 2, (see Figures S3–S6 for all group members).

  G1 comprises MTM1, MTMR1, and MTMR2, which are all catalytically active with a CTD of 50–60 residues, of which the CC makes up about 50% of the CTD‐length, on average 33 ± 2 residues long (Table 1). The most C‐terminal third of the CTDs is strongly enriched in serines, threonines, and valines. The average G1‐member contains 33% ± 11% serine, 11% ± 2% threonine, and 13% ± 4% valine. MTM1 and MTMR2 are additionally enriched in prolines (23% and 10% respectively). The average human protein contains only 5%–6% of these residue types (Figure 2E and Table 1). G1 members are therefore catalytically active with short CTDs carrying a high density of potential serine/threonine phosphorylation sites at their C‐terminus [51] (Figure 2A and Figures S1 and S3).

  G2 is the largest and most diverse group, containing three catalytically active MTMRs (MTMR6‐8) and four catalytically inactive MTMRs (MTMR9‐12). On average, their CTDs are over 100 residues long. MTMR9, with an only 49‐residue long CTD, lacks the enrichment in phosphorylation sites at its C‐terminus that would otherwise place it in G1. The longest members of this group are MTMR7 and MTMR8 with 161 and 200 residues‐long CTD, respectively (Table 1). For G2, the CC domain is on average 33 ± 9 residues long, similar to G1, and follows right after the core domains at the very N‐terminal end of the CTD (Table 1). AF3 shows the remainder of the CTDs to be disordered (Figures S1 and S4), while the disorder predictor IUPRED [52], shows lower scores for large parts of the CTDs of MTMR8 and MTMR10, hinting at a potential to form stable structures (Figures S1 and S4). MTMR7 is shown as the representative of this group in Figure 2B. Thus, G2 members have on average longer CTDs (> 100 residues), which are mostly disordered with no folded domains or other shared amino acid patterns in the CTDs, aside from the CC domain, and are otherwise very diverse.

  The catalytically inactive MTMR5 and MTMR13 are the only members of G3 (Figure 2C and Figures S1 and S5). Both contain a folded PH domain at the very C‐terminus of their CTDs, which are 272 and 266 residues long, respectively (Table 1). The CC domains of this group are short (15 residues) and hardly predicted to be helical by AF3. The rest of the CTD is predicted to be largely disordered by IUPRED [52]. They heterodimerize exclusively with MTMR2 [5]. Thus, G3 members are catalytically inactive MTMRs with long disordered CTDs and short CC domains that bind to the CC domain of MTMR2. They contain a folded PH domain at the C‐terminal end.

  G4 contains the MTMRs with the by far longest CTDs. For MTMR3 and 4, the CTDs are 615 and 620 residues long, respectively, and contain a folded FYVE domain at their very C‐terminus (Figure 2D and Figures S1 and S6). They have long sequences with high IUPRED scores [52], indicating disorder, and they have on average CC domains of 40 residues, located the most distant from the core domains among all MTMRs, almost 450 residues away in sequence space (Table 1). This makes MTMR3 and 4 the only MTMRs where the closest folded domains to the CC domain are not the core domains, but the C‐terminal FYVE domains. G4 contains therefore the MTMRs with the most different but most closely related CTDs of all.

  In conclusion, depending on length, amino acid composition, and architecture of their CTDs (Table 1), the MTMRs can be divided into four structurally distinct groups G1 to G4. Common to all MTMRs are a high degree of structural disorder interspacing the core domains, CC domains and other folded domains. None of the predicted disorder has yet been structurally or functionally explored.


### Potential for Interactions
  IDRs often interact via short linear motifs (SLiMs), which are between three and 15 residues long and, while often folding upon binding, can interact with their partners without requiring or acquiring stable structures [53]. Additionally, IDRs are enriched in PTMs [54], making them extremely prone to regulation. To identify potential interaction and modification sites in the MTMR CTDs, the sequences where submitted to the Eukaryotic Linear Motif Data base (ELM) [37] predicting SLiMs (Table 1).

  For each MTMR, we focused only on interaction sites in the cell compartments in which the respective MTMR is known to exist in [38]. Additionally, sequences were submitted to MobiDB, a database on IDPs, specialized on predicting linear interaction peptides (LIPs) as potential interaction areas [55]. LIPs can be longer than SLiMs, but do not give suggestions for potential partners. Finally, we sourced the PhosphoSitePlus webserver, a website that collects and curates information on experimentally determined PTMs reported in published research [50]. The position of SLiMs, LIPS and phosphosites were mapped along the sequence and representatives of each group are shown in Figure 2. Members of all groups can be seen in Figures S3–S6. An overview of SLiMs in all MTMRs is provided in Table 2.


#### Motifs With Implication for the Life Cycle of MTMR Proteins
  Several MTMRs contain specialized SLiMs, like Y‐based signals for the sorting of membrane proteins [56], found in members of all groups. One sorting signal is present in two members of G1 (MTM1 and MTMR2) and two members of G2 (MTMR9 and ‐10) and one member of G3 (MTMR5), respectively. MTMR3 and MTMR13 (G3 and G4, respectively) contain two Y‐based sorting signals each, and MTMR4 contains three. The Y‐based sorting signal is the only sorting signal found in G1. Some members of G2‐4 also contain di‐leucine motifs for endosomal, basolateral, and lysosomal trafficking [57]. These are MTMR7, ‐9 and ‐12 in G2, MTMR5 in G3 and MTMR4 in G4. PtdIns3P, one of the MTMR substrates, is found in endosomal membranes [58] and many MTMRs localize to the endomembrane system [5].

  MTMR9 and ‐11 (G2), MTMR5 (G3) and MTMR4 (G4) contain di‐arginine motifs in their CTDs, which are predicted to serve as signals for retention at and localization to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [59, 60]. Of the four MTMRs, only MTMR9 has been experimentally shown to be enriched at the ER [61], whereas there seems to be a lack of experimental data to support ER‐localization of the other three MTMRs.

  Some transport signals are unique to the CTDs of a single group or even a single MTMR. Both members of G3 (MTMR5 and ‐13) contain a di‐tryptophan SLiM predicted to serve retrograde trafficking from the golgi to the ER [62]. This motif is not found in any other CTDs (Table 2). Uniquely among MTMRs, MTMR4 contains a predicted interaction site for the light chain of kinesin (KLC1), an important motor protein [63]. At last, and in agreement with its localization at the nuclear envelope [5], MTMR8 contains a nuclear export signal [64].

  Half of the MTMR‐CTDs (MTMR1‐5, ‐10 and ‐13) contain binding SLiMs for the N‐lobe of the activity‐regulated cytoskeleton‐associated protein (ARC) (Table 2), which indicates that these MTMRs (and potentially their dimerization partners) get transported to and are functional at the synapses of neurons [65]. The presence of this ARC‐binding site stresses the reported roles of MTMRs in health and disease of the neuromuscular system [24, 25, 66, 67].

  SLiMs targeting the MTMRs for degradation are observed in only a few MTMRs, suggesting that their degradation is dependent on phosphorylation to establish phosphodegrons [68]. MTMR2 contains a phospho‐dependent FWB7‐degron [69], and MTMR4 contains a PUB‐domain binding motif, found in proteins regulating ubiquitinoylation and are linked to the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation pathway [70]. MTMR8 is the only MTMR predicted to contain deubiquitination sites for ubiquitin‐specific peptidase 7 [71].

  Thus, we can find information about localization and trafficking of MTMRs as well as their degradation, encoded in the sequence features of their CTDs, highlighting the importance of these disordered tails for the life cycle of MTMRs. Many of these SLiMs fit very well with the reported location of the MTMRs at cellular membranes, especially the endosomes, the localization of MTMR8 at the nuclear envelope and MTMR9 at the ER. In addition, there are also new hints for MTMRs being localized to the ER or bound to motor proteins like kinesin, which have not yet been described in the literature.


#### Motifs Responsible for MTMR Functions Beyond Phosphatase Activity
  In the analyses, we found many different SLiMs that may direct interactions with signaling proteins, proteins involved in autophagy, cancer metabolism, inflammation and immunology, and even protein translation, as well as interaction with transcription factors.

  In the CTD of all MTMRs, ELM predicts SLiMs for modular domains of adaptor proteins including SH2, SH3, WW, and PDZ domains [72, 73, 74]. All MTMRs contain at least one SLiM for binding one of these domains and most contain multiple SLiMs for more than one (Table 2). For example, all G1 members contain SLiMs for Class IV WW, PDZ, and SH3 domains. G1 members only have one single predicted SLiM for each of the interaction domains. G3 and G4, with the longest CTDs contain on average 10 and 25 predicted SLiMs for modular interaction domains, respectively (Table 2). These types of adaptor proteins are involved in linking different proteins together facilitating cellular signaling, for example by connecting receptors at the top of a pathway to down‐stream effectors [73]. The presence of these SLiMs makes it likely that MTMRs are more directly involved in cellular signaling pathways, than merely by regulating PI‐levels.

  Multiple binding sites to such adaptor proteins introduces multivalency [75, 76], which is a prerequisite for condensate formation [77]. This multivalency may suggest that MTMRs carry the potential to form cellular condensates and be involved in phase separation (PS) [78]. Punctae‐formation has been shown for MTMR7 [6] and other MTMRs [5], which supports the potential for PS. We used two different webservers to predict the probability for PS formation for the CTDs (Figure S7 and Table S1), FuzDrop [79, 80, 81] and ParSe [82]. MTMR6 and ‐9 are the only two MTMRs lacking PS‐prone sequences in their CTDs. This makes the pair of MTMR6 and MTMR9 the only MTMR‐heterodimer without any predicted PS‐forming capacity, fitting with data reporting lower punctae‐formation for MTMR6 [61]. It could be at odds with both being predicted to contain SH2 and SH3 SliMs. The multivalency of the MTMRs is intriguing and more research into the exact features and to which adaptor proteins that may drive PS for MTMRs is needed, just as the role of the multivalency for MTMR functions remains to be resolved.

  Another protein family predicted to interact with many MTMRs are 14‐3‐3‐proteins, which regulate a broad spectrum of cellular functions and recognize a phosphorylated serine/threonine SLiM [83, 84] (Table 2). Eight MTMRs are predicted to contain 14‐3‐3 interaction sites, with the number of predicted sites varying from one to eight (Table 2). Thus, it seems likely that many MTMRs bind to 14‐3‐3 proteins, although none of these complexes have yet been reported. With 14‐3‐3 proteins functioning as scaffolding proteins that modulate signaling pathways involved in multiple cellular functions, including apoptosis, cell cycle control and energy metabolism [85], this feature directly links MTMRs to these same functions through the interaction potential of their disordered CTDs.

  Many predicted SLiMs in the CTDs relate to interaction with mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), making up essential parts of prominent oncogenic pathways in cells and especially in tumor cells, where they regulate, among others, proliferation, survival, and apoptosis [86]. In case of MTM1, MTMR1, ‐7, ‐12, and ‐13, the predictions were for MAPKs in general, and more specifically for MTMR3‐5 and MTMR11, the MAPK‐subfamilies ERK1/2 and p38 were suggested as potential binding partner.

  The tumor necrosis factor receptor‐associated family (TRAF), involved in signaling events related to immune responses, cell death, and survival [87], is also predicted to interact with multiple MTMRs (Table 2). TRAF interaction motifs (TIMs) are most prominent in G3 and 4, where all members contain at least one TIM, and MTMR4 even three, each for a different TRAF family member, which is unique for MTMRs. TIMs are rarer in G1 and 2, with just one member of each group carrying one. This potentially relates MTMRs to multiple cellular signaling pathways involved in cell survival, stress response, and embryonic development [88].

  Of additional relevance to cell fate and survival, many MTMRs were predicted to contain canonical LC3‐interacting regions (LIRs) (Table 2), which bind to microtubule‐associated proteins 1A/1B light chain 3 (LC3), an important component of the autophagy machinery [89]. Notably, all catalytically inactive MTMRs, except for MTMR11 contain LIRs, irrespective of their CTD length or group association, while there are large group differences for catalytically active MTMRs. Here, none of the three MTMRs in G1 contain LIRs, while, in contrast, both members of G4 contain multiple ones (Table 2). It is interesting to note that every single MTMR‐heterodimer [5] would contain at least one member with at least one LIR, and this may be an observation underscoring an important role of MTMR(‐heterodimers) in autophagy. All members of the G3 and 4 contain two to three of these SLiMs, while three out of the four catalytically inactive members of G2 (MTMR9, ‐10 and ‐12) and one active member (MTMR6) contain one LIR each. In MTMR6 and MTMR9, which are heterodimerization partners [5], these single LIRs were located within the CC domain.

  There are several interaction sites that have been predicted only in one or two MTMRs, and this may reveal more diverse functions (Table 2). MTMR4 and MTMR6 are for example, predicted to contain WH2 motifs, which bind actin monomers preventing or enhancing actin filament formation [90]. MTMR4 also contains a binding site for the transcriptional repressor C‐terminal binding protein (CtBP), with important roles in oncogenesis [91]. MTMR8, the MTMR at the nuclear envelope [5], contains both a canonical LXXLL motif for nuclear receptor binding [92] and two SLiMs for binding of forkhead‐associated (FHA) domains found in transcription factors and kinases involved in nuclear signaling [93]. MTMR13 contains two SLiMs binding the dorsal surface of eukaryotic translation initiation factor eIF4E [94]. Binding of MTMRs to transcription factors has been described for MTMR7 binding to PPARγ [9], so it is possible that MTMR4 and MTMR8 have similar functions. There is no published information on MTMR4 or MTMR6 being involved in cytoskeleton formation or eukaryotic translation. Maybe the GEF‐function of the DENN domain of MTMR13 could play a role in the GTP‐controlled steps of translation under certain conditions [95].

  Taken together, MTMRs can be predicted to be highly regulated and involved in numerous signaling functions ranging from common functions to very specialized MTMR‐specific functions. The longer the CTD the more SLiM‐based interaction sites can be predicted, and the possibility of multiple sites of the same SLiM enhances the multivalency important for example, condensate formation. Many of these predicted interactions remains to be experimentally explored, and therefore the functional landscape of the MTMRs still shows many unknowns.


#### (De)‐phosphorylation and Protease Cleavage
  In agreement with the enrichment of serine and threonine residues, G1 members have a high density of predicted phosphorylation sites in the C‐terminal third of their CTDs [51]. All MTMRs except MTMR9, are predicted to be Ser/Thr‐phosphorylated to various degrees (Figure S3–S6), and ELM predicts SliMs for 13 different kinases (Table 3). We can observe group differences: The pool of kinases for G1 is the smallest (six kinases) (Table 3, yellow rows), G2 and G3 have a pool of 12 different kinases (Table 3, red and blue rows) and only G4 has SLiMs for all 13 kinases (Table 3, green rows). Among the shared kinases we find glycogen synthase kinase‐3 (GSK3) [96], MAPK [86], and casein kinases 1 and 2 (CK1 and CK2). CK1 and CK2 play roles in regulating the circadian rhythm and cell signaling, and the most prominent effects of their deregulation are neurological diseases like Alzheimer's for CK1 and cancer for CK2 [97]. Others are phosphoinositide‐3‐kinase‐related protein kinases (PIKK), involved in DNA damage response and nutrient dependent signaling [98] and cAMP‐dependent protein kinase (PKA) [99, 100]. The kinases that are relevant for the longer members, but not G1, are involved in cell‐cycle control like polo‐like kinases (PLK) 1–4 [101], cyclin dependent kinases (CDK) [102], and NIMA‐related kinase 2 (NEK2) [103]. MTMR4 is unique among the MTMRs for being predicted to be phosphorylated by large tumor suppressor (LATS) kinase, which regulates the cell‐cycle in the nervous system [104], potentially linking MTMR4 to cell‐proliferation and differentiation in the nervous system.

  These phosphorylation profiles underscore the likelihood for complex regulation of MTMRs via their CTDs and in numerous cellular pathways, and once more link MTMRs to neurological diseases, cancer and cell survival.

  Some MTMRs contain phosphatase SLiMs (Table 2). The G3 members contain SLiMs for both B56 phosphatase [105] and calcineurin [106, 107] whereas MTMR7‐9 and MTMR12 only has SLiMs for B56. MTMR11 contains one, too, in addition to a protein phosphatase 1 SLiM, also found in MTMR5 [108]. Interestingly, G1, defined by a comparably high density of phosphorylation sites, does not contain any known phosphatase SLiMs.

  ELM also predicted protease cleavage sites in the CTDs of several MTMRs. All members of G3 and 4 and MTMR7 from G2 have at least one or in the case of MTMR3, two cleavage sites for caspase‐3 and ‐7, which are two executor proteases of programmed cell death [109]. MTMR5, ‐6 and ‐12 contain cleavage sites for subtilisin‐like protein convertases [110] and MTMR6 a cleavage site for a N‐arginine dibasic convertase [111], both cleaving precursor proteins to activate them. In MTMR5, this would lead to the removal of the Y‐based sorting signal, the PDZ‐binding motif and a few SH‐ and WW‐binding motifs. MTMR12 would lose its MAPK SLiM and its LIR. In MTMR6, the three cleavage sites are positioned in a way that would remove SLiMs for adaptor protein modules and 14‐3‐3. Thus, we speculate that some MTMRs may have parts of the CTD removed, and that these peptides may function independently from the rest of the MTMR protein.


#### LIPs
  MobiDB predicted LIPs in the CTDs of all MTMRs. The LIPs were in general considerably longer than SLiMs predicted by ELM and not necessarily overlapping. As an example, the LIPs predicted for MTMR1 have almost no overlap with the ELM predicted SLiMs (Figure 2A). The LIP in the C‐terminal half of MTMR7s CC domain (residues 537–559) overlaps partially with a SLiM for B56 (residues 535–541) and for MAPKs (residues 541–550). It also overlaps with the experimentally determined KRAS IA2, spanning residues 537–546 [6]. MobiDB therefore seems to highlight interaction areas that can be covered by ELM predicted SLiMs, and by experimental results and shows additional interaction hotspots, highlighting potential unrecognized spots in the map of the MTMR interactome.

  In conclusion, the IDRs of the MTMR‐CTDs are brimming with SLiMs, many of which are supported by published functional studies or localization data. The properties of the predicted SLiMs hint at novel functions for the MTMRs, but experimental data is lacking. Thus, SLiM positions within the CTDs and their properties constitute promising and plausible starting points for studies in cell biology, biophysics, or biochemistry illuminating new roles of MTMRs.


### The CC Domains
  The predicted CC domains of the MTMRs constitute a second hallmark of the family, present in all but MTMR10, and at different lengths and positions within the chains (Figure 1B). The average length is 32 ± 9 residues. Some are very short (MTMR5 and MTMR13, ~15 residues) and the longest predicted CC domain (47 residues) is found in MTMR9. Not much is known about the CC domains. No structures have been solved, their stabilities are uncharacterized and their ability to form homo‐ or heterodimers or even higher order structures unknown. This leaves more open questions regarding the MTMR functional landscape.

  CC domains are at the same time simple and complex. They can be disordered [36] and contain a sequence composition that only allows for one type of secondary structure, the α‐helix [112]. A CC sequence is a repetition of heptad repeats, in which the amino acids are numbered from a to g with positions a and d usually (80% of cases) occupied by hydrophobic residues, while e and g are populated with charged amino acids (Figure 3A). An important feature of CC domains are trigger sequences, which are areas of higher helical propensity and concentration of charged residues, facilitating encounter and helix formation between the CC partners [113, 114]. The hydrophobic amino acids form a hydrophobic interface between the CC helices, while the charged residues form salt‐bridges between helices and can be involved in controlling homo‐ or hetero‐oligomer formation by charge attraction or repulsion, depending on the charge patterning of the partners [115, 116, 117] (Figure 3A). The sequence features encode CC domain orientation, stability, and order of oligomerization, which spans from a simple parallel dimer of two helices to α‐sheets and α‐cylinders [112, 118, 119]. It has been shown that CC complexes are “two‐stranded if the (a, d) pair is (I, L), three‐stranded if the (a, d) pair is (I, I) and four‐stranded if the (a, d) pair is (L, I)” [112, 120, 121]. β‐branched amino acids (I, V, T) at position a and d promoted trimer formation, while charged residues at a and d gave dimers [112, 122, 123]. CC domains were also shown to favor glutamates over aspartates, as aspartates destabilize helices [124].

  A MSA of the MTMR‐CC domains shows very little sequence conservation (Figure 3B). MTMR10 was excluded, as the DeepCoil CC prediction server and literature on MTMRs agreed that MTMR10 lacks a CC domain [2, 125], despite AF3 predicting one (Figure S1). No residue or residue property is fully conserved throughout all 13 family members. MTMR9 has the only CC domain that aligns with the others but only in its C‐terminal half. The most similar CC domains are found within G1, G3, and G4. G1 and G4 both have CC domains of 30–40 amino acids length, which can be well aligned within the groups and in some cases containing four to five residue‐long stretches that are identical between all group members (Figure 3B). In the G1 CC domains, 29% (10 residues) are identical between the members and an additional 15% (five residues) are similar, meaning that at least properties like charge or hydrophobicity are conserved at specific position. This leads to an overall likeness (sum of identical and similar residues) of 44% between the CC domains in G1. In G3, the CC domains have no stretches of identical residues longer than two, but the CC domains are also short (15.5 residues on average). They contain five identical residues and four positions with similar residues, giving an overall likeness of 58%. In G4, 58% (23 residues) are identical and another 10% (four residues) are similar, giving a likeness of 68%. G2 is again mainly defined by large differences, and only a single hydrophobic residue is similar across G2. The most alike CC domains of G2 are MTMR6‐8, which are the three interaction partners of MTMR9. The three proteins share several identical hydrophobic and negatively charged residues in the middle third of the sequence, which are also partially conserved in the C‐terminal end of MTMR9. Additionally, MTMR6‐8 share hydrophobic residues at the N‐terminus, also found in the central part of the MTMR9 CC domain.

  Nine out of the thirteen CC domains have an overall negative net charge, with MTMR7 and MTMR9 being the most negatively charged (−5). The four positively charged family members are MTMR1 and MTMR2 (+1.9) in G1, MTMR6 (+2.8) in G2 and MTMR4 (+1.6) in G4. With a net charge of −0.1, MTMR3 and MTMR8 have the lowest overall charge. A clear preference for glutamates is seen for all CC domains. The average MTMR‐CC domain contains 5 ± 2 glutamates, and only 1 ± 0.8 aspartates, with five MTMRs lacking aspartates (Figure 4). A preference of glutamates for CC domains has been described, especially for parallel CC domains [124, 126]. For positive charges, the average MTMR contains 2.5 ± 1.7 arginines and 2.7 ± 1.9 lysines, without preference. Thus, MTMRs have a charged amino acid content typical for CC‐domains that could form both parallel and anti‐parallel complexes.

  For hydrophobicity, most MTMR CC domains, except MTMR6, contain more leucines (4.8 ± 1.2) than isoleucines (1.6 ± 1), and only a few of the other β‐branched amino acids: 1.5 ± 1.1 valines and 1.2 ± 0.6 threonines (Figure 4). Based on the knowledge that β‐branched amino acids promote trimeric CC complexes, while leucine promotes dimers [122, 123], we use the ratio between the number of leucines and the number of β‐branched amino acids to predict which oligomeric state may be preferred by each MTMR CC domain. Two MTMRs contain exactly equal numbers of leucines and β‐branched amino acids perhaps dynamically favoring dimerization or trimerization. Six MTMRs contain more leucines than β‐branched residues and may therefore prefer dimeric states (MTM1, MTMR5, MTMR7, MTMR8, MTMR11, and MTMR13). This is the clearest for G3 and MTM1. Overall, only MTMR5 and MTMR13 contain almost exclusively classic heptad repeats, where all a and d positions are hydrophobic. They contain mostly leucines and only one isoleucine, one threonine, and no valines (Figure 4). The overall average β‐branched amino acids content is 4.3 ± 1.8, like leucine. This amino acid composition makes it difficult to predict the average preference for MTMRs to form trimers or dimers from sequence alone [123].

  AF predictions depict the CC domains of MTMR7 and MTMR9 as fully helical in the absence of the CC partner. It does so with very high confidence over the entire CC domain of MTMR9 and with high and very high confidence over the N‐terminal and central part of MTMR7 (Figure 3C). To address if AF3 can be a useful source of information about the MTMR‐CC domains, we modeled experimentally confirmed MTMR‐CC complexes, as well some without experimental evidence (Figure 3D–J). AF3 is equally confident about experimentally confirmed complexes (MTMR9‐CC:MTMR7‐CC) as well as pairs that have never been shown to occur experimentally (MTMR9‐CC:MTM1‐CC). This shows that AF3 is not yet a helpful tool when investigating MTMR CC domains computationally.


### The CCs of MTMR7 and MTMR9 Hetero‐ and Homo‐Oligomerize With Specific Preferences
  To experimentally investigate the interactions and oligomeric states of MTMR‐CC domains, we recombinantly expressed and purified the MTMR7‐CC (residues 514–554) and MTMR9‐CC (residues 494–549) as models known to form heterodimeric CCs [7]. To access the secondary structure and the formation of coiled‐coils, we recorded far‐UV CD data of the individual CCs and their 1:1 M ratio mixtures.

  The CD spectrum of MTMR7‐CC at 28 μM shows a helical profile, however, corresponding only to a helical content of 17% (Figure 5A). Adding the helix‐inducing agent trifluoroethanol (TFE) increases the helicity to 90% at 40%–50% TFE (Figure 5A), showing that the MTMR7‐CC has propensity to form a helix throughout its length. Increasing the concentration of MTMR7‐CC to 40 μM in the absence of TFE increases its helicity to 25% (Figure 5B), suggesting homodimer formation. In contrast to MTMR7‐CC, the CD spectrum of MTMR9‐CC shows 64% helicity at 40 μM, either due to homo‐oligomerization or a higher intrinsic helicity. To address heterodimer formation, the two proteins were mixed at equimolar concentrations (40 μM of each), and a spectrum of the mixture recorded. The spectrum of the mixture of the two proteins is not identical to the theoretical sum of the spectra of the two individual proteins (Figure 5B), with a helical content of the complex of 71% compared with 81% extracted for the theoretical mixture. Thus, the two proteins influence each other, likely through heterodimer formation.

  To investigate where in the CC domains these interactions would occur, we expressed the two proteins in 15N‐stable isotope labeled media. Analyses of the 15N‐1H‐HSQC NMR spectra of 15N ‐labeled MTMR7‐CC alone at low concentration revealed a resolved spectrum with 41 peaks covering the entire length, as shown earlier [6]. Adding unlabeled MTMR9‐CC at increasing concentrations shows that almost all peaks are lost at equimolar concentrations and at MTMR9‐CC‐excess, only one signal from the MTMR7‐CC C‐terminus remains. Three new peaks appear, likely corresponding to the three neighboring residues of the MTMR7‐CC's C‐terminus (Figure 5C), as described for MTMR7‐CC homo‐oligomerization [6]. In previous work NMR experiments of MTMR7‐CC at 160 μM were used to extract structural information about helical propensity for the different residues in the peptide and led to the identification of the potential trigger sequence of the CC domain [6]. NMR experiments of 15N ‐labeled MTMR9‐CC at the same concentration as in this previous study, show nine peaks, compared to the expected 47 peaks (Figure 5D), and thus analysis and extraction of structural information is not possible. CC complexes can be challenging to be observed in NMR, as they tumble slower along their elongated axis, which makes them appear like proteins too big to be observed in NMR [127, 128]. The NMR data is therefore in accordance with the CD data, which shows heterodimer formation of MTMR7‐CC and MTMR9‐CC and higher helicity for MTMR9‐CC than for MTMR7‐CC.

  At last, to investigate the intrinsic oligomerization behavior of the two CC domains, oligomerization was addressed individually using the bis(sulfosuccinimidyl)suberate (BS3) crosslinker, exploiting lysine residues [129] at different concentrations. For MTMR7‐CC, a concentration dependent behavior is observed: at 40 μM only one band, the monomer, at ~4 kDa was visible, while increasing the concentration leads to the appearance of higher mass bands corresponding to dimers at 100 μM and trimers and tetramers at both 400 and 1000 μM, with the monomer and dimer bands being the most intense (Figure 5E). For MTMR9‐CC, higher order oligomers are always visible. In addition to the 5 kDa band of the monomer and a 15 kDa band of a trimer, which are the most intense bands, also 20, 30 kDa and potentially 35, 40, and 45 kDa bands are visible at both 80 and 160 μM concentrations. The four highest‐mass bands are very faint (Figure 5E). These observations are disagreeing with the AF3 prediction for MTMR‐CC‐domains as AF3 was more confident about MTMR9‐CC homodimers than the MTMR9‐CC homotrimers, experimentally proven here (Figure 3F,G). These results finally confirm that the higher helicity of MTMR9‐CC by CD and the lack of peaks in the NMR spectrum can be explained by homo‐oligomerization, but gives no direct information about a potentially higher intrinsic helicity compared with MTMR7‐CC. Thus, we can experimentally show that individual MTMR‐CCs can have different preferences for oligomeric states and distinct behaviors at changing concentrations, reflecting differences in stability (Figure 4).

  In conclusion, we could experimentally show that the CC domains of MTMR7 and MTMR9, differing in length and sequence, can oligomerize with each other and with themselves. We show that they have individual intrinsic helicity and intrinsic oligomerization behavior, resulting in preference for different oligomeric states.


## Discussion
  Bioinformatic investigations of the CTDs of the MTMR family revealed interesting commonalities and differences. It was possible to group MTMRs based on common characteristics of their CTDs, like length, over‐all architecture or amino acid content and sequence conservation of the CC domains (Figures 3B, 4, Table 1). Different groups or subgroups would be imaginable, for example splitting G2 based on whether they dimerize with MTMR9 (MTMR6‐9) or not (MTMR10‐12), or whether they contain a CC domain (all but MTMR10). However, we argue that the groups we chose were already able to capture many commonalities between MTMRs such as sequential likeness of CC domains. At the same time, focusing on the details of the predicted interaction sites and CC domain sequences, it became clear that even MTMRs with very similar CTDs like MTMR3 and ‐4 in G4 or the three members of G1, could contain different predicted interaction sites and thereby implicated in different cellular functions.

  It has been argued that the interactions of MTMR7 with KRAS and PPARγ via its CC domain on one hand and with its CC‐dimerization partner MTMR9 on the other hand are mutually exclusive [6, 9]. We predicted three MTMR dimers using AF3 and mapped the ELM and MobiDB‐predicted interaction sites onto the CTD that would still be accessible for interactions with other partners (Figure 6), showing that numerous of these remain accessible. As it has already been shown that MTMRs can be targeted to and activated at different subcellular locations [61, 130], dimerization may vastly increase the possible effects of single MTMRs in the cell: Dimerizing with one MTMR partner over another, or not dimerizing at all, would lead to different combinations of accessible interaction and targeting motifs for an MTMR complex, and thereby different interaction partners and target locations, as discussed below. It is not possible to discuss the role of all predicted SLiMS for all 14 MTMRs in depth, and in the following, we discuss only selected findings.


### Localization of MTMRs Assisted and Controlled by the Disordered CTDs
  Many of the ELM‐predicted SLiMs agree with function and localization of MTMRs reported in the literature. As such, membrane association of lipid‐phosphatases has been described for many MTMRs, mainly attributed to their PH‐GRAM domains, present and conserved in all 14 MTMRs [5, 8, 14]. It has also been shown how loss of a dimerization partner can impair the proper subcellular localization of the remaining MTMR [131]. While the globular PH‐GRAM domains may explain how MTMRs attach to the membrane, they do not explain how exactly MTMRs are targeted specifically to different membranes in the cell: the endosome, the ER, the nuclear envelope, lysosomal membranes, and the plasma membrane [5, 25].

  Our results show that the answer may lie in the CTDs, as almost all MTMRs contain sorting signals, either to endosomal membranes (Y‐based sorting signal in MTM1 and MTMR2‐5, ‐9, ‐10 and ‐13), to basolateral or lysosomal membranes (MTMR4, ‐5, ‐7, ‐9 and ‐12), to the ER via a di‐arginine motif (MTMR4, ‐5, ‐9 and ‐11), transport via kinesin (MTMR4) or from the Golgi to the ER (MTMR5 and MTMR13). As for example, inactive MTMR9 recruits MTMR6 and MTMR8 to specific locations and activates them to regulate ER‐to‐golgi trafficking [132], it seems plausible that it is the ER‐retention motif in the MTMR9 CTD that plays a role in this context. While this mode of action has to be tested experimentally, it would have general implications for the sorting and co‐functioning of MTMRs: for example, MTMR3, which can either homodimerize or heterodimerize with MTMR4 [5], may be targeted to the ER or basolateral membranes or transported as cargo by kinesins by being bound to MTMR4, which contains all of these sorting signals. An MTMR3 homodimer may conversely only be sorted to lysosomal or endosomal membranes, but not to the ER, as it contains only Y‐based sorting motifs. The choice of partners likely changes MTMR localization, as was seen for MTMR2, which dimerizes with either MTMR5 or MTMR13 (both catalytically inactive) and has been shown to be targeted to its subcellular location by MTMR5 [133]. To fully capture the relevance of these SLiMs for the MTMR family, it would be necessary to mutate them in the respective MTMRs and evaluate changes or abolishment of their normal targeting pattern in cells or if swapping CTDs can change the respective localization of the mutants.


### The Disordered CTDs Support Functions of MTMRs in the Nervous System
  As MTMR1‐5, MTMR10, and MTMR13 contain ARC binding motifs in their CTDs, marking them as synaptic proteins, the connection between MTMRs and neuropathy is strengthened through our results [65, 134]. The most direct connection between ARC and an MTMR comes from data showing that MTMR1 levels affect ARC capsid secretion, which is regulated by PtdIns3P [67]. ARC is specifically produced in the brain and in the testis of mice [135], and MTMR2 and MTMR5 have been linked to both neuropathy and spermatogenesis, further supporting a link between the two proteins [22, 136, 137]. In the catalytically active MTMR2, the ARC interaction site lies within the CC domain. Therefore, it may either work as a monomer in the brain and testis or rely on the catalytically inactive MTMR5 or MTMR13 for targeting [111]. Both contain ARC‐binding sites outside their CC domains (Figure 6A). The latter could also explain why loss of MTMR2, MTMR5, or MTMR13 leads to Charcot–Marie‐Tooth neuropathy, albeit with different phenotypes [22, 23, 24, 66].


### The Disordered CTDs of MTMRs Are Important for Cell Fate
  MTMR5 has been found to be a critical factor in suppressing autophagy in neurons, a function related to the MTMR2/MTMR5 heterodimer lowering PtdIns3P‐levels [138]. High levels of MTMR5 prevent autophagy and the presence of MTMR5 kept levels of fluorescently labeled LC3 punctae low, even in the presence of autophagy inducers [138, 139]. LC3, a part of the autophagy machinery, can bind proteins containing LIRs [89]. MTMR5 as well as almost all other catalytically inactive MTMRs (aside from MTMR11) and catalytically active MTMR3, MTMR4, and MTMR6 contain predicted LIRs. Accordingly, the active MTMR3 was shown to reduce autophagy by lowering PtdIns3P‐levels [11, 140, 141]. LIRs recruit cargo to be degraded to the inner surface of the autophagosome, but they also recruit proteins that help in transport and maturation of the autophagosome to its outer surface [89]. It is not clear which role the LIRs of MTMRs could play in suppressing autophagy, if any. Mutating the LIRs in MTMRs and testing for effects on autophagy could provide important insights.

  MTMR3 has been shown to promote colorectal [142] and breast cancer [143]. In contrast, MTMR7 was found to reduce colorectal cancer growth by direct inhibition of K‐RAS and activation of PPARγ [6, 9]. While interaction sites for RAS or nuclear receptors cannot yet be predicted by ELM, some LIPs were predicted by MobiDB, overlapping nicely with the interaction site for K‐RAS in the CC domain of MTMR7. Aside from that, many interaction sites for MAPKs and TRAFs were predicted for MTMR3 and MTMR7 and other MTMRs, all worth further investigations in search for new strategies to treat cancer. Our findings strengthen the connection of MTMRs with cancer directly through the properties of their CTDs.


### The Disordered CTDs Show Functions Related to Cellular Signaling
  While there was no predicted interaction site for nuclear receptors in MTMR7, despite experimental evidence for its interaction with PPARγ, a classical LXXLL‐motif was identified in MTMR8. LXXLL motifs are found in co‐activators and co‐repressors of nuclear receptors, which bind to their ligand binding domain to regulate activity [144, 145, 146]. MTMR8, located at the nuclear envelope, may thus be able to bind to nuclear receptors and translocate with them to the nucleus, in a similar way as shown for MTMR7 [9]. Additionally, MTMR8 contains interaction sites for other nuclear signaling factors [93] and a nuclear export signal, making retro‐transport of MTMR8 out of the nucleus possible [64] and supporting the idea of its nuclear shuttling. It is not obvious which functions these interactions could have. MTMR8 is known to dimerize with MTMR9 to increase phosphatase activity [5, 130]. But as for MTMR7 there may be more functions unrelated to MTMR9 or dephosphorylations that could be performed by MTMR8 and other MTMRs.

  The SH, WW, 14‐3‐3 and PDZ SLiMs, as well as the number of predicted SLiMs for signaling related kinases (Tables 2, 3), show potential for MTMRs to be involved in diverse signaling networks including PS (Figure S7) [72, 73, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 84, 86, 96, 97, 99, 101, 102, 103, 147]. AF3 predictions of the MTMR3/4 heterocomplex highlights the hundreds of residues long CTDs accessible for interaction with other proteins or for engaging in condensates and PS and show how the CTDs are brimming with SLiMs (Figure 6C). MobiDB predicts many more interaction stretches and with experimentally shown interaction sites for RAS or PPARγ in MTMR7, this data jointly highlights the large potential for plausible uncharacterized functionalities of MTMRs beyond the ELM predictions.


### The Disordered CTDs Support Variable Higher Order Structures Through the CC Domains
  The biophysical and structural characterization of MTMR7‐CC and MTMR9‐CC shown here and previously [6], propose that the monomeric MTMR7‐CC is disordered. The fact that 90% helicity can be reached with the addition of just 40% TFE highlights its high intrinsic helical propensity [148]. Based on its sequence we predicted MTMR9 to have a lower intrinsic helical propensity than MTMR7: it contains more helix‐destabilizing short polar amino acids, like aspartate and asparagine [124, 126], which are completely lacking in MTMR7‐CC. This difference in helix propensity is additionally supported by Lifson–Roig's (LR) classical helix theory [149, 150, 151]: When comparing the product of the LR propagation parameters [151] of the amino acids in the two CC domains a lower helical propensity for MTMR9‐CC was confirmed. Experimentally, the helical propensity of MTMR9‐CC cannot be easily tested by TFE addition, as MTMR9‐CC under similar conditions is more helical than MTMR7 due to more stable CC homo‐oligomerization, deducted from a combination of CD, NMR, and cross‐linking experiments (Figure 5B,E).

  Homo‐oligomerization has been reported for MTMR9 by co‐immunoprecipitation and in yeast‐two‐hybrid assays, but not for MTMR7 [5, 7], supporting homo‐oligomerization to be more favorable for MTMR9 than for MTMR7, as confirmed here. Both peptides show potential to homo‐oligomerize, but while MTMR9 already oligomerizes to its maximal potential at lower concentrations, MTMR7 requires higher concentrations to be driven to form oligomers. Their sequences further explain this finding. All negative charges of MTMR7 lie in a dense group in the center of the sequence, while the positive charges are located towards the termini. Due to electrostatic repulsion from the highly negatively charged center, this effect makes homodimerization unfavorable. MTMR9‐CC shows alternating broader and less dense patches of charges along the sequence, making it compatible with binding both MTMR7 and itself.

  The different properties of MTMR7 and MTMR9 suggest that intrinsic helical propensity and oligomerization potential for each MTMR must be understood independently. It would be necessary to experimentally investigate the CC domains of all MTMRs in a similar way as shown here, to decompose the dimer landscape of MTMRs. Are the dimers usually formed by CC‐domains with similar helical propensity or are they formed by pairs of a less and a more helical CC domain, respectively, where the CC domain with the higher propensity could form a platform for the one with lower propensity to dock and fold?

  The homo‐oligomers differed as well: MTMR7‐CC went from monomer to dimer, while MTMR9‐CC went from monomer directly to trimer (Figure 5E). This can also be explained by their divergent sequences. More β‐branched and fewer charged residues may offer an explanation for the dissimilar behavior [121, 123]. MTMR9 contains six β‐branched residues, an above average number for MTMRs, and many of these at a and d positions. It also has a lower ratio of charged residues compared to MTMR7‐CC. MTMR9‐CC is, therefore, prone to form trimers as its first oligomerization step, whereas MTMR7 first forms dimers.

  Equipped with the observation that biophysical and biochemical results agree with predictions that can be made from the CC‐sequences and available literature, we scrutinized the other 11 CC domains (Figure 4). Thus, G3 MTMRs would form dimers, G4 trimers, while the members of G1 and G2 likely have mixed preferences (Figure 4). Thus, the energy landscapes of the MTMRs are highly diverse and each member is poised to form preferable oligomers and may have vastly different intrinsic helical propensities, that could reveal how the oligomers form in the cell and why some MTMRs cooperate, while others do not. Cellular concentrations as well as localizations play key roles in determining the oligomeric landscape.

  Our experimental results come with the caveat that they only consider the CC domains in isolation. In context of the full‐length proteins at physiological conditions, it may be that only (parallel) dimers are formed, as described for the MTMR2‐homodimer [8]: the globular domains interact with each other, and the PH domains may have to face the same direction to tether the protein complexes properly to cellular membranes. Orienting more than two sets of globular core domains like this, next to each other, may impose steric restraints, as they lie N‐terminally close to the CC domain (Figures 1 and 6). G4 members may be different. Here, the CC domains are over 400 residues away from the core domains and an additional 50 residues from the small folded FYVE domains at the C‐terminus of the CTD. Their CC‐domains also contain seven β‐branched amino acids, more than any other MTMR. This amino acid composition makes it possible that higher homo‐ and hetero‐oligomers of both parallel and anti‐parallel orientations could be formed in the full‐length protein complexes under physiological conditions, providing all folded domains the freedom to orient towards the membrane and pair up for interactions.


### Conclusion
  The disordered CTDs of the MTMRs contain a wealth of unexplored functional potential, independent of the phosphatase function and they serve as interaction scaffolds that support subcellular localization, trafficking, and regulation of the MTMRs. While some of these interactions maybe related to targeting MTMRs to cellular locations where PI dephosphorylation is needed [31], at least one example of MTMR‐CTD fragments performing functions without the phosphatase domains exists [6, 9], and more examples plausibly will emerge. Our bioinformatic analyses paint a picture of a much broader functional landscape of MTMRs, with many more roles than PI dephosphorylation. CTDs of MTMRs may engage in signaling networks, as we found binding sites for adaptor protein domains in all of them. MTMRs may regulate transcription factors as already experimentally shown for MTMR7 and now predicted for MTMR4 and MTMR8, and directly meddle in cell fate pathways, such as autophagy, through interactions with LC3, as every MTMR‐heterodimer would contain at least one partner with LIRs. MTMRs may be involved in cancer, neuromuscular, and neurological conditions as shown through their interaction sites for MAPKs, TRAFs, and ARC. Being highly understudied, extensive experimental work is required to understand the wealth of possible functionalities of the CTDs of MTMRs, and importantly MTMRs constitute a high potential for medical and basic research.
