# Multiscale Modeling of Protein-RNA Condensation in and Out of Equilibrium


## Abstract
  A vast number of intracellular membraneless bodies also known as biomolecular condensates form through a liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of biomolecules. To date, phase separation has been identified as the main driving force for a membraneless organelles such as nucleoli, Cajal bodies, stress granules, and chromatin compartments. Recently, the protein-RNA condensation is receiving increased attention, because it is closely related to the biological function of cells such as transcription, translation, and RNA metabolism. Despite the multidisciplinary efforts put forth to study the biophysical properties of protein-RNA condensates, there are many fundamental unanswered questions regarding the mechanism of formation and regulation of protein-RNA condensates in eukaryotic cells. Major challenges in studying protein-RNA condensation stem from (i) the molecular heterogeneity and conformational flexibility of RNA and protein chains and (ii) the nonequilibrium nature of transcription and cellular environment. Computer simulations, bioinformatics, and mathematical models are uniquely positioned for shedding light on the microscopic nature of protein-RNA phase separation. To this end, there is an urgent need for innovative models with the right spatiotemporal resolution for confronting the experimental observables in a comprehensive and physics-based manner. In this chapter, we will summarize the currently emerging research efforts, which employ atomistic and coarse-grained molecular models and field theoretical models to understand equilibrium and nonequilibrium aspects of protein-RNA condensation.


## Introduction
  Experiments in the last decade have uncovered the ubiquitous presence of membraneless protein and nucleic-acid-based organelles in eukaryotic cells [1-5]. These membraneless bodies, which are also referred to as biomolecular condensates, consist of relatively dense proteins and nucleic acid formations, which are held together primarily by weak intermolecular and electrostatic interactions [6, 7]. Both nucleoplasm [2, 8, 9] and cytoplasm [10-12] have now been shown to contain condensates of varying compositions. Some prominent examples of intracellular bodies formed through protein phase separation include nuclear speckles, Cajal bodies [13], nucleoli [8, 14], and heterochromatin compartments [5, 15-17]. The discovery of membraneless organelles has also fueled a search for universal principles of cellular organization and gene regulation that go beyond the “structure centric” paradigms of biology [6, 18]. Physically, membraneless condensates resemble dynamic, liquid-like droplets ranging in size from 10 to 1000 nanometers, far bigger than just a few molecules clustered together and often exceeding in size even multicomponent biomolecular machines such as the ribosome. The testament to the liquid-like quality of these droplets is their ability to fuse, grow, and dissolve by rapidly exchanging components with the outside cellular environment in response to changing external conditions [19]. The formation and behavior of these droplets in many respects follow the principles of the liquid-liquid phase separation well known in physical chemistry and soft matter science [20]. Liquid-liquid phase separation is familiar through the ordinary experience of shaking a bottle of the vinaigrette and watching the behavior of oil droplets as they gradually coalesce, eventually leading to a phase-separated mixture. While established to be ubiquitous in cells, the functional roles of membraneless organelles are still far from being understood [21-23]. Current hypotheses revolve around diverse regulatory purposes for phase separation such as microreactors, noise reducers, and gene colocalizers [24-26].

  An important clue that sheds light on the functional roles of condensates is that proteins involved in phase separation are often partially or fully disordered with low complexity domains, which often happen to be promiscuous binders such as signaling factors and transcription factors [27-30]. This fact may explain the need for games to explicitly code protein disorder and why nearly one-third of proteins with high conserved sequences are encoding disordered states [31]. Typical, RNA-binding proteins such FUS, TDP43, Tau, hnRNPA1, and hnRNPA2 often contain more than one low complexity domain that serves as nucleating centers due to high affinity to nucleobases. The low complexity and RNA-binding domains also happen to have an innate tendency to form gelated states and self-templating fibrils. The formation of these more solid fibrillar structures from liquid compartments is closely tied to fatal neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia. The liquid compartments formed by protein-RNA LLPS, therefore, face a balanced trade-off between functionality and risk of aggregation, leading to an aberrant and irreversible phase transition into states, which are at the heart of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and presumably also other age-related and mental diseases. Molecular-level understanding of the way in which protein-protein and protein-RNA interactions fine-tune material states and phases of condensates shall offer effective strategies that can prevent protein-RNA LLPS from entering the aberrant aggregation stage.

  Finally, it is important to note that in vivo protein-RNA phase separation takes place at the backdrop of a crowded, heterogeneous, and nonequilibrium environment of nucleus or cytoplasm. Thus understanding how thermodynamically driven phase separation is coupled with biochemical reactions and time-dependent biomolecular fluxes might be key for understanding the functional dynamics of biomolecular condensates. In particular, nonequilibrium reactions can steer thermodynamically favorable states into novel phases, which can then be used for functionality or, in the case of misregulation, lead to undesirable disease-prone states. This emerging view of the connection between phase separation and gene regulation promises new perspectives for the development of new synthetic pot cellular systems and pharmaceutical strategies for combating neurodegenerative disorders.

  Computer simulations, bioinformatics, and mathematical models are uniquely positioned for shedding light on the microscopic nature of protein-RNA phase separation. To this end, there is an urgent need for innovative models with the right spatiotemporal resolution for confronting the experimental observables in a comprehensive and physics-based manner (Fig. 1). In this chapter, we provide an overview of computational models and theoretical ideas, which play increasingly more important roles in the field of biomolecular condensates as the field is beginning a shift toward quantitative predictions of biophysical properties, sequence-specific affinities, and mechanisms in vitro and in vivo condensate formation. These recent efforts encompass analytical theories [7, 32-39], field-theoretic simulations [40-42], lattice models [43], all-atom and coarse-grained simulations [44-51] including ultra coarse-grained patchy particle representations [52-54]. These different theoretical/computational approaches have been used to shed light on various aspects of biomolecular condensates such as sequence-dependent critical temperatures, the viscosity of condensates, hydrostatic pressure, the role of salt, etc. In this chapter, our primary focus would primarily be on reviewing molecular and field theoretical models, which are relevant for understanding equilibrium and nonequilibrium aspects of protein-RNA condensation.


## Materials

### Molecular Dynamics Simulations Using All-Atom and Coarse-Grained Resolution Models
  At a time when the microscopic nature of the forces which drive biomolecular phase separation is still being actively studied, the atomistic simulations provide a natural choice for scrutinizing protein-RNA interactions in condensates in great detail [55-58]. Before the emergence of the field of biomolecular condensation, atomistic studies have already provided a great deal of insight into the conformational preferences of isolated [58-62] and bound complexes of intrinsically disordered proteins [63-66]. Atomistic simulation of single amino acids with nucleic bases has also revealed the free energy landscapes of protein-RNA interactions as a function of salt concentration [67-70]. The analysis of binding free energies has shown that cation-pi interactions are to a significant extent dominating over cation-anion interactions at physiological salt concentrations. This goes to show that simple electrostatic arguments may not be sufficient to explain and accurately assess the tendency of some sequences to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation.

  As a collective phenomenon, simulating phase transitions necessitates a large number of interacting entities which in the case of biomolecules means relatively long chains of proteins and RNAs. Therefore, the study of liquid-liquid phase separation of biomolecules is more routinely done by using simplified coarse-grained models of protein and RNA chains where the solvent is ignored and polyatomic residues and bases are treated via effective interaction sites [40, 44, 45, 49, 61, 71-76]. The degree of coarse-graining is a flexible choice (Fig. 1) that is made in order to strike an optimal balance between computational efficiency and resolution of scales and phenomena of interest [77]. Because of the multiscale nature of biomolecular condensation, many studies have adopted resolutions aimed at capturing scales relevant for predicting thermodynamics and kinetic phenomena of interest including critical temperatures, binodal lines, interfacial tension, surface fluctuations, etc. Some of the notable coarse-grained models used in phase separation studies of proteins/nucleic acids include single multivalent particles [53, 54], intermediate level coarse-graining of chains into one bead per monomer resolution [44, 45, 50, 78], and near all-atom resolution MARTINI models [79]. Currently, the more common resolution appears to be the intermediate coarse-graining with a resolution of 1 coarse-grained bead per amino acid and 1 bead per nucleic acid [44, 80]. This choice has been justified a posteriori and shown to do reasonably well both in terms of capturing sequence-dependent single-chain conformational statistics as well as predicting global phases [44].

  The hydropathy scale (HPS) model of intrinsically disordered proteins has been used extensively for studying liquid-liquid phase separation of disordered proteins by the M Jeetain group [45-47]. The HPS formulation was proposed by Ashbaugh and Hach [81, 82] for capturing the correct balance of forces driving chain collapse and conformational disorder, which give rise to the observed radius of gyration. The HPS model was subsequently parametrized and extensively validated against the radius of gyration data and is parameterized to reproduce experimentally known radius of gyration scaling trends as a function of sequence and charge decoration [45-47]. The energy function of the HPS model includes short-range residue-specific interactions defined by hydropathy parameters lambda λ. The values of lambda parametrize distinct interactions between residues effectively distinguishing the nature of interactions dedicated to amino acid polarity, hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity, and aromaticity:  UHPS(r)=[ULJ(r)+(1−ϵ)λ]⋅θ(rc−r)+λULJ(r)⋅θ(r−rc)+UDH(r)  where ULJ stands for Lenard-Jones potential, θ is a Heaviside function depending on cutoff radius rc, and UDH is a Debye-Huckel potential. Recently, the group of Mittal has generalized HPS energy function to accommodate protein-RNA interactions [48]. This was done by assigning lambda values to RNA bases while keeping everything else intact. This approach while organically incorporating RNA sequence does make a stringent assumption that RNA base behaves the same way as the typically charged protein residue. While HPS is currently the more widely used formulation for simulation phase separation of proteins, it is still far from being transferable and predictive as was shown by recent work [83] on simulating phase separation propensities of Ddx4 as a function of subtle F to A and R to K potent mutations. By using the various HPS formulations, authors evaluated three different residue-residue interaction schemes and found that neither hydrophobicity scheme nor one augmented with arginine/lysinearomatic cation–π interactions consistently accounted for available experimental LLPS data on Ddx4 and its mutations [83]. On the other hand, accounting for detailed PDB-derived contact statistics in folded domains was sufficient for reproducing the overall experimental trend for wild type and mutations of Ddx4. The study also showed that fundamental understanding of forces such as solvent-mediated interactions, the role of aromatic residues, and differential interaction of charged residues that stabilize condensates are understood well enough to encode them in simple and effective coarse-grained models and, therefore, using experiment and bioinformatic derived potentials will remain crucial.

  We note that while using mean-field electrostatic terms is common in coarse-grained models such as HPS for capturing interactions between charged residues [84, 85], some studies have opted to use explicit electrostatics with plain excluded volume Lennard-Jones terms which might be more appropriate for low salt solutions of protein-RNA mixtures. The group of R Pappu has pioneered predictive implicit solvent models [80, 86, 87] for capturing conformational ensembles of intrinsically disordered proteins with an atomic resolution of proteins. The Monte Carlo simulations performed in the intrinsic solvation (IS) limit version of the temperature-dependent self-Assembly of Biomolecules Studied by an Implicit, Novel, and Tunable Hamiltonian (ABSINTH) implicit solvation have turned out to be rather useful for shedding light on the nature of sequences, which display lower versus upper critical temperatures of phase separation [88]. While attractive because of the atomic resolution, the implicit solvent models are still challenging to employ for studying phase separation as system sizes and sampling requirements make such computations expensive even for current GPU-enabled hardware.

  At the other end of the spectrum, minimalistic polyelectrolyte models of protein-RNA condensation were successfully deployed for reproducing RNA-dependent topologies of ternary multiphasic systems [50] consisting of a prion-like polypeptide (PLP), argininerich polypeptide (RRP), and RNA (Fig. 2). The main driving forces for sequence-specific phase separation trends in these minimalistic simulations are the electrostatics treated at the mean-field level, short-range attractive forces due to polar and aromatic residues, charge patterning across chains, and chain length. The functional form of minimalistic energy functions can be broken down into short-term bonded, short-term excluded volume, hybrid long–/short-term electrostatic terms accounting for charge and charge-pi interactions:  E(r)=kb(r−r0)2+kθ(θ−θ0)2+UWCA+Uqq+Uπ−π+Uq−π

  The kb, kθ are two body bonds and three-body angle elastic terms. The UWCA is a Weeks-Chandler Anderson pure repulsive term for excluded volume interactions. For the electrostatic term Uqq, Debye Huckel terms were employed with screening coefficients, however, used as a parameter for fine-turning the condensate critical temperature. Finally, the cation-pi and pi-pi interaction terms Uqπ and Uππ, which lead to lower free energy landscapes of proteins-RNA condensation, were modeled by using Lennard-Jones terms with distinct energy scales. It was shown that disparity in scale is crucial for reproducing multilayered topology of condensates in the ternary PLP-PRP-RNA system. Specifically, simulations with a minimalistic polyelectrolyte model were able to reveal the delicate interplay of sequence-specific interactions, which govern the competition between the PLP and RNA for a single shared partner, the RRP, which leads to RNA-induced demixing of PLP-RRP condensates into stable coexisting phases—homotypic PLP condensates and heterotypic RRP-RNA condensates. In this particular study, the choice of energy function using multiphasic condensates was dictated by consensus terms on one side and close comparisons with experimental data specifically on multiphasic protein-RNA systems on the other side.

  Finally, we note that stoichiometry of RNA with respect to protein components is a key variable that governs the orientational order and morphology of condensates (Fig. 3). In a recent study, it was shown that under highly charged disproportionate conditions, the micellar droplets can be converted to vesicular architecture by rapidly flowing RNAs into the solution. The mechanism resembles the osmotic shock effect seen in the growth and reshaping of charged vesicles [89] where one component penetrates within a soft micellar core driven by osmotic pressure difference to a point where it minimizes the growth of surface and surface deformation. Given the size of the systems needed for inducing vesicle formation (more than one million particles), the systematic study of mechanisms and microscopic driving forces of vesicle formation from biomolecular condensates is challenging using conventional particle-based simulations. Field theoretical methods discussed in the following sections are best positioned for investigating large length scale mesoscopic phases, which also require longer timescales.


### Field-Theoretical Models of Protein-RNA Condensation: Toward a Nonequilibrium Model of the Nucleus
  Field theoretical models are a natural choice for mapping phase diagrams of condensation along with multiple variables of interest (protein and RNA sequence, salt, temperature, pressure, etc.), which is computationally taxing even for highly coarse-grained molecular models. Field-theoretic methods, in particular based on the diffuse interface formulation, have been widely used in polymer and material sciences for studying the self-organization of polymers and alloys into morphologically distinct phases [90-92]. Recently, the phase field methods have also been finding increasing use for studying equilibrium and nonequilibrium aspects of biomolecular phase separation at mesoscopic scales [33, 41, 84, 93-99]. Development and application of field-theoretic models to study protein and RNA components offer a great opportunity to unravel the complex interplay of driving forces by focusing on underlying thermodynamics of component-wise interactions without delving into microscopic details at the level of atoms [99]. Besides the equilibrium picture of phase separation, the field theoretical approach allows one to naturally incorporate various kinetic and nonequilibrium processes such as biomolecular binding, catalytic, and synthesis reactions. An illustrative example of the utility of phase field methods for modeling mesoscopic liquid-liquid phase separation is the recent efforts of modeling nuclear chromatin organization via free energy functions encoding heterochromatin-euchromatin phase separation at the backdrop of other basic polymeric interactions [100, 101]. These studies have explained several experimental observations of nuclear chromatin dynamics couched in terms of thermodynamics and kinetics of chromatin phase separation under different time-dependent nuclear remodeling conditions [100, 101]. These applications show that phase-field models could be a valuable tool alongside more traditional polymeric simulations for examining the nature of mesoscopic biomolecular phase separation in cellular environments where nonequilibrium events become important. When it comes to protein-protein, or protein-RNA phase separation, most of the phase-field formulations to date have employed the same core free energy functional inspired by Ginzburg-Landau’s theory of heterogeneous systems [20, 102].   Once the core free energy functional is formulated, which specifies the thermodynamic landscape of interaction between components (protein, RNA, buffer, salt, etc.), one can consider adding kinetic steps such as binding/unbinding reactions, synthetic degradation of species, spatially resolved reaction-diffusion steps, etc. For instance, in a recent study, a basic phase-field model with the conventional Ginzburg-Landau free energy functional was used to examine the protein-RNA condensation scenario where protein can bind to either one or two RNA sites producing two distinct species [95]. The study showed how multiple binding of proteins to RNA governs the morphology of complexes dictated by the competition over a shared free protein. The study further showed that simple binding competition can give rise to a wide variety of spatiotemporal heterogeneity. In another study [94], authors have extended the conventional Ginzburg-Landau model of protein-protein phase separation by coupling it to experimentally derived macromolecular crowding dynamics and state-dependent reaction kinetics. The study [94] found that under high crowding concentration, there is an accelerated growth and late-stage arrest of droplet formation, effectively resulting in anomalous labyrinthine morphologies akin to protein gelation observed in experiments. The results [94] shed light on the effect of external crowding and further highlight the importance of resolving state-dependent binding/dissociation kinetics in capturing experimental trends.

  Another important problem where phase field models are especially valuable is understanding long timescale droplet coarsening dynamics. Classical coarsening theory predicts that only a single droplet remains following Ostwald ripening. However, in real cells and during finite time experiments with temperature quenching, nontrivial dynamical behaviors are observed often resulting in multidroplet and heterogeneous condensates. This is suggesting that in real cells, there could be some basic mechanism for actively suppressing Ostwald ripening. One possibility of active regulation of liquid-liquid phase separation is through enzymatic reactions and posttranslational modifications, which act as a switch of conformational states of proteins and RNA. In a recent study employing X-Ray Photon Correlation Spectroscopy [96], authors have examined droplet coarsening dynamics upon low temperature quenches. The study finds that the early stage LLPS is driven by the curvature of the free energy, which speeds up upon increasing the quench depth [96]. On the other hand, the dynamics at later stages slows down dramatically with increasing quench depth, hinting at the proximity of the glass transition point. Through phase-field simulations, the authors found that model C formulation best describes the observed behavior. Furthermore, they find that accounting for viscoelasticity and density-dependent mobility of condensate phases are crucial for reproducing the dynamics of the system. To explain the mechanisms of active suppression of droplet coarsening, Paul Bressloff has developed a general theoretical framework that goes beyond mean-field theory predicting single droplet steady-state by asymptotically deriving leading-order conditions for the existence and stability of a multidroplet steady state [105]. Understanding material nature and characterizing microbiological, viscoelastic properties of condensates is currently actively studied [106-108] and will be crucial for validation and furthering the development of phase-field methods into predive tools.

  Protein-RNA phase separation appears at the very heart of transcriptional regulation, and there have been several studies that aim to shed light on the possible functional mechanisms of coupling of phase separation and transcription [41, 97, 109-112]. Transcription is fundamentally a nonequilibrium process where every stage of transcription could potentially be under dynamically regulated feedback through its RNA products. To model the coupling of protein-RNA condensates formed by transcriptional RNA and regulatory proteins, one must look into nonequilibrium and spatially resolved reaction-diffusion models. Here the phase-field formulation provides a natural framework allowing one to study nonequilibrium transcriptional events within the bimolecular solution, the phase separation thermodynamics of which is governed by Ginzburg-Landau free-energy formulation.

  In the work by Laghmach et al. [41], a minimal model of nucleoplasm was proposed based on the idea of protein-mRNA phase separation. In the model protein-RNA phase separation is coupled to the most basic step of spatially localized transcription which takes place at a transcription site with finite lifetimes of protein and RNA constituents (Fig. 4). The study has revealed a broad influence of timescale over the emergent condensate topologies. Specifically, it was shown that the underlying free energy landscape still dictates most of the nature of condensate over finite timescales; kinetic processes can lead to completely inverted topologies compared to the equilibrium picture. In a similar vein, a recent joint experimental and computational study looked at [97] the roles of RNAs in transcriptional control. The study proposed a nonequilibrium feedback mechanism where low levels of RNA promote condensates formed by electrostatic interactions, whereas relatively high levels promote dissolution of these condensates. The studies on the coupling of biomolecular condensation and nonequilibrium biochemical reactions are still very much understandable due to the complexity of dynamical scenarios, which are possible once one leaves the comfortable domain of thermodynamics. And yet it is important to develop models of biomolecular condensation under conditions that are mimicking nuclear and cytoplasmic environments because very likely the many functional roles of phase separations could only then be fully understood.
