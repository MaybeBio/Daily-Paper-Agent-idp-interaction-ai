# Structure Activity Relationships of Novel Tau Ligands: Passive Fibril Binders and Active Aggregation Inhibitors


## Abstract
  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) are core components of many biological processes and are central players in several pathologies. Despite being important drug targets, attempts to design small molecule ligands that would help understand and attenuate their behavior are frustrated by the structural diversity exhibited by these flexible proteins. To accommodate the dynamic nature of IDPs, we developed a procedure that efficiently identifies active small-molecule ligands for disordered proteins. By exploring chemical space around these ligands, we refined their effect on aggregation and identified molecular features critical for activity and affinity. Notably, the discovery of this new family of disordered protein ligands was achieved more quickly and with less expense than conventional high-throughput screening or docking alone would have allowed. Resulting ligands include tau aggregation inhibitors as well as at least one compound that binds fibrils potently but does not appear to perturb the extent of kinetics of aggregation.


## Experimental Procedures

### Chemicals.
  Experimental compounds were purchased from either Chembridge or Enamine, detailed in supplementary table 1. Stocks were made in DMSO. All other reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to confirm that under experimental conditions, artifacts due to compound insolubility were unlikely to contribute to observed activity (Figure S5). DLS assays of compound aggregation were performed using a DynaPro NanoStar laser photometer and accompanying software. For these experiments, DMSO control and compound samples were incubated overnight at room temperature or 37 °C and then assayed.


### Protein Expression and Purification.
  Plasmids for His-tagged full length tau2N4R and His-tagged tau4RD, each with a TEV cleavage site separating the His tag from the rest of the construct, were gifts from the Rhoades lab at the University of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia, PA). These plasmids were transfected into two Escheria coli lines: DH-5α for long-term stocks and BL-21 for protein expression. Protein was expressed by incubating transfected BL21 (DE3) cells in 1L of Luria-Bertani (LB) medium in baffled flasks. Cells were grown at 37 °C and 220 rpm in a New Brunswick Innova 44/44R incubator until the OD reached 0.6–0.7. After reaching the indicated OD, Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside was added to a final concentration of 0.4 mM; the temperature was decreased to 16 °C, and the culture was allowed to grow overnight. The following day, cells were collected by centrifuging the solution and resuspending the resultant pellet in lysis buffer [50 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole (pH 8) at 4°C]. Cells were then frozen and stored at −80°C until the protein was purified. To purify cells, they were first brought to room temperature, and Halt Protease inhibitor cocktail (Life Technologies; 300 μL), 0.1 mg/mL DNase, 0.1 mg/mL RNase, and 300 μL of a saturated solution of phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride in ethanol were added per liter of growth medium before lysis with a French press. The lysate was then centrifuged at 2800g for 45 min and the supernatant collected. This supernatant was passed through 0.8 and 0.4 μm syringe filters before being loaded onto a 5 mL Ni-NTA agarose column. The protein was eluted from the column by increasing the concentration of imidazole to 250 mM, and the eluent was buffer exchanged back to 10 mM imidazole during an overnight incubation with 240uL of 2.8 mg/mL TEV protease at 4 °C. The cleaved protein was separated from the solution by running the solution over the Ni-NTA column and collecting the flowthrough. The flowthrough was then concentrated to 1-2 mL using a 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filter. This concentrated solution was then fractionated using a 25 mL S200 extended gel-filtration column with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP (pH 7.4) as the mobile phase. Protein purity was confirmed by running samples on SDS-PAGE gels. Concentrations of protein were determined by using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein was concentrated, aliquoted, flash-frozen, then stored at −80 °C until used.


### Aggregation Assays.
  All aggregation assays were performed 37 °C and pH 7.4 in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. Thioflavin T (ThT) is commonly used as a probe to monitor amyloid fibril formation.21, 22 For ThT absorbance assays 100 μL aliquots of tau4RD and ThT at twice the desired final concentration were prepared in blackwalled polystyrene 96-well plates (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA). Equal volumes of unfractionated ~3 kDa heparin sodium (Acros Organics, Morris Plains, NJ) at twice the desired final concentration were added to the wells to initiate the reactions. Unless otherwise specified, final concentrations of protein and heparin were 5 and 3 μM respectively. Reaction progress was monitored by the increase in ThT fluorescence with a λex of 440 nm and a λem of 485 nm in a BioTek (Winooski, VT) Synergy HTX plate reader. Reads were taken every 5 min after agitation for 1 min (for tau4RD) or every 10 min with continuous agitation (for full-length tau). The intrinsic fluorescence from compounds was in all cases ≤5% of the background, for which we corrected by baseline subtraction. Experiments were performed with at least three technical replicates.


### Tyrosine Fluorescence Assays.
  Tau4RD contains a single native tyrosine, which we used as an intrinsic spectroscopic probe of concentration. Tyrosine fluorescence assays were performed to measure the tau4RD concentration in solution using an Agilent Cary Eclipse spectrophotometer. Samples were excited at 276 nm, and the emission spectra was measured from 300 to 310 nm. The area under the curve between 300 and 310 was used to quantify emission. A standard curve was created using varying concentrations of Tau4RD in the aggregation buffer described above. Samples were incubated in the same was as the aggregation assays described above, except that duplicate samples were made without ThT. Initial concentrations were measured immediately after the addition of heparin. Additionally, after incubation for 3 h, samples were centrifuged (30 min at 21100g) at4 °C and the tyrosine fluorescence of tau in the supernatant was measured. Each experiment was performed with three technical replicates, and the results were averaged. The amount of aggregation was defined as the difference between the initial quantification and the amount of tau in the supernatant after 3 h.


### Determination of compound 6 concentrations.
  Compound 6 concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 248 nm. Compound 6’s absorbance is sufficiently distinguishable from background absorbance of buffer and Tau4RD. Absorbance of samples was recorded between 195 and 345 nm using a Cary 3E spectrophotometer. The reference cell contained 1% DMSO in buffer. Raw absorbance data were baseline adjusted such that the 345 nm absorbance was set to 0. A standard curve was constructed using buffer-matched samples of compound 6 and used to determine soluble compound levels. Additional controls containing heparin and compound in the absence of protein were compared against compound-only controls to affirm that heparin did not influence the apparent absorbance of the compound.


### Gel Densitometry.
  Gel Densitometry was used as an orthogonal means to quantify soluble protein. After three hours of aggregation as detailed in the aggregation assay method, samples were spun down and 15 μL of supernatant loaded and run on SDS-PAGE. Gels were then scanned with a Li-Cor Odyssey CLx gel-scanner and band intensity was determined using Fiji.23


### Computational Docking.
  Three-dimensional models of each compound were downloaded from the Pubchem chemical library and then converted to Autodock’s pdbqt format using OpenBabel and Autodock Tools.24–27 Compounds were then docked against all 250 peptide targets that were established in our previous work, and using the same docking protocol. In brief: 9-10 residue segments of Tau4RD were isolated and highly populated conformations of these segments were identified using enhanced sampling molecular dynamics and conformational clustering methods. These populated conformations served as molecular docking targets and docking scores for each compound were generated for each representative structure using Autodock Vina.27


### Estimating Fibril-binding Affinity.
  We derived an equation to describe the equilibrium between soluble and aggregate-bound states of a drug such as compound 6 as follows. Let drug C bind to the soluble states S of a protein (whether monomeric or oligomeric) with dissociation constant Ks:  (1)S+C⇌SC;Ks=[S][C][SC]  Let drug C also bind to the insoluble fibrillar states F of a protein with dissociation constant Kf:  (2)F+C⇌FC;Kf=[F][C][FC]  Aggregation assays were performed with varying concentrations of tau4RD (5 μM, 10 μM) and 3KDa heparin (0, 1.5, 3, 6 μM), along with 10 µM Compound 6. Samples were incubated overnight at 37ºC, at which point the concentration of fibrillar material had stabilized. This yielded samples with varying amounts of tau4RD fibril formation. Crucially, the fraction f of drug that binds to fibrils and co-precipitates when centrifuged can be used to infer the affinity of drug for fibrils, based on the following system of equations:  (3)Ctot=[C]+[SC]+[FC]=[C](1+[S]Ks+[F]Kf)   (4)Ftot=[F]+[FC]=[F](1+[C]Kf)   (5)Ptot=[S]+[SC]+Ftot=[S](1+[C]Ks)+[F](1+[C]Kf)   (6)f=[FC]Ctot  Here, the total amount of protein Ptot and the total amount of drug Ctot are known a priori, and the concentration of fibrillar protein Ftot as well as f are determined experimentally. This system of equations is analytically challenging without the use of simplifying assumptions. Instead, numerical simulations implemented in Copasi 28 were used to estimate the best-fit values of the two free parameters Kf and Ks.

  In order to robustly estimate the uncertainty in the resulting parameter values, we employed a resampling protocol. Briefly, 100 decoy f vs. Ftot data sets were generated based on the best-fit Kf and Ks values, with random noise added at the same level observed in the individual data. The decoy data sets were then refit to the model defined by equations 3–6, providing an ensemble of Kf and Ks values that accurately reflects model uncertainty.


## Results
  Our pilot work identified two compounds of interest, designated compound 1 and compound 6 (Figure 1).18 Compound 1 decreases and delays fluorescence in a ThT-based Tau4RD aggregation assay, which suggests it inhibits the aggregation of Tau4RD. In contrast, compound 6 increased the amount of fluorescence in that assay. When probed further with orthogonal methods, we showed that compound 1 was genuinely decreasing the amount of aggregation, but compound 6’s increase in fluorescence in the ThT assay was likely photophysical in origin. In this work we probe the structure-activity relationship of compound 1 with chemical analogs. Then we investigate the interaction between Compound 6 and Tau4RD and finally we explore the chemical space surrounding that compound.

  Compounds from the Chembridge and Enamine chemical libraries that shared chemical similarities to parts or all of compound 1 were purchased and examined with computational and in vitro methods. (Figure 1). The 6 rings of compound 1 have been labeled to facilitate discussion of chemical differences between analogs. Compounds 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3 share similarity with the “left” half of compound 1, consisting of the A, B, and C rings, but lack the remaining three. Compound 1.4 and 1.5 are very similar to the lead compound, differing only in the substituents of the B-ring. In place of a phenyl ring (ring A), Compound 1.4 has a cyclohexenyl moiety, and compound 1.5 has two methyl groups. Finally, Compounds 1.6, 1.7, and 1.8 probe the impact of the ring systems on the opposite end of the molecule (rings D, E, and F). Contrasting the activities of compounds 1.6 and 1.7 against compound 1.4 provide insight to rings E and F, as they are otherwise identical to compound 1.4. Compound 1.8, when compared to Compound 1, provides information on the importance of the tetrazole ring (ring E) of the lead compound. This specifically informs on the impact of the tetrazole as compound 1.8 differs from compound 1 by a single nitrogen to carbon substitution in that ring. To assess the predicted behavior of these compounds, we used the same computational screening procedure that was used to identify compound 1 (Supplementary Table 1). Computational results predicted most of the compounds to bind in a similar position as the lead compound (Compound 1) with good docking scores.

  After identifying these compounds their effects on aggregation were tested using an established Thioflavin T assay and their activity was compared to vehicle control and the aggregation in the presence of Compound 1. Apart from the lead compound, only compounds 1.4, 1.5 and 1.7 had notable and consistent activity when compared to vehicle control. For the compounds that showed activity, this activity was verified with both dose-dependence experiments as well as orthogonal methods of examining aggregation. Compound 1.7 showed a direct relationship between dose and effect on aggregation as shown by ThT fluorescence assays, while the dose response for 1.4 and 1.5 was somewhat less consistent. (Figure 2: B–D). All four compounds inhibit the aggregation of the full-length tau construct (Supplementary Figure 1). Tyrosine fluorescence assays of protein in solution corroborate the ThT data and show that compounds 1, 1.4, 1.5 and 1.7 inhibit amyloid formation of Tau4RD (Supplementary Figure 2), and gel densitometry confirmed the activity of compounds 1 and 1.7 (Supplementary Figure 3).

  We sought to determine how Compound 6 was amplifying the fluorescence of the ThT assay. It appears to bind to the Tau4RD aggregate and increase the fluorescence of ThT through that interaction, but we could not discount the possibility that it somehow amplifies the ThT fluorescence without binding Tau4RD . To establish this, we first needed to know if the compound was associating with aggregated Tau4RD. We determined the amount of compound 6 in solution by measuring the solution’s absorbance at 248 nm. By comparing the amount in the supernatants of samples that were induced to aggregate with heparin to those that did not aggregate establish that samples with Tau4RD aggregation had notably depleted concentrations of compound 6 (Figure 3).

  To expand upon this study, two amounts of initial Tau4RD were used (5µM and 10 µM) in combination with a variety of heparin concentrations. Increasing amounts of heparin increase the amount of aggregation and therefore precipitated protein at the end of the assay (Figure 4). Varying the starting protein concentration further yielded differing ratios of soluble to fibrillar Tau4RD. This information combined with quantification of the amount of compound lost from solution due to association with the fibrillar tau allowed us to estimate the affinity of Compound 6 towards Tau4RD fibrils. A resampling protocol was necessary in order to accurately estimate the uncertainty in recovered parameters. The affinity of Compound 6 binding fibrils (Kf) was 0.31 μM (95% CI: 0.21–1.0 μM). However, the resolution of our data was not sufficient to confidently establish the value of Ks, the affinity of Compound 6 for soluble Tau4RD (95% CI: 0.01–36 μM).

  Once we established that Compound 6 was a genuine ligand of tau aggregate, we sought out other potential ligands related to it. We obtained 3 compounds chemically similar to compound 6 and tested them with our ThT assay (Figure 5). All of the compounds showed a notable increase in ThT fluorescence, but like Compound 6, gel densitometry showed that they were not affecting the amount of aggregation occurring and the increase of ThT fluorescence is likely a photophysical effect.


## Discussion
  Disordered proteins are a growing focus as they are increasingly shown to be involved in number of diseases, but their disordered nature frustrates attempts to design ligands to modulate their activity in disease states. Their disordered nature obfuscates structure-based ligand design as well as assays often used to assess ligand binding. We utilized computational methods in our previous work to accelerate the search for small molecule ligands of Tau4RD. Our computational efforts led us to 10 compounds, of which 2 showed interesting activity in our initial ThT screening. We investigated these compounds and found that Compound 1 was inhibiting the aggregation of Tau4RD, and Compound 6 was not changing the aggregation, but was amplifying the fluorescence. This work sought to expand our understanding of both compounds.

  We investigated the chemical space surrounding the aggregation inhibitor compound identified in our computational work, Compound 1. With this lead compound we were able to identify a diverse set of chemically related compounds and tested them with our computational method and ThT fluorescence assay. The compounds related to compound 1 had a diverse range of scores, with the highest scores concentrated around two segments of the peptide. This information suggests that targeting segments 3 and 21 may lead to the identification of more active compounds. When we obtained the compounds and tested them with our ThT assay, we were able to identify the contributions of different parts of Compound 1. Compounds 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3 had no activity and thus illustrated that the A, B, and C rings of Compound 1 are not sufficient for activity. Compounds 1.4 and 1.5 show that the substituents extending from ring B are not critical for activity but do influence it. Those two compounds have consistent activity, but are less effective at slowing aggregation than the lead compound. In our computational studies, these compounds were predicted to have a higher affinity to Tau4RD than compound 1, but the ThT traces suggest that their effect is weaker. It is unclear whether the lower potency is due to weaker binding, or a difference in binding mode of these compounds relative to compound 1 that decreases their effects on aggregation. It is likely that steric hindrance is a factor, and the B-ring substituents alter how well the compounds bind to Tau4RD.

  Furthermore, the effects of compounds it appears as though the tetrazole (Ring E) is critical and the phenyl ring (Ring F) is detrimental. These results show that once a lead compound is found, classical medicinal chemistry approaches such a deciphering structure-activity-relationships (SAR) can be used to refine ligand activity, even for disordered proteins for which characterizing the “structure” of the protein/ligand complex is difficult or impossible.

  Our results show that although compound 6 does not alter the aggregation of Tau4RD, it does bind to the aggregate and amplify the fluorescence of ThT as evidenced by the loss in absorbance at 248 nm (Figure 5). As the amount of aggregated tau varies (based on the amounts of heparin and protein used), the ratio of compound loss to aggregate provides insight towards the compound’s affinity towards Tau4RD. In order to estimate the affinity of compound 6 towards Tau4RD, we needed to first develop a model that did not depend on knowing the amount of compound associated with soluble protein. Our model is based on the minimal assumption that the association of compound 6 to Tau4RD reaches equilibrium by the time we examine it, and that compound bound to the fibrillar species is spun down with the aggregated protein. This leaves the amount in solution the sum of the unbound compound and the compound that is bound to soluble species of Tau4RD. Samples of compound 6 without any fibrillar tau (prepared by incubating them without any heparin) behaved the same as samples with no tau, and neither displayed any loss of compound (data not shown). Our model takes these basic assumptions and manipulates them (detailed in the Methods section) to estimate the affinity of compound 6 to Tau4RD. Comparing the data to simulated curves made by calculating the expected amount of compound pulled down for given affinities towards fibrillar Tau4RD suggests that the affinity is in the high nanomolar range (Supplementary Figure 3). The resolution of the data is not sufficient to make any determinations about the relative affinities of the compound towards soluble forms of Tau4RD.

  With this we have shown that the original computational screen was successful when it identified Compound 6 as a potential Tau4RD ligand in our previous work. The original goal of that search was to identify compounds that bind to tau regardless of if they change tau’s aggregation behaviors. To instead specify the search for compounds that affect aggregation, we would need a better understanding of what areas of Tau4RD drive aggregation. This would enable us to limit our search to those regions, searching for compounds that interfere with the intramolecular interactions that drive aggregation. When we searched the chemical space near Compound 6, we found compounds with similar activity and as such developed a novel class of amyloid ligand. Additionally, when we look at the docking profiles of Compound 6 and its analogs, we see that while they seem to have a preferred location on Tau4RD to dock onto, they can viably dock across many segments of the protein. If these docking predictions reflect the genuine binding position of the ligands detailed in our studies, they add to a list of active compounds that display affinity to disordered proteins without a singular binding site or conformation. It’s possible that even these weak interactions could cause a population shift that alters the behavior of disordered proteins in pathological settings.29, 30

  Recent cryo-electron microscopy studies have yielded valuable insights into the structure and polymorphism of tau fibrils,31,32 both generated in vitro33 and observed in vivo in a variety of dementias.34–40 The diversity of pathological tau aggregates speaks to the complexity of potential aggregation pathways. In general, the preferentially sampled locally structured states derived from ReSA-MD and predicted to be druggable based on docking calculations do not strongly resemble motifs from any of the deposited tau fibril structures (Supplementary Figure 6). The most notable exception is segment 21, which broadly resembles a hairpin observed in Pick’s disease fibrils,35 with a Cα root-mean-square deviation of 1.62Å. The lack of strong similarity between modeled monomeric and fibrillar states speaks to the dramatic conformational changes that tau undergoes during aggregation, and is consistent with the idea that targeting different stages of the aggregation pathway will yield aggregation modulators with distinct biochemical activities and specificities.

  This work took the next logical step in ligand development after identifying lead compounds. We explored the chemical space surrounding our lead compounds using computational and in vitro methods and discovered more active compounds. Much like attempts that were made before high resolution protein structures were readily available, the activity and structure of the ligands guides our understanding of their interactions without the need for high resolution representations of binding interactions.
