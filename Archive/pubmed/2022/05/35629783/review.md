## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (review article)

- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors review the role of intrinsically disordered proteins and regions (IDPs/IDPRs) in autophagy, focusing on their interactions with membranes. They describe mechanisms including membrane binding via coupled electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, disorder-to-order transitions forming amphipathic helices, membrane anchoring via post-translational modifications, and membrane remodeling orchestrated by extended disordered conformations. They also discuss unresolved mechanisms for several autophagy proteins.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This is a well-structured and informative review that consolidates current knowledge on the interface between intrinsically disordered proteins and membranes in autophagy. The authors present a coherent narrative organized by mechanistic themes, which is useful for readers seeking an entry point into this specialized area. The review is comprehensive in scope and highlights important open questions. However, the absence of figures and table limits the ability to fully evaluate the clarity and accuracy of the presented information. The review would benefit from a more critical synthesis of the evidence, including explicit discussion of conflicting findings or gaps in direct experimental support for some proposed mechanisms.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of autophagy, membrane biology, and intrinsically disordered proteins would find this review valuable. It provides a focused summary of a niche intersection that is not commonly covered in a single article. Cell biologists studying membrane dynamics, biochemists interested in protein-lipid interactions, and structural biologists working on disordered proteins may also benefit. Clinicians or translational researchers with an interest in autophagy-related diseases might use this as background reading, though the review does not emphasize therapeutic implications.
- **Major strengths** The review fills a specific niche by focusing exclusively on the IDP-membrane interface in autophagy, which is not comprehensively covered elsewhere. The organization by mechanistic themes (binding, folding, modification, remodeling) is logical and aids readability. The authors include both well-established examples and recent findings, and they explicitly identify unresolved questions, which is valuable for guiding future research. The inclusion of evolutionary comparisons, such as the conserved BARA domain mechanism, adds depth.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Evidence quality
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that the Atg6/BECN1 BARA domain utilizes a disordered loop with an aromatic triad (FFW) that inserts into the lipid bilayer and mediates membrane binding, and that this mechanism is evolutionarily conserved from yeast to human.
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 2, Figure 2
  - **Concern** The claim of evolutionary conservation is based on two studies (references 19 and 20) that show similar membrane-binding regions in human and yeast. However, the text does not discuss whether the functional consequences of these interactions have been directly compared across species, or whether the structural context of the loop differs between the two orthologs. The term "evolutionarily conserved" implies a degree of functional equivalence that may not be fully supported by the cited evidence.
  - **Why it matters** Overstating conservation could mislead readers into assuming that findings from yeast models directly translate to human systems, which is not always the case for membrane-interacting proteins. A more nuanced discussion of the comparative evidence would strengthen the claim.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should clarify whether direct comparative experiments have been performed, or alternatively, temper the claim to state that similar mechanisms have been observed in both species without asserting full evolutionary conservation.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Completeness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that the Atg20 PX-GAP is longer than that in Vam7 and Snx4, and that it "is plausible to assume" the long PX-GAP adopts additional membrane-inducible structures to allow phosphoinositide-binding pockets to reach PtdIns3P.
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 2, Figure 3A
  - **Concern** The authors present a speculative hypothesis without discussing alternative explanations or the limitations of the sequence alignment on which the length comparison is based. The functional significance of the longer PX-GAP is not experimentally tested, and the text does not acknowledge that the length difference could be unrelated to membrane binding.
  - **Why it matters** Speculation is acceptable in a review, but it should be clearly framed as such and accompanied by a discussion of alternative possibilities. As written, the statement may be read as a more established conclusion than the evidence warrants.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should explicitly label this as a hypothesis and briefly discuss other possible functions of the extended loop, or note what experiments would distinguish between these possibilities.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Evidence quality
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that the Atg13 IDPR is dephosphorylated at multiple sites upon starvation, and that this may facilitate release from Vac8 and enhance membrane binding, functioning as a "fast mechanistic switch."
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 2, Figure 4E,F
  - **Concern** The text acknowledges that "direct evidence still needs to be acquired" for the dephosphorylation mechanism, yet the subsequent discussion presents the switch model as a plausible explanation without clearly separating the speculative nature of this model from the established facts. The relationship between dephosphorylation, Vac8 release, and membrane binding is presented as a coherent narrative, but the causal links are not experimentally established.
  - **Why it matters** The switch model is an interesting hypothesis, but presenting it without sufficient caveats could lead readers to overestimate the strength of the evidence. This is particularly important because the model has implications for how the field thinks about regulation of the Atg1 complex.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should more clearly distinguish between established findings (e.g., dephosphorylation occurs) and the proposed functional consequences, and explicitly state which steps of the model remain untested.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Completeness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that the Atg23 dimerization and vesicle tethering are "spatiotemporally coupled events" and that a disordered loop undergoes a disorder-to-order transition upon dimerization, forming an amphipathic helix and positioning residues for membrane binding.
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 6, Figure 11
  - **Concern** The text presents this as a coherent model, but the evidence cited (reference 99) appears to show that a dimerization-deficient mutant has decreased helical content and is partially deficient in membrane binding. The causal relationship between dimerization, helix formation, and membrane binding is inferred rather than directly demonstrated. The text does not discuss whether the observed effects could be due to other structural changes caused by the mutation.
  - **Why it matters** The model is presented with more certainty than the evidence supports. A more cautious interpretation would acknowledge that the coupling mechanism is one possible explanation among several.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either present the model as a working hypothesis with explicit caveats, or discuss alternative interpretations of the mutant data.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the Atg6/BECN1 BARA membrane-binding mechanism is "evolutionarily conserved from yeast to human" is supported by two studies but may overstate the degree of functional equivalence without direct comparative experiments.
- The hypothesis that the longer Atg20 PX-GAP adopts additional membrane-inducible structures is speculative and not experimentally tested.
- The proposed "fast mechanistic switch" model for Atg13 involving dephosphorylation, Vac8 release, and membrane binding is presented as a coherent narrative, but direct evidence for the causal links is lacking.
- The statement that Atg23 dimerization and vesicle tethering are "spatiotemporally coupled events" is an interpretation of mutant data that may have alternative explanations.
- The physiological relevance of Atg17 membrane association observed in overexpression experiments is not established.
