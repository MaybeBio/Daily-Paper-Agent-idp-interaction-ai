# Optogenetic control of GGGGCC repeat-containing RNA phase transition


## Abstract
  The GGGGCC (G4C2) hexanucleotide repeat expansion in the C9ORF72 gene is a major cause of both hereditary amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and familial frontotemporal dementia. Recent studies have shown that G4C2 hexanucleotide repeat-containing RNA transcripts ((G4C2)n RNA) could go through liquid-liquid phase separation to form RNA foci, which may elicit neurodegeneration. However, the direct causality between these abnormal RNA foci and neuronal toxicity remains to be demonstrated. Here we introduce an optogenetic control system that can induce the assembly and phase separation of (G4C2)n RNA foci with blue light illumination in human cells, by fusing a specific (G4C2)n RNA binding protein as the linker domain to Cry2, a protein that oligomerizes in response to blue light. Our results demonstrate that a higher number of G4C2 repeats have the potential to be induced into more RNA foci in the cells. Both spontaneous and induced RNA foci display liquid-like properties according to FRAP measurements. Computational simulation shows strong consistency with the experimental results and supports the effect of our system to promote the propensity of (G4C2)n RNA towards phase separation. This system can thus be used to investigate whether (G4C2)n RNA foci would disrupt normal cellular processes and lead to pathological phenotypes relevant to repeat expansion disorders.


## Introduction
  Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is one of the major neurodegenerative diseases. ALS’ main symptom is progressive muscle atrophy, which eventually leads to a lethal respiratory failure in most patients. With the pathogenic mechanism not yet clear, there is a lack of effective clinical treatments for ALS patients. Expansions of nucleotide repeats serve as disease-causing mutations for several common neurological disorders [1]. Being identified in more than 40% of familial ALS and about 5% to 20% of sporadic ALS [2], the G4C2 hexanucleotide repeat expansion of the C9ORF72 (chromosome 9 open reading frame 72) gene is the most common genetic cause of ALS [2], [3], [4]. In normal individuals, the G4C2 sequence is only repeated 2 to 5 times, and no more than 25 times [5]. In C9orf72-mutated ALS patients, the sequence can be repeated hundreds to thousands of times. The same G4C2 repeat expansions are also commonly found in many patients with familial frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [2], [3], [4], [5]. Likewise, the expanded CAG repeats in the HTT gene are responsible for Huntington's disease.

  Besides the hypothesis that mutant proteins confer neurotoxicity, another potential mechanism of pathogenesis focuses on the accumulation of repeat-containing RNA into aberrant condensates [4], [5]. It is hypothesized that these RNA aggregates bind and sequester RNA-binding proteins (mostly transcriptional regulators), leading to functional disruption and dysregulation of transcription or translation, ultimately resulting in neurotoxicity. In neurons from the prefrontal cortex and spinal cord of C9orf72-mutated ALS patients, the G4C2 repeat expansion was shown to form a liquid-like droplet of RNA aggregates (RNA foci). Such RNA foci, assembling through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) [2,6], may elicit neurodegeneration. But the causal relationship between RNA foci formation and neurotoxicity has not yet been fully established [6]. A technique that could manipulate the intracellular RNA phase transitions would be essential to observe the immediate effects of RNA foci on cellular functions.

  Systems that control phase separation of proteins have been reported previously, such as optoDroplets [7]. To design an optogenetic control system that can induce the formation of RNA foci intracellularly, we take advantage of the light sensitivity of Arabidopsis thaliana cryptochrome 2 (Cry2), which could assemble quickly and form puncta in response to blue light [8], [9], [10]. Upon the excitation wavelength of 405-488 nm, the Cry2 protein oligomerizes through its PHR domain, forming liquid-like droplets in cells [7]. Cry2 aggregates have the property of dissociating reversibly when being switched back to the dark, which makes Cry2 an important member of the optogenetics toolbox [9] and allows us to use it as a tool protein for studying RNA phase separation. The Cry2-oligo has been utilized in previous optogenetic studies on phase-separation of proteins to optimize the oligomerization. Cry2-oligo is the E490G variant of wild-type Cry2 and an enhanced light-induced assembler of the latter, providing rapid, robust, and reversible protein oligomerization in response to the blue light [9]. In this study, we tested the possibility to establish an optogenetic control system based on Cry2-oligo (Cry2 in the main text hereafter) that can induce the assembly and phase separation of (G4C2)n RNA foci with blue light illumination in human cells.


## Materials and methods

### Materials
  Plasmid constructs: The CRY2olig-mCherry plasmid (#60032), the pHR-Tre3G-29xGGGGCC-12xMS2 plasmid (#99149) and the pHR-tdMCP-YFP plasmid (#99151) were obtained from Addgene. The Pur α cDNA was amplified from the mouse cDNA library and cloned into Nhe I/Xho I sites of CRY2olig-mCherry through restriction enzyme digestion and homologous recombination. The 5 × G4C2-MS2 plasmid was generated through transformation of pHR-Tre3G-29xGGGGCC-12xMS2 in DH5α Competent Cells. All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing.


### Methods

#### Cells culture and transfection
  HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11965) with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10082-147) and maintained at 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. For transient transfections, the plasmids were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11668019) according to the manufacturer's instructions when the confluence of cells reached 70%. To induce the expression of G4C2 RNA, a plasmid encoding rtTA was co-transfected and doxycycline was added in 1,000 ng ml−1 after transfection.


#### Live cell imaging
  For live cell imaging, HEK293T cells were seeded in a culture dish with glass bottom and transfected with indicated plasmids. Cells were transferred 48 h after transfection to a 37°C live-cell imaging chamber supplemented with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. Cells were imaged using an Andor Dragonfly high speed confocal microscope under a 60 × oil immersion objective. For RNA foci assembly, cells were photographed every 5 s for 6 min in total. Collected data were processed with Imaris Viewer and FIJI. RNA foci were identified and counted using the FIJI 3D Objects Counter plugin.


#### FRAP
  Since previous studies have shown that the fluorescence recovery of MS2CP–YFP can be used to represent the RNA dynamics [9], we bleached MS2CP–YFP protein to perform FRAP experiments of (G4C2)n RNA. An entire punctum was photobleached by 100% laser intensity for 1 s and the fluorescence recovery was monitored by time-lapse imaging, which collected images every 1.4 s. The fluorescence intensity of bleached regions was analyzed using the FIJI Time Series Analyzer plugin and normalized to their initial intensity before photobleaching. The signals were normalized to unbleached puncta in the same cells. The recovery curve figure was generated by GraphPad Prism 8.


#### Caspase-3 activity measurements
  For the caspase-3 activity assay, the NucView 488 caspase-3 dye (Biotium, #30029) was used for the caspase activity detection reporting on the apoptosis signals. The culture medium of HEK293T cells were replaced by serum-removed Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #31985) 24 h after transfection. The Incucyte system takes images of 9 different fields each well with a 3 h interval of 48 h in total. For each transfected condition, 3 independent wells were imaged and analyzed. The green fluorescent puncta were identified and counted using the Incucyte 2011A software. The fluorescent object count normalized by the cell confluence served as caspase-3 signals.


#### Computational simulation
  To model our system, we simplified it to a ternary solution with solvent A, polymer B ((G4C2)n RNA) and polymer C (Pur α-Cry2) and analyzed the Gibbs free energy of mixing per lattice of this system. We utilized the two-dimension lattice model used in the Flory-Huggins mean field theory, which assumes that each identical subunit of the polymer or one solvent molecule occupies one lattice site and the connected subunits of the polymer are arranged in adjacent lattice sites [11]. Since B and C are supposed to partition together, we set the mole fraction of the solvent as φ and see ΔGmix as the function of φ and a for simplicity, where a is the mole fraction of B in polymers (A and B). Based on the mean field approximation (which simplifies the effect of the other individuals on any single individual by a single average effect), the quasi-chemical model (which assumes that the heat of mixing is only due to the bond energies between adjacent atoms) [12] and the Flory-Huggins mean field theory (which is used to calculate the entropy of mixing of polymer solutions) [11], we can derive the ΔGmix of this system per lattice site:ΔGmix=kBT[logφ+a(1−φ)log[a(1−φ)]NB+(1−a)(1−φ)log[(1−a)(1−φ)]NC+χABaφ(1−φ)+χAC(1−a)φ(1−φ)+χBCa(1−a)(1−φ)2]wherekB is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature, NB and NC are the number of subunits in one polymer molecule of B and C, respectively, which are set as the number of amino acid or the nucleic acid of the Pur α-Cry2 protein or the (G4C2)n RNA, and χAB is the difference between the A-B bond energy and the average of the A-A and B-B bond energies (similar with χBC and χAC). If χ<0, the atoms in the solution will prefer to be surrounded by atoms of another type, whereas, if χ>0, atoms of the same type favor to stay together.

  We changed the value of NB, NC, χAB, χBC and χAC to represent different conditions of the system. When the G4C2 repeats of (G4C2)n RNAs increase, both NB and χAB are larger, denoting its increasing length and length-dependent interaction. When Pur α-Cry2 is substituted by Cry2, NC decreases. Since Cry2 features light-induced self-interaction, χAC is set as 2 with light and 0 without light. While χAB and χAC are positive, representing the tendency of RNAs and proteins for self-assembly against the solvent, χBC is negative, which corresponds to the co-partition assumption of B and C. Especially, since Pur α-Cry2 specifically binds long (G4C2)n RNA, χBC is of larger absolute values for 29 × (G4C2) RNA than for 5 × (G4C2) RNA. #### Statistical analysis
  Welch's t test was used for the comparison of foci number between the 29 × (G4C2) and 5 × (G4C2) groups. P<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Statistical analyses were carried out using Excel and GraphPad Prism 8.


## Results

### Establishment of the optogenetic control system
  A critical step in establishing this system is to identify specific linker proteins interacting with the corresponding RNA. We selected Pur α as the linker protein for (G4C2)n RNA in our optogenetic control system. Pur α is a multifunctional protein that binds specifically to (G4C2)n RNA in vitro and in vivo [13,14]. Additionally, the interaction between Pur α and (G4C2)n RNA is well conserved between flies and mammals [14]. We constructed a plasmid that linked Pur α to mCherry-labeled Cry2 (Pur α-Cry2-mCherry) to co-express this fusion protein and (G4C2)n RNA in HEK293T cells. We reason that Pur α-Cry2-mCherry would bind to (G4C2)n RNA inside the cell, and Cry2 oligmerization could bring together (G4C2)n RNA molecules upon blue light illumination.   To visualize these RNA foci in the cells, we tagged (G4C2)n RNA with MS2-hairpin (n × G4C2-MS2), which allows us to perform live cell imaging of (G4C2)n RNA using YFP-tagged MS2-coat binding protein (MS2CP-YFP) as the indicator. Thus, we co-expressed Pur α-Cry2-mCherry, n × G4C2-MS2, and MS2CP-YFP in HEK293T cells for inducing the formation of (G4C2)n RNA foci. The next step is to test whether this optogenetic control system could indeed induce the formation of (G4C2)n RNA foci through blue light illumination.


### Induction of (G4C2)5 RNA foci assembly
  First, we used 5 × G4C2-MS2 to represent the normal genotype. A previous study has reported that (G4C2)n RNA foci and ALS/FTD neurodegeneration only manifest beyond a threshold number of G4C2 repeats, while the G4C2 sequence is only repeated 2 to 5 times in normal individuals [2], [3].

  Consistent with the optogenetic principle, almost no Cry2 puncta and (G4C2)5 RNA foci formed in the cells before blue light illumination, as no condensates appeared in both mCherry and YFP channels (Fig. 1b). Then we exposed the cells to 488 nm laser light. Within 5 minutes, Pur α-Cry2 underwent conspicuous clustering. At the same time, 5 × G4C2 RNA foci also started to assemble, as a few YFP droplets that co-localized with Pur α-Cry2 emerged in response to blue light illumination (Figs. 1b and S2). Note that the Cry2 clustering activation wavelength overlaps with the excitation wavelength of YFP, but the illuminating time for clustering activation (> 60 seconds) is much longer than the exposure time for YFP imaging (< 1 second). Thus, our optogenetic control system can induce the formation of RNA foci that would not condense spontaneously.


### Induction of (G4C2)29 RNA foci assembly
  Next, we used 29 × G4C2-MS2 to represent the disease genotype. Since expanded G4C2 RNA foci could form spontaneously in the neurons of affected ALS patients, the repeat-containing RNAs may accumulate as aberrant foci before blue light exposure, which would influence the effect of our system. On the other hand, Pur α was reported to rescue neurodegeneration arising from G4C2 repeat expansion [14], thus may attenuate the foci formation of 29 × G4C2 RNA.

  We co-expressed Pur α-Cry2-mCherry, 29 × G4C2-MS2, and MS2CP-YFP in HEK293T cells. We predicted that few spontaneous RNA foci could form in cells co-transfected with Pur α-Cry2-mCherry and 29 × G4C2 RNA, because Pur α would prevent 29 × G4C2 RNA from foci assembling (Fig. 1c). We further reasoned that the oligomerization of Cry2 is strong enough to drive the foci formation of Pur α-bound 29 × G4C2 RNA. Therefore, 29 × G4C2 RNA foci would emerge upon blue light illumination, most of which would be induced RNA foci instead of spontaneously formed ones. Consistent with our prediction, there were almost no Cry2 puncta and G4C2 RNA foci in cells before blue light exposure (Fig. 1d). Within 5 minutes of blue light illumination, Pur α-Cry2 underwent conspicuous clustering, and 29 × G4C2 formed distinct RNA foci as well (Figs. 1d and S1). More importantly, Pur α-Cry2 puncta and (G4C2)29 RNA foci had evident co-localization (Fig. 1d), suggesting that the (G4C2)29 RNA foci were indeed induced by the clustering of Pur α-Cry2. The quantifications of the fluorescent intensities and foci numbers are presented in Fig. 1e-f, showing a significantly higher foci number of the longer repeat RNA, whereas the fluorescent intensities illustrate strong colocalization.

  As a negative control, we co-expressed Cry2-mCherry that lacks the linker domain Pur α, 29 × G4C2-MS2, and MS2CP-YFP in HEK293T cells. Consistent with our model, some spontaneous 29 × G4C2 RNA foci existed within the cells before blue light exposure, due to the absence of Pur α (Fig. 2a). Upon blue light illumination, we observed massive Cry2 clustering but no increased number of G4C2 RNA foci (Fig. 2a). Notably, light-activated Cry2 puncta did not co-localize with those spontaneous 29 × G4C2 RNA foci neither (Fig. 2a) in all the cells tested. Additionally, the spontaneous foci number was smaller compared to that of 29 × G4C2 RNA foci induced by Pur α-Cry2. These results indicate that the specificity of our system is fundamentally based on the linker protein connecting Cry2 and repeat-containing RNA, i.e. connecting Cry2 to Pur α is required.   To examine the influence of nucleotide repeat number on the capability to drive phase separation in our system, we also counted the number of both 29 × and 5 × G4C2 RNA foci per cell after 6 minutes of blue light illumination. Although either 29 × or 5 × G4C2 RNA accumulated through phase separation, the statistical comparison showed that the number of 29 × G4C2 RNA foci per cell is significantly higher than that of 5 × G4C2 RNA (Fig. 1f). Consistent with recent reports [6], our results suggest that the ability of RNA to form foci depends closely on the repeat number of nucleotides.

  We further examined whether induced G4C2 RNA foci would influence cell survival. Staining with the fluorescent dye which assays caspase 3 activity, we found that HEK293T cells with light-induced 29 × G4C2 RNA foci did not have a higher apoptosis signal than control cells that are maintained in the dark (Fig. 2b), which is consistent with the previous report [15]. Whether neurons exhibit a similar phenomenon remains to be tested.


### Physical properties of (G4C2)n RNA foci
  We then probed the physical properties of (G4C2)n RNA foci using fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP). Molecules within liquid droplets constantly undergo dynamic exchange with the external environment. Consistent with previous reports [6], the fluorescence of spontaneously formed 29 × G4C2 RNA foci recovered rapidly in cells, indicating their liquid-like properties (Fig. 3a, c). Importantly, for induced 29 × G4C2 RNA foci, we found a nearly identical recovery of the fluorescence signal in terms of rate and level compared to spontaneously formed 29 × G4C2 RNA foci (Fig. 3b, c), suggesting that our system induces RNA foci that maintain the liquid-like properties of the ones naturally formed by G4C2 repeat-containing RNA.   The observation that both long and short G4C2 RNA foci in the cells exhibited the liquid-like property is consistent with the previous study [6]. The repeat numbers utilized in our study (5 × and 29 ×) may be too small to see the difference. Alternatively, it is also possible that these RNA foci are liquid-like because of the involvement of other RNA-binding proteins that prohibited liquid-to-solid phase transition in the cells, as suggested by the previous study [6]. Meanwhile, longer (G4C2)n RNA does form significantly more intracellular puncta than the shorter one (Fig. 1f).


### Computational simulation of (G4C2)n RNA foci assembly
  Finally, we tried to examine the effect of our system on the propensity of (G4C2)n RNA towards phase separation theoretically by computational simulation. We first simulated the free energy curve as a function of mean solute molar fraction. This is widely used for modeling and prediction of the occurrence of LLPS [16]. A simple example is presented here (Fig. 4a, b) to show how we analyzed the propensity of a system towards phase separation by plotting the free energy curve. Since biomacromolecules like DNAs, RNAs, and proteins can be viewed as polymers, consider a binary solution with solvent A and polymer B. We utilized the two-dimension lattice model and the Flory-Huggins mean field theory, which assumes that each identical subunit of the polymer or one solvent molecule occupies one lattice site and the connected subunits of the polymer are arranged in adjacent lattice sites (Fig. 4a). Based on the mean field approximation and the quasi-chemical model, we can calculate the Gibbs free energy change of this system (ΔGmix) when two solutions mix by different ratios (Fig. 4b). With certain properties of polymer B and their interactions, the curve of ΔGmix as a function of the concentration of the polymer (φ) will exhibit a W-shaped feature [11] (Fig. 4b). Note that a state with lower Gibbs free energy is more stable, so the system is inclined to reach a mixing state with the lowest possible value of the Gibbs free energy [15]. As a result, when φdilute<φ<φdense, rather than remaining in a single phase with polymer concentration of φ, the system will partition into two phases at equilibrium, one with polymer concentration of φdilute and the other with polymer concentration of φdense, so that its Gibbs free energy is as low as possible (Fig. 4b). Therefore, if the free energy curve of a system is wavier with lower φdilute and higher φdense, phase separation is more preferrable in this system, since partitioning into two phases lower the free energy more dramatically, and there is a larger window for phase separation within the concentration range. An ultimate situation is an incomplete W-shaped ΔGmix curve comprising one or no lowest point (a lying S-shaped wave for the former and an inverted U-shaped curve for the latter), where the concave part of the curve is still the region for phase separation. On the other hand, certain properties of A, B, and their interaction result in a U-shaped ΔGmix curve (Fig. 4b), where the polymer will always dissolve in the solvent without phase separation.   To model our system, we simplified it to a ternary solution with solvent A, polymer B ((G4C2)n RNA), and polymer C ((Pur α-)Cry2) and analyze its ΔGmix similarly as above. Since B and C are supposed to partition together, we set the mole fraction of the solvent as φ and calculate ΔGmix as the function of φ for simplicity (see methods). Another independent variable of ΔGmix is a, which is the mole fraction of B in polymers B and C (a=nBnB+nC). As ΔGmix is dependent on two rather than one independent variables here, it is now plotted into a free energy surface instead of a free energy curve. Next, we changed the value of the parameters representing the polymer subunit number and the interaction strength to characterize the different length of (G4C2)n RNA and dark/light treatment (see methods). As a result, we can visualize the ΔGmix surface depending on φ and a under different conditions (Fig. 4c-f), and the ΔGmix curves under certain values of a can be extracted from the surface plots (Fig. 4g-i).

  The simulation results show strong consistency with the experimental results. Whatever the ratio of (G4C2)n RNA versus (Pur α-)Cry2 (different a), co-expression of 29 × (G4C2) RNA and Pur α-Cry2 plus light treatment almost always presents the system with the waviest S-shaped ΔGmix curve (Fig. 4c-i), indicating the strongest propensity of the system towards phase separation, considering the largest “partition-induced” energy decrease and the widest concentration range for phase separation. This effect is the most obvious when a=0.5 (Fig. 4c, h), and becomes neglectable as a approximates 0 or 1 (Fig. 4c, g, i), implying that Pur α-Cry2 should be expressed at a similar level with 29 × (G4C2) RNA to promote its phase separation effectively. This promoting effect of 29 × (G4C2) RNA and Pur α-Cry2 plus light treatment compared to the three other control conditions fits well with our in-cell data (Figs.1b, 1d and 2a). Similar to 29 × (G4C2) RNA and Pur α-Cry2 plus light, the ΔGmix curve is always S-shaped under different values of a when Pur α-Cry2 is substituted with Cry2 (Fig. 4f, g-i), suggesting that phase separation can always happen under this condition but is less preferred, consistent with Fig. 2a. By contrast, for the other two situations, phase separation cannot happen all the time as a changes. Strikingly, when a approximates 0, phase separation is impossible when blue light treatment is discarded due to the U-shaped ΔGmix curve (Fig. 4d, g), which accords with our experimental result (Fig. 1b) and our reasoning that binding of Pur α will distract 29 × (G4C2) RNA assembly without light (Fig. 1c). A similar U-shaped ΔGmix curve is exhibited when a approximates 1 and 29 × (G4C2) RNA is replaced by 5 × (G4C2) RNA (Fig. 4e, i), which is also very reasonable as phase separation is inaccessible for 5 × (G4C2) RNA alone. In brief, the simulation results accord well with our intracellular experiments, confirming the effect of our system to promote the propensity of (G4C2)n RNA towards phase separation via the proposed mechanism by Pur α-Cry2 and light.

  It is worth noticing that detailed intramolecular interactions of (G4C2)n RNAs, especially their G-quadruplex structure [17], were not considered in this study. To model the G-quadruplex structure, the interactions between every two polymer units needs to be quantified, which has not been well characterized to provide reasonable parameters. A more complex 3D model instead of the current 2D lattice model is also needed, which is technically challenging. Meanwhile, our current over-simplified model can indirectly reflect the G-quadruplex structure to some extent, since we set χAB to be larger when the repeat number of (G4C2)n RNA increases, which is consistent with the property of G-quadruplex structured (G4C2)n RNAs.


## Discussion and conclusion
  In this study, we started with choosing a protein, Pur α, that can specifically bind to (G4C2)n RNA. By fusing Pur α as a linker domain to Cry2 protein and utilizing the light-dependent clustering property of Cry2, we induced the phase separation and aggregation of (G4C2)n RNA, and thus achieved artificial control of the formation of RNA foci by light illumination.

  We also studied the effects of the G4C2 repeat number that is relevant to ALS disease on the level of induced RNA foci. Our results show that the number of aggregates formed by 29 × (G4C2) RNA is more than that of 5 × (G4C2) RNA. The statistical analysis supports a positive correlation between the number of RNA aggregates and the number of G4C2 repeats, which is consistent with clinical observation that ALS/FTD neurodegeneration only manifests in patients carrying G4C2 repeats beyond a threshold number [2], [3]. Compared with spontaneously aggregated RNA, the number of (G4C2)n RNA aggregates generated after blue light induction was higher. Computational simulation supports the effect of our system to promote the propensity of (G4C2)n RNA towards phase separation by Pur α-Cry2 and light.

  We further confirmed that the RNA aggregates induced by the light control system were all liquid by FRAP experiments, and the number of repeats of G4C2 did not affect the liquid properties of RNA aggregates. At the same time, the spontaneously formed (G4C2)n RNA aggregates were also in a liquid state, which indicated that the light-induced RNA aggregates had similar characteristics to the RNA aggregates formed in the physiological as well as pathological states. This allows us to investigate the cellular consequences of the light-induced RNA foci.

  We carried out biological function studies to investigate whether induced RNA foci would disrupt normal cellular processes and lead to pathological phenotypes related to repeat expansion disorders. As a neurodegenerative disease, the motor neurons and neurons in the frontal and temporal lobes of ALS patients eventually die. Thus, we verified whether RNA aggregates affected cell survival. Using high-content live-cell imaging, we investigated whether RNA aggregates lead to apoptosis. Although induced RNA foci do not negatively influence HEK293T cell survival, we speculate that neurons, especially motor neurons and neurons in frontal and temporal lobes, might be affected by those foci, which could be investigated by our system as well. Since it takes ALS patients who carry (G4C2)n more than 1 year to show neurodegeneration [1], cell toxicity may not be observed on the time scale of our experiments. Investigating whether these RNA foci have cytotoxicity, and further exploring whether the formation of RNA foci will lead to subsequent transcriptional and translational disorders and the emergence of ALS-related pathological phenotypes through in vivo experiments, may provide evidence for the pathogenic mechanism of G4C2, and possibly new entry points for the ALS treatment.

  To demonstrate the physiological relevance, it is desired to test whether known components of the (G4C2)n RNA foci are also present in opto-induced (G4C2)n RNA granules. Unfortunately, one limitation of our system is that almost all the opto-induced RNA foci were detected in the cytoplasm, different from the localization of the endogenous RNA foci, which are in the nuclei. This proposed a technical challenge to test the recruitment of known proteins in the (G4C2)n RNA granules such as hnRNP H and MBNL1 [6,18], because they are largely in the nuclei. The exact reason for this phenomenon remains to be elucidated. Another limitation is that the over-expression of Pur α may influence the function and property of (G4C2)n RNA granules. By comparing the RNA foci signal before and after blue-light exposure, both in cells with Pur α over-expression, we hope to minimize the effect of Pur α per se, also this cannot be completely excluded. The CRY2 system used in this study may also influence the property of (G4C2)n RNA foci, and thus the disease-relevance of the system may need further justification. Meanwhile, the optogenetic control of protein phase-separation has similar limitations, and yet it still provides important tools to study phase-separation. In fact, interfering with RNA phase-separation is likely inevitable, because it is necessary for the manipulation of RNA phase-separation. The system may still be useful to test possible functional impact for RNA foci, although further demonstrations by other approaches in the endogenous system are desired. Finally, orientation is important for the development of optogenetic tools. To further optimize the system, it is desired to set up a screening system to find the best version in future studies.

  Taken together, our current form of the light-induced (G4C2)n RNA foci is certainly not enough to faithfully mimic ALS conditions. Meanwhile, considering the lack of approaches to manipulate RNA foci formation in a controllable manner, our approach may still have some merits. Similar to the optogenetic control system of protein phase-separation [7], our system only provides a possible tool to study the cell biology and possible functional impact of (G4C2)n RNA foci. Any observation made by this approach needs further demonstration in more disease-relevant systems.

  In summary, we successfully established an optogenetic control system to induce RNA phase separation, providing a possible approach to study the functional impact of ALS-related (G4C2)n RNA aggregates, although with limitations mentioned above. Our system is based on the blue light-induced aggregation of Cry2 and the specific binding of Pur α to (G4C2)n RNA, utilizing the oligomerization of Cry2-Pur α fusion protein upon blue light illumination to drive (G4C2)n RNA aggregation in HEK293T cells. Fluorescent imaging results demonstrated that (G4C2)n RNA foci assembly can be efficiently induced through blue light illumination in live cells. In this system, the introduction of blue light can induce the (G4C2)n RNA aggregates de novo, which is essential to reveal the direct causality of aggregates and ALS disease phenotypes. Our results also indicate the potential to develop an in vivo optogenetic control system of RNA foci in the future.
