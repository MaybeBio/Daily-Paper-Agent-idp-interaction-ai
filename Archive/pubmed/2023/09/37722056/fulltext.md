# Combined prediction and design reveals the target recognition mechanism of an intrinsically disordered protein interaction domain


## Significance
  We report that an α/β-type structure module formed through synergetic folding is used by an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) domain, the C-terminal domain of protein 4.1G, to recognize IDP regions of the protein’s binding partners. To elucidate the target recognition mechanisms of IDP domains is challenging because the corresponding complexes are usually highly dynamic. With little information about their structures, many IDP-IDP complexes could only be grossly classified as being “fuzzy.” Here, by leveraging recent developments in deep learning-based structure prediction and computational protein design, we are able to segregate what are truly IDP regions from what are the core structural domains of an IDP-IDP complex and to reveal the key roles of structure in the molecular interaction.


## Results

### An Overview of the Complete Workflow.
  First, we used dihydrofolate reductase-based protein complementarity analysis (DHFR PCA) (17) to systematically analyze the effects of mutation and sequence truncation on the interaction between the 4.1G CTD and NuMA (Fig. 1A). We then applied AF2 (22) to predict the possible structures of complexes formed by the interaction-reserving truncated peptides (Fig. 1B). The predicted complex structure is highly consistent with the mutational effects probed by DHFR PCA.

  Next, we designed mutants based on the predicted complex structure using ABACUS-R (24), a deep-learning method for structure-based sequence design. Protein crystallization was attempted for the designed mutants (Fig. 1C). For one quadruple mutant, a high-resolution structure was solved by using X-ray crystallography. Once again, the binding mode of the IDPs suggested by the X-ray structure is highly consistent with both the experimentally measured mutational effects and the AF2-predicted structure.

  We then attempted to predict other possible targets of 4.1G CTD using AF2 structure prediction to scan the sequence windows of other known protein partners of 4.1G. All high-confidence predictions presented the same binding mode as the 4.1G CTD-NuMA complex (Fig. 1D). We experimentally tested 38 predicted sequence regions from 10 proteins using DHFR PCA, and seven of them showed detectable interactions with 4.1G CTD.


### Essential Regions and Key Residues Identified by DHFR PCA.
  Results (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) indicate that DHFR PCA can detect the IDP-IDP interaction between 4.1G CTD and NuMA fragments (these fragments are named according to their lengths, and their exact locations in the complete proteins are given in Fig. 2A). DHFR PCA result (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) shows that when the NuMA (4.1G CTD) fragment is placed at the C-terminal (N-terminal) of the N-terminal (C-terminal) complementary fragment of DHFR, the assay is more sensitive than when the NuMA and 4.1G CTD fragments are placed at switched positions. Thus, the former configuration has been used in subsequent experiments.

  The experimental result (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) shows that DHFR PCA can reliably distinguish between previously identified binding and nonbinding sequence variants. Previous isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments have shown that two mutants of 4.1G67, one containing the single mutation V988D and the other the triple mutations V991, 992, 993D, did not exhibit detectable binding with NuMA26, while two other 4.1G67 mutants, D967,969A and E996,1002A, still retain binding with NuMA26 (9). DHFR PCA can clearly distinguish between these binding and nonbinding mutants. However, we note that the DHFR PCA results for D967, 969A relative to E996,1002A did not fully correlate with the ITC-measured binding affinities of these two mutants to NuMA26 (Kd ~ 18.3 µM for D967,969A and Kd ~ 176.68 μM for E996,1002A) (9), probably due to interfering factors such as the varied effects of mutations on protein expression levels or protein stability.

  DHFR PCA with alanine scanning on 4.1G67 and NuMA26 (See raw data in SI Appendix, Fig. S2) suggested that the two fragments can be further shortened while still retaining their essential regions for binding. Indeed, as shown in Fig. 2A, the fragment pair 4.1G57 and NuMA26 led to similar TMP resistance in DHFR PCA as the pair 4.1G67 and NuMA26 [whose Kd value was reported to be 8.40 ± 0.91 µM (9)]. Fragment 4.1G57 has been obtained by truncating 10 residues from the N terminus of 4.1G67, while fragment 4.1G57′ is another 57-residue fragment obtained by truncating 10 residues from the C terminus of 4.1G67. Alanine scanning showed that individually mutating residues in the regions removed in either 4.1G57 or 4.1G57′ had little effect on binding (see a summarized view of the alanine scanning results in Fig. 2B). However, compared to 4.1G67 or 4.1G57, 4.1G57′ seemed to bind significantly weaker to NuMA26 (Fig. 2A).

  The region removed in 4.1G57′ is highly concentrated with negatively charged residues (6 out of 10 residues are negatively charged, Fig. 2B). The effects of simultaneously mutating 4 of the negatively charged residues into positively charged ones were further examined by DHFR PCA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), which showed that the C-terminal oppositely charged 4.1G57 or 4.1G67 mutant paired with NuMA26 indeed led to substantially reduced TMP resistance. Interestingly, the N terminus of NuMA26 is concentrated with positively charged residues (Fig. 2B), which are also associated with minor effects of separated single residue mutations in alanine scanning. When four of these NuMA residues were simultaneously mutated into negatively charged ones, the mutations did not seem to affect the binding of NuMA26 with the wild-type 4.1G57 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). However, the combined mutations on NuMA26 did affect the binding of the fragment with the C-terminal oppositely charged 4.1G57 mutant by recovering the TMP resistance to almost the same level as that of the wild-type NuMA26-4.1G57 pair (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). These results taken together indicate that the electrostatic interactions between the oppositely charged C-terminal region of 4.1G57 and the N-terminal region of NuMA26 can make some contributions but cannot be the major driving forces for binding. This conclusion is consistent with the observation that NuMA16, which lacks the positively charged N-terminal region in NuMA26, still exhibited substantial TMP resistance when paired with 4.1G57 in DHFR PCA (Fig. 2A).

  In summary, fragment truncating and alanine scanning have identified that key residues of notable mutational effects are restricted to a region of about 30 residues in 4.1G57 and a region of about 10 residues in NuMA16. As shown in Fig. 2B, the key residues in both regions are mostly hydrophobic, with some of them occurring in pairs separated by one nonconserved interval residue along the amino acid sequence. In structured proteins, such a sequence pattern signals a β-strand at the surface of a folded structure.


### A Well-Defined Complex Structure Predicted by AF2.
  The mutational effects probed by DHFR PCA constitute systematic data against which any structural model of the corresponding complex can be compared. A recent 200 ns replica exchange MD (REMD) simulation of the complexation between 4.1G67 and NuMA26 has produced a conformational ensemble dominated by 15 main conformational clusters (9). Even though certain properties averaged over all sampled conformations, such as the overall frequencies of individual amino acid residues engaged in interresidue contacts, seemed to correlate well with mutational data, the representative conformational clusters extracted from the REMD trajectory seemed to be unable to explain some of the main mutational effects found by DHFR PCA (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). For example, in the REMD sampled ensemble, residues I1814 to V1825 of NuMA exhibited very large structural variations relative to the 4.1G fragments, with the hydrophobic residues I1814, I1815, I1817, and M1819 frequently exposed to solvent. This left the strong disruptive effects of mutating these residues unexplained, at least by individual conformational states extracted from REMD. Given that the conformational space of the complex is enormous and the REMD simulations had to be started from a limited number of initial states constructed based on highly insufficient experimental information (9, 33), there was no guarantee that the REMD covered the truly dominating parts of the conformational space.

  The advent of AF2 (22) provides an alternative approach to propose structural models based solely on amino acid sequences. Applying AF2 to predict the possible structure formed by 4.1G57 and NuMA16 upon their complexation led to a structure model shown in Fig. 1B. In this model, the respective 4.1G57 and NuMA16 regions that contain the key interacting residues identified by DHFR PCA form a well-defined structure of one α helix (α1) packed against a three-strand antiparallel β sheet. The α helix and the two edge strands of the β sheet (β1 and β2) come from 4.1G57, while the central strand of the β sheet (β3) comes from NuMA. The α1 is flanked by β1 and β2 in the primary sequence of 4.1G57, and it crosses over β3 in the three-dimensional structure of the complex. Closer inspections suggest that almost all the key interacting residues found by DHFR PCA are at structurally important positions for maintaining the stability of the complex according to the AF2-predicted structure (Fig. 2C). Especially, the side chains of the key residues I1815, I1817, and M1819 of NuMA are located on the central strand, facing the buried side of the β sheet. These side chains are packed against the residues A975 and A979 on the α helix to form a hydrophobic core that can stabilize the entire complex structure. It is worth mentioning that AF2 has also been applied to predict the structure of the 4.1G57-NuMA16 fusion protein. The model predicted for the covalently linked fusion protein has the same core structure as described above. Thus, the core structure predicted by AF2 is rather insensitive or robust with respect to sequence changes outside the core region.

  As shown in Fig. 1B, the antiparallel architecture of the β strands leaves the N-terminal region of NuMA16 (i.e., the upstream region flanking β3) in close proximity to and in between the β1-α1 loop and the C-terminal region of 4.1G57 (i.e., the downstream region flanking β2). At the same time, the C-terminal region of NuMA16 (i.e., the downstream region flanking β3) is close to and in between the α1-β2 loop and the N-terminal region of 4.1G57 (i.e., the upstream region flanking β1). These features may potentially lead to favorable electrostatic interactions between 4.1G57 and NuMA16 in these nonstructured regions, as the corresponding regions in 4.1G57 are concentrated with negatively charged residues while those in NuMA16 are concentrated with positively charged residues (Fig. 2B). To examine some residue-specific contributions of electrostatic interactions, we considered two intermolecular residue pairs of oppositely charged residues that are in close proximity in the AF2-predicted structure: K1821 of NuMA and D985 of 4.1G, and K1822 of NuMA and E951 of 4.1G (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). DHFR PCA showed that mutating each of the residues in these pairs to an oppositely charged one substantially weakened but did not eliminate the binding of the mutant with the corresponding wild-type partner (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Nevertheless, simultaneously mutating both residues in one pair was not found to be able to recover the interaction strength to the wild-type level (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Thus, relative to hydrophobic packing, electrostatic interactions from loops or disordered regions outside of the core structure (according to the complex structure predicted by AF2) seem to have measurable but still only secondary contributions to binding.

  We note that the sequence regions forming the core structure in the AF2 predicted model consist of well-mixed composition of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues, in contrast to many other IDP regions that exhibit a low-complexity amino acid composition. Although these regions have been experimentally shown to be structurally disordered in the uncomplexed state (31, 32), it is interesting to explore the propensity of these sequences to form ordered structures using common sequence-based bioinformatics predictions. We conducted IUPred3 (35) disorder prediction and PredictProtein (36) secondary structure prediction on the relevant sequences, and the results are depicted (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

  The prediction result (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) shows that the regions of interest in the full-length sequences of both 4.1G and NuMA are all predicted to have disorder scores above 0.5 (the default threshold of the program for assigning disordered regions). On the other hand, Fig. 2D illustrates that only the residues in β1 and in the loop following β1 are predicted to have disorder scores below 0.5 when the prediction is performed on the 4.1G67-NuMA26 fusion peptide. Furthermore, PredictProtein predicts the presence of β1, α1, and β2 in 4.1G CTD based on either the full-length 4.1G sequence or the 4.1G67-NuMA26 fusion peptide. However, the β3 element in NuMA was not predicted solely based on the NuMA sequence, but it was predicted when some 4.1G sequence was included in the 4.1G67-NuMA26 fusion peptide. Overall, the predicted disorder scores and secondary structure probabilities support a disorder-to-order transformation mechanism for complex formation.


### The Crystal Structure of a Fusion Peptide with Designed Mutations.
  As AF2 is a computational model learned from data of mainly monomeric structured proteins (22), its applicability to complexes of IDPs have not been established. To determine whether the complex structure predicted by AF2 truly represents the major or dominant conformational state of the IDP-IDP interaction, more direct data, such as structures resolved by X-ray crystallography or NMR, are needed. Considering the challenges associated with complex crystallization, we expressed and purified the 4.1G57-NuMA16 fusion protein and attempted protein crystallization. However, despite the highly ordered nature of the AF2-predicted structure, our crystallization efforts were unsuccessful. NMR spectra of the fusion protein (see below) also indicated significant conformational dynamics that hindered further structural analysis. Obtaining more reliable structural data seemed unattainable by solely considering the wild-type proteins.

  Therefore, we explored the possibility of designing mutants that could potentially stabilize the complex structure and enable direct structural analysis. For this purpose, we performed sequence design based on the AF2-predicted structure of the fusion protein using ABACUS-R, a recently developed structure-based protein sequence design program utilizing deep learning (24). Six mutants of the fused 4.1G57-NuMA16 protein were designed (mutants B1 to B6), and the mutated sites on the AF2-predicted structure are shown (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). After confirming that the mutations retained interaction with DHFR PCA (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), we attempted protein expression and crystallization for all six mutants. Crystals were successfully obtained for mutant B5, and its structure was subsequently solved. Parameters for X-ray data collection and structural refinement are reported (SI Appendix, Table S1). Importantly, ITC measurements on 4.1G57 and NuMA16 fragments containing the mutations in B5 produced a Kd value of 11.5 ± 2.86 µM (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

  The crystal structure reveals that B5 forms a tetramer in its crystalline state (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). The four monomers are arranged in a circular fashion, with their β sheets integrated to form an antiparallel β barrel consisting of twelve strands. The tetramer can be viewed as a dimer of dimers, with the monomer-monomer interface in the dimer stabilized primarily by main chain–main chain hydrogen bonds between the β2 strands of the two neighboring subunits and the dimer–dimer interface stabilized by main chain–main chain hydrogen bonds between the β1 strands of two neighboring subunits.

  The dimeric structure suggests that the mutations in B5 have stabilized the crystalline state by establishing a network of coupled polar interactions (Fig. 3A). Notably, the H994N mutation introduces two asparagine side chains that are symmetrically arranged at the dimer β2-β2 interface, with the amide oxygen of one side chain closely contacting the Cβ of the other side chain, potentially forming two X...H-C “hydrogen bonds.” The network also involves the intermolecular interaction between the side chains of H994N and the wild-type E996. Furthermore, the side chain of E996 forms a hydrogen bond with the side chain of the mutated T1812R. This network of polar interactions cannot be formed by the wild-type residue types, which may explain why the wild-type protein did not yield crystals while B5 did.


### The Crystal Structure Can Represent the Complex in Solution.
  Even though B5 is a fusion peptide of mutated 4.1G57 and NuMA16, and its crystal structure has been determined in the tetrameric state, several lines of evidence support the notion that conformational states closely resembling the crystal structure serve as the structural basis for the interaction between the two wild-type IDPs.

  The first line of evidence is that the monomer structure observed in the crystal aligns well with the structural model predicted by AF2 for the wild-type complex (or for the fusion peptide) (Fig. 1C). The types and three-dimensional arrangements of the secondary structure elements are consistent, with only slight differences in strand lengths and sheet curvatures. Thus, according to AF2, the monomer structure observed in the crystal can be considered as an intrinsically stable conformational state of the wild-type complex.

  The second line of evidence is that the wild-type sequences of 4.1G57 and NuMA16 are highly compatible with the crystal structure, as evaluated by the ABACUS-R sequence design program. In Fig. 3B, a comparison is made between the wild-type sequences and the sequence probability profiles calculated by ABACUS-R based on the monomer structure observed in the crystal. For 42 out of the 73 sequence positions, the wild-type residues rank among the top three residue types selected by ABACUS-R. Interestingly, two regions where the ABACUS-R predicted sequence profile and the wild-type sequence align particularly well are the β1-α1 and α1-β2 loops (Fig. 3B). It is likely that the intrinsic local conformational preferences of these two loops make significant contributions to the structural stability of the complex. This is consistent with the mutational effects detected by alanine scanning for residues in these regions (Fig. 2B).

  The third line of evidence is that the crystal structural model is in agreement with the DHFR PCA screening results obtained from saturated mutation libraries of the IDPs (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). These libraries covered the key residues identified by alanine scanning, as well as most of the hydrophobic residues, encompassing 20 sites on 4.1G67 and six sites on NuMA26. The libraries were subjected to high-throughput sequencing before and after applying DHFR PCA to screen for variants that retain interaction. The enrichment ratios obtained from the screening were used to indicate the site-specific preferences for residue types that promote interaction. It turns out that these preferences at respective positions align closely with the corresponding structural environments in the crystal structure. The results for the six examined sites on NuMA are illustrated in Fig. 3C. At the three sites buried within the hydrophobic core of the crystal structure (1815, 1817, and 1819), all the residue types favoring interaction are hydrophobic. At site 1814, which is outward facing on a β strand but still exhibits low solvent accessibility, the residue types favoring interaction are the hydrophobic, β-preferring valine and isoleucine. At sites 1823 and 1825, located outside the core structure, hydrophilic residues are tolerated despite the native residues types being strongly hydrophobic.


### The Structure in Solution Is Ordered but Dynamic.
  The screening results for most of the considered sites on 4.1G57 are also consistent with the crystal structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Two exceptional sites are 972 and 976 (Fig. 4A), which are located on the α helix and exhibit strong intolerance toward hydrophilic substitutions despite their relatively large solvent accessibility in the crystal structure (Fig. 4A). However, the AF2-predicted structure suggests that a slight shift of the α helix would bring the side chains of L972 and I976 into close contact with the hydrophobic residues on the β sheet (Fig. 4A). Thus, the mutational screening results for these two sites indicate that the conformations in solution may be distributed dynamically around the crystal structure.

  To further explore the aggregation state of the fusion peptide in solution and the effects of the mutations introduced in B5, we examined the wild-type and B5 fusion peptides in solution using multiangle light scattering (MALS) (Fig. 4B). While the molecular weight of the fusion peptide in the monomer state is 8.8 kDa, the apparent molecular weights derived from MALS fluctuated around 20 kDa. Thus, both the wild-type and the B5 fusion peptides predominantly exist as dimers in solution. Compared with B5, the wild-type fusion peptide exhibited larger fluctuations in its MALS data. This observation suggests that the wild-type dimer is less compact and more dynamic than the mutant dimer, aligning with the stabilization of the dimer structure by the mutations.

  To examine the influence of fusion/complexation on the conformation of the peptides in solution, we measured the NMR 1H-15N HSQC spectra of wild-type and B5 4.1G57 peptides and wild-type and B5 4.1G57-NuMA16 fusion peptides (Fig. 4C). The 4.1G57 peptides displayed typical spectra for disordered proteins, characterized by a narrow 1H chemical shift dispersion. After the three mutations of B5 were introduced into 4.1G57, the 1H chemical shift dispersion remained narrow, indicating that the mutated peptide also remains disordered. In contrast, the fusion peptides yielded spectra that differed qualitatively from those of 4.1G57 before fusion. The 1H chemical shift dispersion significantly increased, suggesting the presence of well-ordered structures. Thus, fusion with NuMA16 qualitatively alters the conformational distribution of 4.1G57 from highly disordered to partially ordered. However, the broad line widths in the spectra indicate the occurrence of conformational exchange on the micro-to-millisecond timescale in solution, posing challenges in solving the solution structure by NMR. Nevertheless, considering the high consistency between the crystal structure and the data on mutational effects on binding, it is expected that the core structure of the complexes closely aligns with the crystal structure. It is worth noting that the spectra of the wild-type and mutant fusion peptides align well, indicating that the peptides share a very similar aggregation state and core structure. Moreover, the smaller number of observable peaks for the wild-type peptide indicates greater dynamics of the peptide in solution.

  MD simulations of the complex of wild-type 4.1G67 and NuMA26 in solution were also performed. Starting from a model constructed based on the crystal structure, the complex structure remained stable throughout the 200 ns simulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). After a short relaxation period, the structures sampled in the MD simulations exhibited similar RMSD values from the crystal structure and the AF2 structure, with noticeable differences in the position of the α helix relative to the β sheet among the three sets of structures (Fig. 4D). These findings may explain the aforementioned inconsistency between the mutational effects and the crystal structure for positions 972 and 976, supporting the dynamic nature of the core structure.


### Prediction and Assessment of Other 4.1G CTD-Binding Regions.
  We attempted to use AF2 to predict potential regions targeted by 4.1G CTD in other known interaction partners of 4.1G. Assuming that these regions would bind to 4.1G CTD through the same well-defined structural unit as formed by 4.1G57 and NuMA16, we extracted sequence windows from the candidate proteins and applied AF2 to predict possible complex structures with 4.1G57. The string database (37) lists 10 partner proteins of 4.1G. For six of them (CADM1, CASK, FCGR1A, FKBP2, GRIA1, and MPP6), we considered every 16-residue window sliding along the entire sequences. For the remaining four (SPTAN1, SPTBN1, SPTBN2, and SPTBN4), which are long and highly homologous with each other, we only considered candidate fragments corresponding to the coil regions in their AF2-predicted structures. If a candidate fragment was 16 residues or longer, it was directly linked to the C terminus of 4.1G57 for AF2 prediction. Otherwise, a short linker (-GGGGSGGGGS-) was used to connect the candidate peptide to 4.1G57 for AF2 prediction.

  As shown in Fig. 5 A and B, most of the AF2 predictions on candidate fragments resulted in predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT) scores below 75, indicating low confidence. However, a small fraction of predictions yielded high pLDDT scores. Fig. 5C shows that these high-pLDDT-score predictions consistently featured structures similar to the structure of the 4.1G57-NuMA16 complex. This suggests that the latter structure is the preferred mode used by 4.1G CTD to recognize its various ligands.

  Based on the AF2 predictions, we selected 38 candidate fragments (SI Appendix, Table S2) from 4.1G partners for experimental testing using DHFR PCA. These candidates included 35 fragments of high pLDDT scores, 1 fragment with a medium score, and 2 fragments containing sequence windows with both high and low scores. According to DHFR PCA, 7 of the tested fragments were found to interact with 4.1G57 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). All of the AF2-predicted structures for these positive fragments fused to 4.1G57 aligned well with the predicted model of 4.1G57-NuMA. (Fig. 1D) enabling structure-based sequence alignment of these fragments (Fig. 5D). The alignments clearly demonstrated the preference for β-preferring hydrophobic residues such as isoleucine and valine at the positions aligned to I1815 and I1817 of NuMA. Moreover, the short segments centered around these two positions were enriched with β-preferring residues (i.e., residues with a branched side chain at Cβ) and often exhibited a short sequence pattern of alternating hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues, characteristic of a surface β-strand. These sequence features are consistent with the structural environments of the respective residues in the predicted complex structures. The sequences of the remaining 31 candidate fragments that did not exhibit interaction with 4.1G57 in DHFR PCA (the negative fragments) were also aligned (SI Appendix, Fig. S14) based on structure alignments of complex structures predicted by AF2. Some of them lacked hydrophobic residues at the positions corresponding to either I1815 or I1817 or did not have separating hydrophilic residues between the hydrophobic positions. However, a large fraction of the negative fragments exhibited similar sequence features for the predicted β strand in comparison with the positive fragments. Thus, compatibility with the core structure of the complex seems to be necessary but insufficient for target sequences of 4.1G CTD. The residues flanking the structured core can still contribute to binding (38), or they can disrupt binding when one or several of them interact highly unfavorably with surrounding residues upon forming the complex structure.


## Discussion
  Because IDP-IDP complexes are typically highly flexible, exhibit extensive dynamics, and tend to aggregate (39, 40), elucidating the structural basis or biophysical mechanism underlying direct IDP-IDP binding presents a significant challenge. The lack of means to obtain structural information for dynamic IDP-IDP complexes has often resulted in the invocation of the fuzzy complex model (11, 12, 41). While this model rightly emphasizes the structure disorder or high flexibility, it usually provides limited insights into the roles of structural order, particularly the potentially large changes in structure order upon complex formation. Consequently, fuzzy complex models often fail to explain important, functionally relevant characteristics such as the reliance on specific sequence sites or critical residues (although we note an exceptional example in which highly disordered structure does not contradict the strong electrostatic interaction between two protein regions with high densities of opposite charges) (10).

  In this study, we have overcome the challenge of dissecting the structure of a dynamic complex by leveraging some of the most recent developments in computational biology. One is the deep learning structure prediction program AF2 (22). As a machine learning method trained on datasets of structured proteins, the applicability of AF2 to IDP-IDP complexes may not be self-evident. Nonetheless, we have successfully applied AF2 to propose structural models for a pair of known IDP partners as well as to identify previously unrecognized binding partners. Our results indicate that AF2 can be highly valuable in addressing similar problems. However, we also acknowledge that AF2 may tend to overpredict ordered structures, possibly because the model has been trained with the prior assumption that the input sequences would fold. Therefore, the results of AF2 alone should be treated as inconclusive, and complementary evidence is necessary to establish conclusiveness. The other computational tool used in this work is ABACUS-R, a deep-learning program for structure-based sequence design. Protein design programs like ABACUS-R can find, among the vast space of possible mutations, those mutations that diversify the interresidue interactions to maintain or enhance the stability of a given structure (24). In this study, this approach led to the successful crystallization of one out of six designed mutants tested experimentally. Applying ABACUS-R to the crystal structure also revealed sequence features of the original IDPs that are compatible with the structure.

  We have elucidated that two IDP regions in 4.1G CTD and NuMA, previously characterized as forming a fuzzy complex (11), actually interact via a mechanism more aligned with “synergetic folding” or “mutually induced folding and binding.” While synergetic folding has been proposed as a viable mechanism in computational studies (42), real examples supported directly by structural data of complexes are rare. One such example is the binding between the interaction domains of a cAMP-responsive element-binding protein and a nuclear receptor coactivator (7). Another example is the WASP (8), which mediates the effect of Cdc42 on cytoskeleton assembly. WASP possesses a disordered GTPase-binding domain that can bind either the disordered carboxy-terminal VCA region of the same molecule or Cdc42 in a mutually exclusive manner. In these earlier computational and experimental examples, binding induced the synergetic folding of α-helices. In comparison, the formation of a β sheet, as demonstrated in our current study, is a far more cooperative process because the constituent strands can be widely separated in sequence or located on different chains. This study provides an example of two natural IDPs interacting through their synergetic folding to form a new β sheet. We have shown that despite the extensive conformational dynamics of the respective complexes, the core structure motif identified here serves as the fundamental mechanism for the binding between 4.1G CTD and its various IDP targets. The requirements for compatibility with this core structure impose strong constraints on the allowed amino acid types at specific positions on 4.1G CTD and its targets. At the same time, diverse residue types are still tolerated at positions such as the exposed positions on the secondary structure elements or positions outside the core structure. In this way, the structure enables a delicate balance between the diversity and selectivity of the binding targets, which is crucial for the cellular function of 4.1G as an interaction hub or adaptor protein.

  It is noteworthy that the fragments comprising the core structure motif mentioned above exhibit a combination of hydrophobic and hydrophilic amino acid residues, encoding distinct preferences for secondary structure types. Although these IDP regions in isolated proteins can be correctly predicted as being disordered (with above-threshold scores) by contemporary bioinformatics tools, they differ from IDP regions that are with low-complexity amino acid sequences. In this study, we have demonstrated that these two types of IDP regions can be segregated by integrating computational and experimental approaches. By doing so, we may uncover core structure motifs formed through mechanisms such as synergetic folding, enabling a structure-based understanding and prediction of a wider range of IDP-IDP interactions that play crucial roles in cellular functions. We anticipate that integrated approaches like those adopted here will contribute significantly to unveiling the elusive mechanisms underlying other IDP-IDP interactions.


## Materials and Methods

### DHFR PCA Plasmid Construction and Screen.
  The DHFR-PCA vector was constructed by splitting the E. coli DHFR gene at the V88 site into two complementing fragments, F[1,2] (N terminus) and F[3] (C terminus), as previously reported (17). Although TMP can inhibit the endogenous DHFR of E. coli and lead to the loss of the ability to grow and proliferate, an excessive amount of active exogenous E. coli DHFR can still resist a certain concentration of TMP (43). The wild-type or mutated 4.1G fragments were fused to the N terminus of F[3] with a GSAGSAAGSGEF linker, and their binding partners were separately fused to the C terminus of F[1,2] with a (GGGGS)2 linker. The 38 candidate fragments of 4.1G partners were synthesized by General Biotech. The constructed plasmid was transformed into E. coli NEB 10-beta cells (New England Biolabs) and expressed under the pCAT promoter. Exponential-phase cells grown in LB medium at 37 °C were harvested and normalized to an A600 value of 1.0 with PBS. Two microliters of gradient dilutions of cells ranging from 100 to 10−5 were spotted on M9 agar plates containing various concentrations of TMP. After approximately 18 h of incubation at 37 °C, the growth for each dilution at each TMP concentration was examined.


### Alanine Scanning Mutagenesis.
  The alanine (for nonalanine wild-type sites) or serine (for alanine wild-type sites) single mutants were produced based on the DHFR plasmid using high-throughput mutagenesis as previously reported (44). The I1814D, I1815D, and I1817D mutations of NuMA26 were also made in the same way. TMP resistance measurements were performed on the total 96 mutants expressing cells with 2 µg/mL TMP at different OD600 values (0.5, 0.1, and 0.01).


### Saturation Mutagenesis Libraries and High-Throughput Sequencing.
  The key residues identified by alanine scanning (I1814, I1815, I1817, M1819 of NuMA and I954, I958, Q974, A975, I976, A979, H983, V988 of 4.1G) were chosen for double-site mutation, with one mutated site from NuMA and the other from 4.1G. The double-site saturation mutagenesis library was constructed by GENEWIZ. In addition, a single-site saturation mutagenesis library covering the remaining 14 hydrophobic amino acid sites (L1823, V1825 of NuMA and I941, V945, I949, V959, I960, I966, L972, M986, V991, V992, V993, L999 of 4.1G) was constructed by overlap-extension PCR using degenerate NNK primers. After electroporation and outgrowth, the plasmids from non-TMP and TMP (2 µg/mL) plates were isolated and sequenced on an Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform.

  The sequencing data were analyzed by counting the occurrences of each residue type at each position before and after screening. The log enrichment ratios (relative to the enrichment ratios of the corresponding wild-type residues) were determined. A pseudocount of 0.1 was added to the actual counts to avoid dividing by zero. The inverse of the number of occurrences of a residue type in the prescreening sample is used to indicate the certainty or confidence of the corresponding estimated enrichment ratio. Two (three) sets of independent experiments, including repeated culturing, pre- and postscreening, and sequencing, were performed on the single (double) mutation libraries. The reproducibility of the results was confirmed by examining correlations, between independent experiments, of the collective relative enrichment ratios of hydrophobic residues [i.e., alanine and residues with hydrophobicity scale values (45) larger than alanine] and of hydrophilic residues (residues with smaller hydrophobicity scale values than alanine).


### Designing Mutants by ABACUS-R.
  Based on the AF2-predicted structure for the 4.1G57-NuMA16 fusion peptide, we selected 27 solvent-exposed positions (positions 949, 950, 951, 952, 953, 955, 957, 959, 961, 987, 988, 989, 990, 991, 992, 993, 994, and 996 on 4.1G57 and 1812, 1814, 1816, 1818, 1820, 1822, 1823, 1824, and 1825 on NuMA16) as candidate sites for introducing mutations without disrupting the core structure. For each of the 17,550 possible 4-position combinations out of the 27 positions, ABACUS-R was used to select the quadruple mutants with the largest logits. The top three ranking mutants are B3, B5, and B6. These mutants together covered six mutated positions. Thus, we manually designed the five-site mutant B2 and the six-site mutant B1. The quadruple mutant B4 ranked 33 according to ABACUS-R logits, but all of its mutated positions were included in the six positions.


### Protein Preparation.
  DNA sequences encoding the 4.1G or 4.1G-NuMA fusion peptide were cloned into the BamHI and SacI sites of the pET.M.3C plasmid (9). To facilitate purification and concentration measurements, a tryptophan residue was added to the C terminus of each peptide. All plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) and induced at OD600 ~ 0.8 with 0.5 mM IPTG for 20 h at 16 °C. For the 1H-15N HSQC NMR study, uniformly 15N-labeled proteins were prepared by growing the bacteria in inorganic medium (24 g/L KH2PO4, 5 g/L NaOH, 0.5 g/L 15NH4Cl, 2.2 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2, and 2.5 g/L glucose) using 15NH4Cl as isotope source. Selenomethionine-labeled protein was used to solve the B5 structure. Cells were harvested and sonicated in buffer containing 20 mM Tris and 500 mM NaCl at pH 8.0. All proteins were initially purified by Ni2+-affinity chromatography. Then, the His-tag was removed by incubating with HRV-3C protease at 4°C for 16 h. The proteins were further purified by gel filtration using a Superdex 75 column with the ÄKTA purifier system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). The purified proteins were confirmed by SDS‐PAGE, and the concentrations were determined by measuring the UV absorbance at 280 nm.


### ITC.
  ITC measurements were performed on a PEAQ-ITC Micro calorimeter (MicroCal) at 25 °C. Assays were performed in buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM EDTA. Forty microliter aliquots of solutions containing 0.8 mM NuMA peptides were injected into the cell containing 40 µM 4.1G fragments at 2-min intervals. Data were analyzed using Origin software provided by Microcal. NuMA peptides were synthesized by GenScript company.


### MALS.
  MALS analyses were performed on an AKTA™ pure HPLC system (GE Healthcare, USA) coupled to a DAWN HELEOS II eight-angle light-scattering and a refractive-index detector (Wyatt Technology, USA). 0.1 mL (8 mg/mL) of 4.1G-NuMA fusion proteins was passed over a Superdex 75 increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) in 20 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM EDTA. Data were analyzed with the ASTRA (v7.0.1) software (Wyatt Technology) and graphed using Origin 2020.


### NMR Spectroscopy.
  All NMR data were acquired at 298 K on a Bruker 600 Avance III spectrometer equipped with triple resonances, self-shielded z-axis gradient probes. The NMR samples typically contained ~0.2 mM 15N-labeled proteins, 25 mM MES monohydrate, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.05% (v/v) TFA (pH 6.8), and 10% (v/v) D2O. Data were processed using NMRPipe (46) and analyzed using the program Sparky 3 (http://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/home/sparky/). HSQC spectra were overlaid using Topspin 4.1.0 (Bruker).


### Crystallography.
  Crystallization screening was conducted at 289 K using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method with commercial screens. Approximately 1.4 µL of purified selenomethionine-labeled proteins at ~10 mg/mL was added to 0.7 µL of crystallization buffer. The crystals used for data collection were grown for 1 wk in 90 µL 0.2 M Trimethylamine N-Oxide dihydrate, 0.1 M Tris, pH 9.4, 20% w/v Polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 2,000, and 10 µL 0.1 M TCEP hydrochloride. Crystals were briefly soaked in a reservoir solution supplemented with 20% glycerol (v/v) and then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Datasets were collected on BL18U1 at 2.9 Å and processed using XDS (47) at the Shanghai Synchrotron Facility. The Crank2 package was used to calculate the phase by the SAD method. Automatic protein model building was performed with the Autobuild module in PHENIX (48). The model was iteratively corrected using the program Coot (49) and was refined using PHENIX. Molprobity was used to evaluate the final refined model. The statistics for data collection and structural refinement are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1.


### MD simulations.
  For the 4.1G67/NuMA26 complex system, the initial structure was constructed based on the crystal structure of mutant B5. Terminal loops that were missing from the crystal structure were modeled as random coils. The all-atom protein was solvated in a cubic box of side length 12.754 nm, containing 67,273 water molecules. One Cl− ion was added to neutralize the system. The simulations were conducted using the GROMACS-2021.5 software package (50) with the CHARMM36 force field (51) and TIP3P water model. The system was first energy minimized using the steepest descent algorithm. It was further equilibrated through 100 ps constant volume (NVT) simulations at 300 K followed by 1 ns constant pressure (NPT) simulations at the same temperature. Position restraints on the heavy atoms of the protein were applied during energy minimization and equilibrium MD simulations. The simulation time step length was 2 fs, with all bonds constrained using the LINCS algorithm (52). Electrostatic interactions were treated with the Particle-Mesh-Ewald method, and van der Waals interactions were calculated with a cutoff of 1.2 nm. The NVT sampling simulation lasted for 200 ns, with the coordinates saved every 20 ps for analysis.
