# A New Phase
of Networking: The Molecular Composition
and Regulatory Dynamics of Mammalian Stress Granules


## Abstract
  

  Stress granules (SGs) are cytosolic biomolecular condensates that form in response to cellular stress. Weak, multivalent interactions between their protein and RNA constituents drive their rapid, dynamic assembly through phase separation coupled to percolation. Though a consensus model of SG function has yet to be determined, their perceived implication in cytoprotective processes (e.g., antiviral responses and inhibition of apoptosis) and possible role in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and frontotemporal dementia) have drawn great interest. Consequently, new studies using numerous cell biological, genetic, and proteomic methods have been performed to unravel the mechanisms underlying SG formation, organization, and function and, with them, a more clearly defined SG proteome. Here, we provide a consensus SG proteome through literature curation and an update of the user-friendly database RNAgranuleDB to version 2.0 (http://rnagranuledb.lunenfeld.ca/). With this updated SG proteome, we use next-generation phase separation prediction tools to assess the predisposition of SG proteins for phase separation and aggregation. Next, we analyze the primary sequence features of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within SG-resident proteins. Finally, we review the protein- and RNA-level determinants, including post-translational modifications (PTMs), that regulate SG composition and assembly/disassembly dynamics.


## Introduction

### Biomolecular Condensates: A Source of Cellular
Organization and Responsiveness
  To simultaneously coordinate tens of thousands of unique processes with precise temporal and spatial control, eukaryotic cells have evolved multiple ways to compartmentalize their molecular constituents. Membrane-bound organelles, such as the nucleus, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum, are widely recognized as the principal form of subcellular compartmentalization. The identity and stoichiometry of their molecular inventories are carefully controlled by machineries embedded within their lipid membranes, such as nucleoporins, transporters, and pumps. By contrast, a diverse array of membraneless organelles contribute to subcellular organization without the aid of a strict lipid enclosure.1 Rather, membraneless organelles, or biomolecular condensates, are concentrated, nonstoichiometric bodies of biomolecules assembled and supported by phase transitioning.

  Biomolecular condensates exist in distinct phases from their surrounding liquid environment. A phase describes a chemically and physically uniform region within a physical system. Although a single phase can be system-spanning, the conditions within complex cellular systems often give rise to multiple phases that coexist in dynamic equilibrium. These phases are mutable and can undergo significant restructuring in response to internal and external influences. While “phase transitions” have historically been used to describe transitions in density, the recent application of network science in biological systems has helped contextualize these changes with regard to their molecular connectivity.2 We now understand biomolecular condensates as subcellular bodies assembled and maintained through a combination of density (liquid–liquid phase separation) and network (percolation) transitions that support their structure internally.2

  Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a density transition characterized by the demixing of molecules within a solution into distinct light and dense liquid fractions.1,3 Our understanding of LLPS is based on in vitro measurements of isolated components that have been carefully measured and analyzed using the combined expertise of polymer chemistry and physics. Ordinarily, ideal solutions tend toward a single, homogeneous phase to minimize the global free energy of the system. Here, the lowest free energy state is achieved by maximizing the entropic contribution of mixing, leading to a high degree of solute–solvent interactions as the solutes (e.g., proteins) are evenly dispersed throughout the system (e.g., the cytosol). However, under conditions that increase the energic favorability of solute–solute (e.g., protein–protein) interactions, such as seen with high concentrations of multivalent proteins, the entropic contribution of mixing is not sufficient to overcome the enthalpic benefit of substituting higher energy solute–solvent interactions for lower energy solute–solute interactions.4 In this case, minimizing interaction potentials through the partitioning of solute molecules into separate high- and low-density fractions minimizes global free energy; in cellular systems, this is achieved by the phase separation of macromolecules (proteins and mRNAs) into distinct biomolecular condensates (Figure 1A). These concepts are derived from Flory–Huggins ideal solution theory.5,6

  Therefore, LLPS is a thermodynamic process dictated entirely by solute concentration (initiated beyond a threshold saturation concentration, Csat) and other modulators that influence the interaction strength between component molecules (e.g., temperature, pressure, pH, etc.). While LLPS is crucial for biomolecular condensate formation, it alone cannot fully describe their membraneless features, as condensates observed in cells and in vitro exhibit non-Newtonian characteristics that are not a natural consequence of phase separation.7−11 As such, the coupling of LLPS to networking transitions, such as percolation, more adequately explains the formation and material properties of biomolecular condensates.2

  Network percolation occurs within a system of multivalent nodes that surpasses a critical concentration threshold (Cperc), prompting widespread internodal connectivity to form a single system-spanning network (Figure 1B).13 In biological systems, this is enabled by the multivalency of site-specific or sequence-specific interactions between folded protein domains, linear motifs within intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), and individual residues, collectively termed “stickers”.14 Reversible interactions between stickers act as the physical cross-links underlying biological network percolation. Key evidence supporting network percolation was recently derived from a study of FET family RNA-binding proteins (FUS, EWSR1, TAF15).10 At a concentration well below an effective Csat, these proteins display a clear distribution of nanoscale clusters, contradicting the classical nucleation theory of LLPS. As an alternative mechanism, this phenomenon is explained by network percolation, where the FET family RNA-binding proteins form clusters upon reaching the Cperc threshold.

  At the amino acid sequence level, the same multivalent interactions that promote LLPS, such as electrostatic (charge–charge), π-π, cation-π, and hydrogen bond interactions, also enable such physical cross-linking.2,15 These network transitions provide the stabilizing forces within condensates that bestow their viscoelastic properties. As LLPS and percolation underlie the formation of biomolecular condensates within cells, it is important to consider the molecular determinants of these biophysicochemical transitions.


### Introduction to Stress Granules (SGs)
  Like their membrane-bound counterparts, membraneless organelles exhibit a wide range of functions and compositions.1 Among them, stress granules (SGs) are a cytosolic, RNA-rich variety that form transiently in response to adverse cellular conditions.16 These condensates assemble rapidly during cellular stress in a highly coordinated, stepwise fashion17,18 and then dissolve soon after stress is relieved.19,20 Following the initial discovery of SGs and their molecular components (TIA-1, TIAR, and polyadenylated mRNAs) by fluorescence microscopy,21 global transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have revealed these assemblies to contain hundreds of unique proteins and thousands of mRNAs.22−26 Though we will briefly discuss the molecular processes underlying SG dynamics (assembly and disassembly) and their putative functions, we recommend Hofmann et al.27 and Glauninger H et al.,17 respectively, for in-depth reviews concerning these topics.

  SGs have been assigned a myriad of functions, including the post-transcriptional regulation and stabilization of mRNAs during stress,28,29 the suppression of cell death via sequestration of specific signaling molecules (RACK1,30 ROCK1,31 and DDX3X32), and various roles in the antiviral response.33,34 However, conflicting observations, such as reports that fail to identify any observable effect of SG formation on mRNA transcript fate,35,36 and the wide spectrum of assigned functions have made it difficult to assign a consensus model of SG function from published literature. Furthermore, their specific functions are often inferred from compositional analyses of microscopically visible SGs or by measuring the phenotypic consequences of genetic alterations. For example, an investigation may knock out essential SG nucleators (e.g., G3BP1/2) to abrogate SG formation and assess the functional consequences. However, interpretations of these results have some caveats. First, SGs may exist in functionally active submicroscopic forms. Despite lacking microscopically visible SGs, proteomic analyses have revealed proximal interactions between SG proteins prior to SG assembly,24,26 suggesting that SG function may be independent of a highly assembled state (for details, see Glauninger et al.17). Second, as SG proteins are not entirely sequestered within SGs (only 18% of total G3BP1 is estimated to occupy SGs37), genetic manipulations might impact additional biological processes, such as ribosome-associated quality control,38 thereby obfuscating functional readouts. This unfortunate consequence prevents the assignment of SG function based purely on phenotypic observations. Overcoming these complications will require improved technologies. This includes the application of improved microscopy to detect nanoscale condensates, possibly by super-resolution microscopy or fluorescence cross-correlation spectroscopy of endogenously tagged SG components.39,40 Additionally, synthetic models, such as optogenetically induced SGs using the Corelet or OptoGranule systems,41−43 will allow for the isolation of SG-specific functions without the added influence of cellular stress.

  One significant motivation for the functional characterization of SGs regards their tightly linked relationship with various neurodegenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and its related spectrum of disease, frontotemporal dementia (FTD). The aggregation of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43, TARDBP) in neuronal cells is a hallmark of ALS and is observed in ∼97% of ALS patients.44,45 Less frequently, aggregated fused in sarcoma (FUS) is also evident in ALS- and FTD-affected patient tissues. TDP-43 and FUS are metastable and can transition from liquid to gel-like (or glass-like) and then to amyloid fibril structures in vitro, supporting the idea that dysregulated transition of TDP-43 and FUS underlies their aggregation in aging neurons.44 Conceptual advancement in understanding the ALS-FTD relationship with SGs came with reports identifying various ALS-associated mutations in genes encoding SG proteins (including FUS and TDP-43) altering the assembly/disassembly dynamics of SGs and inducing TDP-43 or FUS cytoplasmic mislocalization to SGs. For example, ALS-associated mutations in TIA-1 delay SG disassembly and recruit TDP-43 to SGs.46 In addition, toxic dipeptide repeats (DPRs) produced from ALS-causing GGGGCC repeat expansion within C9orf72 also mediate aberrant SG kinetics and induce TDP-43 cytoplasmic aggregation. Generated through repeat-associated non-AUG (i.e., start codon-independent) translation of GGGGCC repeats, C9orf72 DPRs include glycine-alanine (GA), glycine-arginine (GR), proline-alanine (PA), proline-arginine (PR), and glycine-proline (GP) repeats.47−49 Expression of poly-PR induces the formation of SGs and alters their disassembly dynamics in cellulo.50 Furthermore, poly-GR200 drives the cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43 and its coaggregation within SGs in mouse brain tissues.51 Together with reports of SG markers colocalizing with TDP-43 and FUS inclusion bodies in ALS- and FTD-affected patient samples,52,53 these observations propose a possible role for SGs in promoting ALS and FTD pathogenesis by recruiting TDP-43 and FUS to the cytoplasm and promoting their aggregation.54 However, how SGs would enable TDP-43 and FUS to transition from liquid to gel-like (or glass-like), and then to amyloid fibril structures,44 is still an open question.

  With that said, the exact relationship between SGs and ALS-FTD disease pathogenesis continues to be hotly debated. Other reports show that the RNAs enriched in SGs prevent aggregation of TDP-43 and that TDP-43 aggregation occurs outside of SGs,55−57 disrupting the idea that SGs seed TDP-43 aggregation. Furthermore, in contrast to previous reports that observed transiently expressed TDP-43 ALS variants localizing to SGs, TDP-43 M337V is not found in SGs following heat stress that robustly induces SGs in the mouse central nervous system.58 Resolving these controversies would require careful analyses of endogenous TDP-43 or FUS localization and aggregation processes, alongside SG markers and ideally in an in vivo ALS/FTD model system.


### SG Assembly and Disassembly

#### Upstream Events Triggering SG Assembly:
Release of mRNAs from Polysomes
  With few exceptions, the onset of cellular stress is followed by translational arrest through activation of the integrated stress response (ISR). The ISR is a stress response pathway conserved across eukaryotic cells to better endure adverse conditions by limiting protein synthesis and focusing energy resources toward the expression of specific stress-response genes. Activation of the ISR relies on the phosphorylation of eIF2α (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha) by one or more of four eIF2α kinases. These kinases are suited to detect specific cellular imbalances, including heme deprivation (EIF2AK1/HRI), viral infection (EIF2AK2/PKR), ER stress (EIF2AK3/PERK), or amino acid deprivation (EIF2AK4/GCN2).59 Phosphorylated eIF2α prohibits the formation of eIF2-GTP-Met-tRNAi ternary complexes, thus blocking the loading of ribosomes onto mRNA—the rate-limiting step of translation initiation.60 This causes widespread disassembly of polysomes (complexes of multiple actively translating ribosomes along a single mRNA) thereby releasing large quantities of unbound mRNA into the cytosol. This cytosolic pool of mRNAs is dynamically incorporated into SGs and other related RNA granules, such as processing bodies (PBs), and this dynamic incorporation is necessary for SG formation and stability.61 For example, disrupting the equilibrium of mRNA flux by stabilizing polysomes, using chemicals such as cycloheximide or emetine, induces the disassembly of SGs formed by ISR-activating stressors.20

  Chemical inhibition of eIF4A, a component of the eIF4F complex, is an alternative means to prevent translation initiation and induce SG formation. Chemicals that block eIF4A’s ability to bind RNA (e.g., hippuristanol) or induce clamping onto RNA and deplete eIF4A in the eIF4F complex (e.g., rocaglates and Pateamine A) trigger SG formation in an eIF2α-independent manner.62 Other stressors can induce SG assembly by directly altering intracellular conditions to induce phase separation coupled to percolation. For example, molecular crowding caused by hyperosmotic stress induces SGs that are resistant to the effects of polysome-stabilizing agents.63 In this case, the assembly and maintenance of SGs are uniquely uncoupled from the state of translation and the pool of unbound mRNA; however, this mechanism remains poorly understood. These divergent assembly pathways, along with subtle changes in their recruited proteins, have led to the definition of various SG subtypes, discussed in detail by Ivanov and Kedersha.64


#### Multivalent Interactions Driving SG Assembly:
RNA–protein, Protein–Protein and RNA–RNA Interactions
  Following the imposition of stress, SG assembly is catalyzed by the cumulative effect of many weak, multivalent interactions among the newly available mRNAs with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs).65 In fact, over 80% of the SG proteome is annotated with RNA-binding ability.66 It is widely held that a combination of protein–protein, protein–RNA, and RNA–RNA interactions supports the phase-separated state of SGs (Figure 2). Homotypic protein–protein interactions driven by the nuclear transport factor-like (NTF2L) domain of the essential proteins G3BP1/2 have been shown to be critical for SG formation.42,67 Appropriate G3BP1 levels are also crucial for efficient formation, as loss of or mutations in its upstream regulators (e.g., YBX1, also called YB-1, and TDP-43) impair SG assembly.68,69 However, this G3BP-centric model covers only a portion of the highly connected SG proximal protein–protein interaction network26 and cannot explain SGs formed in contexts that do not rely on G3BP1/2 (i.e., hyperosmotic stress conditions).

  Protein–RNA interactions are also crucial for SG formation. For example, removal of the RNA-binding domain of G3BP1 completely abolishes SG assembly.67,70 Other experiments using the SG Corelet system to investigate highly connected SG “nodes” (G3BP1, CAPRIN1, and UBAP2L) identified RNA-binding capacity as a necessary feature for the formation of SGs.42 Although the role of RNA–RNA interactions in SG formation has not been tested directly, these interactions can drive the formation of RNA condensates in vitro when RNA concentrations are high (150 μg/mL).71 In addition, SG-resident eIF4A helicases actively limit RNA–RNA interactions to prevent extensive recruitment of RNAs into SGs.72

  While the relative contribution of these interactions is unclear, their cumulative effects drive SG assembly and maintenance through phase separation coupled to percolation.2 These intermolecular interactions are likely responsible for other SG characteristics (Figure 2). For example, the dynamic exchange of SG components with the cytosol, as observed by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching,18 relies on the transient nature of these weak interactions. Additionally, nanoscale SG organization, observed as cores or subcompartments by high-resolution microscopy, most likely relies on the affinities of specific interaction networks. Specific proteins such as UBAP2L, G3BP1, and IMP1 have been observed to localize to non-overlapping subregions within SGs.73,74 While we are beginning to understand the sub-compartmental organization of SG proteins, the sub-compartmentalization of SG-resident mRNAs has yet to be determined.

  SG proteins boast specific features that support their dynamic condensate properties. Prominently, SGs are highly enriched for proteins containing large intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs).24−26,66 These protein segments lack a stable conformation, instead fluctuating between disordered and ordered states depending on the nature of their amino acids and binding state.75 For certain IDRs, their disordered structure stems from electrostatic repulsion between highly abundant polar and charged residues.76 Additionally, IDRs generally lack hydrophobic residues, which typically contribute to secondary and tertiary structures by limiting conformational landscapes. As a result, these regions assume a continuum of differently disordered conformations77 or ensembles of rapidly fluctuating conformations,78,79 all with variable levels of secondary structure.76 A significant fraction of IDRs are low-complexity (LC) sequences, marked by their enrichment for a small subset of naturally occurring amino acids. LC regions can be further classified based on their sequence features and phase separation properties.4,80 Other IDRs can adopt polymeric “cross-β” structures, such as those seen in amyloids or prion proteins, and are called prion-like domains (PrLDs). Their ability to form “cross-β” structures relies on their enrichment for specific amino acids, such as glycine and polar amino acid residues (glutamine, asparagine, serine, and tyrosine).80,81 TDP-43 and FUS are notable proteins containing PrLDs.

  The essentiality of IDR–IDR interactions in SG assembly has not yet been shown. While their flexible structural conformations enable the weak multivalent IDR–IDR interactions necessary for the phase separation of numerous SG proteins in vitro, such as hnRNPA1, FUS, and TDP-43,82−85 these proteins are not essential for SG formation. Additionally, whereas the C-terminal IDR domain of G3BP1 enables RNA-binding activity, it is the structured NTF2L domain that mediates the protein–protein interactions necessary for SG formation.42,67,86 Furthermore, the central IDR in G3BP1 provides an autoregulatory mechanism to keep the protein inhibited via intramolecular IDR–IDR interactions, until polysome-released mRNAs relieve this inhibition and enable G3BP1-mediated SG nucleation.67,86 That said, while not essential for SG formation, IDR–IDR interactions likely provide redundant mechanisms to promote G3BP-mediated condensation. For example, in the absence of G3BP1/2, expression of the C-terminal IDR of UBAP2L induces self-IDR–IDR association and is sufficient to induce SG formation.42

  Positively charged IDRs play an important role in SG formation by directly interacting with negatively charged RNAs. For instance, RG/RGG motifs within IDRs are RNA-binding segments, often found as repeats, that permit multivalent protein–RNA interactions.87,88 Positively charged lysine residues are enriched in the IDRs of proteins that localize to the cytoplasmic RNA granule, P-bodies, and promote phase separation with RNAs.89 The importance of such cationic IDR–RNA interactions is well demonstrated in simple in vitro condensation assays using arginine- or lysine-containing peptides mixed with RNAs.89−91 For example, while unable to phase separate on their own or with negatively charged bovine albumin, PR dipeptide repeats (PR30) mixed with various forms of RNA result in the spontaneous formation of condensates with distinct material properties.90

  Lastly, IDRs are also platforms for various post-translational modifications (PTMs) that can fine-tune IDR-mediated interactions. PTMs in IDRs can change their interaction affinity or alter their conformational state, thereby modulating their influence on phase separation.92 These modifications allow for tight control of interactions that mediate condensate formation, giving the cell flexibility in its response to various stress conditions. This will be further discussed in section 4.1.


#### Mechanisms Controlling SG Disassembly
  The mechanisms driving the disassembly of SGs are less well characterized but likely involve several independent pathways. Whereas SG assembly can begin as early as 5 min following stress initiation,86 complete SG disassembly often takes several hours.93,94 A notable exception occurs in cold shock-induced SGs, where disassembly is complete within 5–10 minutes after stress relief.19 During their disassembly, SGs break into smaller foci which are then dissolved individually,18 likely reflecting a stepwise reversal of the multivalent interactions involving proteins and RNAs. A likely mechanism of SG disassembly is the decreased availability of ribosome-free mRNAs that follows the resumption of translation, as SGs require a constant influx of translationally inactive mRNAs.20 It should also be noted that translation resumes well before SGs fully disassemble. Targeted SunTag-based translation imaging analysis of 5′ terminal oligo pyrimidine-containing mRNAs showed that nearly half of the transcripts are actively translated early during stress recovery when SGs are still present.95 However, the total recovery of polysomal ribosomes to pre-stress levels can lag several hours behind the complete dissolution of SGs.96,97

  The initial activation of the ISR also prepares for translation reinitiation by selective translation of negative ISR pathway regulators. During stress, phosphorylated eIF2α delays the regeneration of the eIF2-GTP-Met-tRNAi ternary complex, allowing the 43S preinitiation complex to bypass upstream open reading frames (ORFs) and translate downstream ORFs. This includes ATF4, a transcription factor that induces the expression of GADD34 (PPP1R15A), a phosphatase regulatory subunit that recruits phosphatase 1 to eIF2α to provoke its dephosphorylation, thereby alleviating translational arrest.98

  Additional secondary mechanisms include pathways that systematically degrade/disassemble SGs. These secondary processes tend to rely on either general disaggregases (e.g., VCP),99,100 autophagic proteins (ULK1/2, C9orf72, and SQSTM1)100,101 or molecular chaperones (e.g., HSPB8-BAG3-HSP70 complex).102 Indeed, impairment of these components significantly delays SG disassembly and can induce aberrant SG forms.96,99−102 Although the functions of these proteins were identified in stress conditions where their activity is important for preventing nonspecific protein aggregation (e.g., heat shock for VCP and ULK1/2, and proteasome inhibition for HSPB8-BAG3-HSP70), mutations in VCP103,104 or loss-of-function of these proteins (ULK1/2100) also delays the disassembly of sodium arsenite-induced SGs, suggesting their general roles in SG clearance. The mechanisms of SG disassembly also depend on the duration and severity of the initial stress context. For example, whereas SGs formed from acute heat stress rely on autophagy-independent disassembly pathways, the clearance of SGs induced by prolonged heat stress requires autophagy activity.105,106

  Numerous reports identify PTMs as key regulators of SG disassembly. These modifications, such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and SUMOylation, can influence disassembly through direct modification of disassembly machinery or by altering SG resident proteins.25,100,105 The role of PTMs in regulating SG dynamics is discussed in detail in section 4.1.


### Focus of This Review
  In this review, we discuss and evaluate the many determinants that influence SG phase separation and percolation. We begin with a literature-curated update of the SG proteome and subsequent gene ontology (GO) and domain enrichment analyses of high-confidence SG proteins. We then provide an analysis of the intrinsic properties of SG proteins and their IDRs that contribute to phase separation. Finally, we discuss the various PTMs that influence SG dynamics and protein-specific recruitment, as well as the characteristics of SG mRNAs and their role in SG regulation.


## Update of the SG Proteome: RNA Granule Data
Base v2.0
  With the goal of understanding the intrinsic features and properties of the SG proteome, we manually curated the literature to generate an up-to-date gold-standard SG protein catalog. These efforts produced an updated version of RNA granule database (RNAgranuleDB v2.0), a comprehensive protein database for mammalian SGs and processing bodies (PBs), which are related condensates.66 For the purpose of this review, we limited our literature curation to studies reporting protein localization to SGs or some functional association with these organelles, published in the years since the initial launch of RNAgranuleDB.

  Using SG-related keywords, we retrieved ∼650 research articles published after our first literature curation (PubMed [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/]: Feb 2019 to Nov 2021) and entered experimental evidence from ∼100 primary research articles using the same curation annotation strategy as version 1.0.66 Briefly, curated information is divided into “discovery-based” or “candidate-based” approaches (to differentiate unbiased screening vs hypothesis-driven studies), organized into specific evidence categories (cell biological, physical, genetic, etc.) and then finally organized according to the specific assay or data set (evidence type) (Figure 3). Any evidence or data sets supporting a protein association with SGs is linked to the corresponding PubMed Identifier (PMID). Users can search, filter, and download these data in the updated database, RNAgranuleDB v2.0 (http://rnagranuledb.lunenfeld.ca/), which is released with this review. RNAgranuleDB v2.0 now contains 13,552 curation entries for 4,731 proteins in mammalian cells (human, rat, or mouse).

  The newly curated data include individually tested cell biological evidence (e.g., colocalization) and physical associations identified through proximity-dependent biotinylation techniques (APEX and BioID)25,107 or affinity-purification coupled to mass spectrometry (AP-MS).108,109 We also incorporated proteins predicted to localize to “cytoplasmic RNA granules” in a large-scale BioID map of the human cell.110 This cell map was generated from BioID screening of 192 subcellular markers at steady state, which identified ∼35,900 unique interactions with ∼4,400 high-confidence proximal interactors. This study was centered on the idea that proteins with highly correlated BioID labeling patterns likely reside in the same complex, compartment, or subcellular location26 and predicted the intracellular locations of ∼4,100 proteins in the human proteome by defining groups of endogenous proteins with similar BioID labeling patterns. This global analysis included the prediction of 155 proteins to “cytoplasmic RNA granules” (SGs or PBs).

  To define the SG proteome, we utilized a weighted scoring system, in which evidence type is weighted according to the strength of its support for a protein’s residence in SGs (Table 1). We maintain the same overall structure and scoring system as version 1.0. Microscopy-based colocalization evidence remains the strongest evidence for SG residence (weight 7), followed by prediction from endogenous prey-based analysis of BioID (weight 5), physical association evidence (e.g., AP-MS) (weight 1–3), genetic evidence (weight 1), and finally, RNA-binding evidence (weight 0.1). Individual scores were summed to generate “SG-PB curation scores”, ranging from 0.1 to 48.5, allowing us to separate proteins into four tiers based on levels of confidence. Note that proteins with high scores often localize to both SGs and PBs. As with version 1.0, we use the arbitrary score cutoff of 7 to define the “Tier 1 SG-PB proteome”, which includes 473 proteins. Using this cutoff, proteins would be considered Tier 1 proteins if they have evidence for either (1) colocalization with known SG or PB marker(s), (2) residence in SGs or PBs based on endogenous prey-based analysis as well as additional evidence for physical association, or (3) physical association with known SG or PB marker(s) from two or more data sets plus other associations (e.g., genetic) with a total score of 7 or above.

  To focus our analysis solely on the SG proteome, we first extracted Tier 1 (SG and PB proteomes) and excluded proteins known to localize specifically to PBs using information in the database (66 proteins in total, see Methods section). This generated a list of 407 proteins that defines an up-to-date catalog of SG proteins, henceforth referred to as the SG proteome (Table S1).


## Analysis of the SG Proteome

### Gene Ontology Analysis of the SG Proteome
  Despite the outstanding amount of work characterizing the biophysical and biochemical properties of SGs and their protein and RNA inventories, recent reviews and perspectives recognize that we lack definitive evidence of their function.17,111 Nevertheless, understanding the function and nature (e.g., characteristics or stress-specificity) of the proteins within SGs remains an invaluable means of directing further functional investigations and understanding disease-associated phenotypes.

  GO enrichment analysis of our updated SG proteome using ShinyGO 0.76 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/)112 reveals GO term enrichment for different facets of translational control, as well as a myriad of functions across RNA metabolism (Figure 4). Proteins involved in translation initiation (GO:0006413), and the positive (GO:0045727) and negative (GO:0017148) regulation of translation reveal that a wide range of translation modulators are sequestered to SGs. Proteins involved across the RNA life cycle with effects on RNA localization (GO:0050658), splicing (GO:0008380), stability (GO:0043489, GO:0050779), or silencing (GO:0016441) highlight the extent to which SGs can indirectly influence the fate of RNA. GO:MF enrichment for the ability to bind messenger (GO:0003729) or regulatory (GO:0035198) RNAs, or exhibit translation initiation factor (GO:0003743) or helicase (GO:0003724) activity further supports SG functions in post-transcriptional regulation and translation. Detailed GO enrichment analysis results are summarized in Table S2.

  Of note is the striking enrichment of ALS-associated proteins per the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Though not entirely surprising due to the implication of SGs and SG-resident proteins in proteinopathy diseases along the ALS-FTD spectrum,113,114 what is striking is the absolute number of SGs proteins linked to disease pathogenesis. In fact, we identified nearly 15% of the SG proteome (60 proteins; Table S3) as having at least one piece of clinical or experimental evidence tying them to ALS (KEGG; HPO; OMIM; ClinVar; Humsavar).115 While the current view of SGs as vehicles for pathological protein aggregation focuses on key genetic causes of familial ALS (e.g., mutant TDP-43, FUS, etc.), the involvement of SG proteins in the pathogenesis of this disease is far more extensive. This prompts us to reconsider the role of SGs in ALS pathogenesis beyond a simple “conduit” to protein aggregation, but rather as a nexus of biomolecules whose altered states synergistically contribute to ALS disease progression, by localizing (or failing to localize) to SGs and changing SG functions.


### Domain Analysis of the SG Proteome
  Having expanded our list of Tier 1 SG proteins by nearly 50%, we were motivated to replicate our previous efforts to identify specific folded domains and motifs enriched in the SG proteome (Figure 5A). A total of 70 domains and motifs were significantly enriched in SG proteins relative to the total human proteome, six more than previously identified66 (Table S4). Unsurprisingly, many hallmark domains/motifs that reportedly contribute to phase separation, and which previously had high enrichment scores, remained highly enriched in the SG proteome. These include several well-characterized RNA-binding domains (RRM, KH, DSRM), RNA-binding RG/RGG motifs, helicase domains (DEAD box, helicase C-terminal domain), argonaute family domains (ArgoN, ArgoMid, ArgoL2/1), and zinc finger motifs (zf-CCHC, zf-CCCH).

  Our curation of a study highlighting the sequestration of ER-Golgi trafficking proteins to SGs116 added five SEC family proteins to the SG proteome. With the addition of these proteins, we found four SEC family domains (Sec23_trunk, Sec23_BS, Sec23_helical, zf-Sec23-Sec24) to be enriched in the updated SG proteome. That aside, few significant changes were observed otherwise across the remaining list. It is worth noting that the number of proteins harboring RNA-binding domains and motifs increased slightly (Figure 5B). This reaffirms our previous findings on enrichment of RNA-binding or RNA-associated domains in the SG proteome66 and indicates that the significant fraction of newly added SG proteome had no appreciable domain or motif enrichment other than SEC family domains.


### Phase Separation Capacity of the SG Proteome
  As described above, SGs are viscoelastic condensates that are primarily maintained by phase separation.2 Given that the material properties of SGs are imparted by the characteristics of their protein and RNA constituents, we sought to examine the inherent properties of SG-resident proteins from our updated database. Multiple SG proteins have been shown to phase separate either on their own or in two-component systems alongside RNA, including but not limited to G3BP1, FUS, TDP-43, YTHDF1–3 and MAPT (Tau).83,86,117−121 Based on these observations, our first goal was to identify what fraction of the SG proteome has the intrinsic capacity to phase separate in isolation. Many software tools have been developed to identify proteins with intrinsic properties predicted to promote phase separation. The first generation of these tools, such as R+Y or DDX4-like, relied on the recognition of very specific features identified in specific groups of proteins known to phase separate.122 Newer “second-generation” phase separation predictors leverage machine learning to detect novel inherent, unbiased markers of phase separation.

  A previous study had found that phase separation proteins (PSPs) were significantly enriched in the v1.0 high-confidence (Tier 1) SG protein set when compared to all (Tiers 1–4) SG proteins or the entire human proteome.123 Our analysis leveraged six first-generation prediction tools, PLAAC,124,125 LARK,126 R+Y,84 DDX4-like,127 CatGranule,128 and PScore,129 each employing unique predictors of phase separation. However, a recent comparison has reported relatively low overlap between these tools in their identification of PSPs, owing to their divergent criteria.122 To obtain comprehensive insight into the phase separating ability of SG proteins, we analyzed the SG proteome using both the first-generation and relatively recent “second-generation” predictors: PSPer, FuzDrop, PSAP, PSPredictor, and LLPhyScore (Table 2). Here, we briefly review the second-generation tools that were used to predict protein phase separation, followed by a summary and analysis of our findings.


#### PSPer
  PSPer aims to identify prion-like RNA-binding PSPs.130 The software employs a hidden Markov model that was trained using molecular determinants found to be important for the phase separation of 22 FUS family proteins.84 Its identification of PSPs relies on a computed probability score derived from the combined presence of prion-like domains, RRM domains, and RG-rich regions that identify larger functional phase-separating regions. We find that PSPer predicts that 110 of the 407 Tier 1 SG proteins (27.0%) have an intrinsic propensity to phase separate (∼3.6-fold enrichment compared to the human proteome, Table S5).


#### FuzDrop
  Using sequence-based profiling, FuzDrop identifies protein regions that promote droplet formation to predict if a protein is prone to forming reversible phase-separated droplets.131−133 These regions are identified by comparing the predicted conformational entropy of their free vs bound states, under the assumption that droplet-forming proteins are conformationally heterogeneous (i.e., disordered) both natively and upon binding.132,134,135 FuzDrop then estimates a protein’s probability of spontaneous LLPS (PLLPS) by using the calculated number of droplet-promoting regions and their estimated number of hydrophobic interactions. “Droplet drivers”, proteins with PLLPS ≥ 0.60, are predicted to spontaneously phase separate under physiological conditions, whereas “droplet clients”, proteins with PLLPS < 0.60, are only predicted to phase separate upon binding to partners. This tool identifies 242 droplet-driving proteins in the SG proteome (59.5%; 1.6-fold enrichment) and 165 proteins as droplet clients (40.5%). Additionally, FuzDrop identifies aggregation-prone regions and predicts the likelihood of conversion to amyloid states. The SG proteome also contains 357 proteins (87.7%, no significant enrichment) harboring at least one aggregation-prone region, consistent with the expected rate within the human proteome (Table S6).


#### PSAP
  PSAP predicts a protein’s proclivity for phase separation by evaluating the compositional, biochemical, and sequence features of its primary structure.136 The tool’s machine learning classifier was trained using 55 shared primary sequence features from a manually curated list of 90 human PSPs. Features most highly correlated with phase separation according to the training set, such as low leucine and cysteine content and high IDR percentage, enable the random forest classifier to generate phase separation probability scores for any protein sequence. Our analysis of the entire human proteome using PSAP generated a ranked list according to their probability scores, where we find 104 SG proteins (25.6%) in the top 1,500 (∼3.4-fold enrichment; Table S5).


#### PSPredictor
  PSPredictor is sequence-based prediction tool that was created to address the inherent bias of early phase separation predictors.137 As noted above, early predictors such as PScore, DDX4-like, and PLAAC relied on the presence of very specific protein features to identify PSPs. As such, their calculations carried inherent biases toward the protein types that helped develop their scoring criteria. Using a machine learning algorithm trained with an extensive, manually curated LLPS database (LLPSDB),138 PSPredictor minimizes protein type bias by strictly evaluating a protein’s sequence and compositional information. However, as a result, the tool fails to provide region-specific information or identify specific features that promote phase separation. Nonetheless, when evaluating the entire human proteome, we find 123 SG proteins (30.2%) in the top 10% of PSPredictor probability score (∼3-fold enrichment; Table S5).


#### LLPhyScore
  LLPhyScore specializes in predicting IDR-driven phase separation.139 Understanding the ability of multiple physical interactions to synergistically drive LLPS, this machine learning-based tool evaluates a broad set of physical interactions and features to provide a detailed analysis of the propensity of a given protein for phase separation. Its three-layered neural network-like model considers the physical features, residue types, residue counts, and positions of an amino acid sequence to infer statistics for multiple LLPS-driving physical interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and π–π contacts. By training the model with a manually curated set of PSP sequences, as well as an extensive set of non-phase-separating sequences, LLPhyScore offers unbiased prediction of protein phase separation, as well as a quantitative breakdown of the physical features responsible. We find 109 SG proteins (26.8%) in the top 10% of the human proteome according to LLPhyScore (∼2.7-fold enrichment; Table S5).


#### Analysis of the SG Proteome Using Phase
Separation Predictors
  Using the aforementioned first- and second-generation phase separation predictors, we analyzed all SG proteins catalogued in RNAgranuleDB v2.0 (Table S5, see Methods). Evaluating fold enrichment for PSP identification in Tier 1 (high confidence) and Tiers 1–4 (total) SG protein sets against the background frequency of the entire human proteome revealed a far greater propensity for the phase separation of SG proteins across all predictors (Figure 6A). Second-generation predictors generally had lower enrichment scores than those of the first generation. This was primarily a result of the newer predictors using machine learning algorithms that synthesize generalizable indicators of phase separation, rather than looking for specific features that were identified and selected from specific subsets of phase-separated proteins, which include SG-resident proteins. These changes are better at avoiding bias and tend to predict a larger fraction of the human proteome to have the capacity to phase separate, hence the lower enrichment of PSPs in the SG proteome. Tier 1 invariably scored higher than Tiers 1–4, ranging from 1.6- to 9.2-fold enrichment relative to the total human proteome, compared to 1.1 to 2.2 for the latter. Of the 407 Tier 1 SG proteins, nearly three-quarters (72.7%, n = 296) were identified as PSPs at least once, and although each tool relies on a unique set of scoring criteria, a significant fraction of the proteome (59.0%, n = 240) were predicted to phase separate by two or more (Figure 6B). Roughly 30% (n = 125) were extensively cross-validated (5+ predictors), thereby demonstrating that a wide range of molecular features could facilitate their phase separation. These include key proteins involved in SG assembly (G3BP1, PRRC2C, UBAP2L, CAPRIN1), as well as established ALS-associated genes such as FUS and many hnRNP proteins.

  PSP assemblies are often metastable, displaying the ability to transition from dynamic liquid-like states to less dynamic solid or amyloid states.140 SGs contain a roster of aggregation-prone proteins that have been observed to convert to amyloid states (e.g., FUS, TDP-43, hnRNPA1),56,82,83 supporting a possible role for SGs in the conversion of various ALS-associated proteins into pathogenic amyloid states.43,141 For this reason, we utilized the quantitative information provided in FuzDrop to compare the droplet-promoting and aggregation-promoting regions of the SG proteome to the “droplet drivers” of the human proteome. Tier 1 SG proteins had significantly higher FuzDrop probability scores (PLLPS) than both Tiers 1–4 SG proteins and human droplet drivers (Figure 7A). However, SG proteins have a smaller fraction of droplet-promoting regions when compared to droplet drivers (Figure 7B), and as such, the higher PLLPS scores of SG proteins are likely a consequence of other factors, such as a greater affinity for hydrophobic interactions. Interestingly, SG proteins contain significantly more aggregation-promoting regions than droplet drivers (Figure 7C), which make up a larger fraction of their primary sequence (Figure 7D, Table S6). Given the number of ALS-associated proteins that localize to SGs, it is important to evaluate their amyloid-promoting capabilities. Our observations highlight the need to better understand the role of aggregation-promoting regions in the SG proteome (e.g., intermolecular interactions with metastable ALS-associated proteins), to elucidate the roles of SGs in protein aggregation.

  In summary, our analysis highlights the high phase separation and aggregation capacity of SG proteins and the diverse array of phase separation-promoting features embedded in their primary amino acid sequences. However, the methods employed in this analysis are constrained by their evaluation of phase separation at the individual protein level, and we are far from understanding the phase separation of SGs as complex mixtures of proteins and RNAs. Future efforts to study the phase separation of complex protein/RNA mixtures, using techniques such as in vitro reconstitution142 or artificial SGs generated from cell lysates,143 will be necessary to better understand the synergistic phase separation properties of multicomponent systems.


### Feature Analysis of SG IDRs
  Recent characterization of G3BP1 highlights the importance of multivalent interactions enabled by its folded domains (NTF2L, RRM) and regulatory mechanisms facilitated by disordered regions.42,67,86 However, we have yet to understand the roles IDRs play in SG assembly, organization, and function. As noted above, IDRs occupy a significantly greater fraction of the primary sequence of SG proteins when compared to the average human protein.24,25,66,144 The ability of IDRs to facilitate many weak, multivalent interactions has proven extremely important for density transitions82−85 and likely also contributes to network transitions necessary for condensate formation. Thus, feature analysis of the SG IDRome might help us identify the specific characteristics of SG IDRs that define their function and group SG proteins based on their shared IDR features, to reveal shared molecular properties that enable their dynamic and specific assembly into SGs. For this reason, we evaluated 87 physicochemical sequence features (e.g., net charge, charge-mixing [kappa], fraction of charged residues, KL-hydropathy) and short linear motifs (e.g., kinase phosphorylation consensus motif) of SG IDRs using the human IDRome, a comprehensive data set of 20,668 predicted IDR sequences extracted from 13,386 human proteins using SPOT-Disorder145 (See Methods).


#### Distinct Features of SG IDRs Relative to
Human Proteome IDRs
  Of the 20,668 unique sequences in the human IDRome, 538 IDRs (∼2.6%) were identified across 322 IDR-containing SG proteins (Table S7). Using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, we identified 26 features that are significantly different across the SG IDRome when compared against the human IDRome (Table S8, Figure Supplementary). Notably, the SG IDRome displayed significantly lower KL-hydropathy scores (padj = 2.99e–13) than the human IDRome (Figure 8), indicating lower mean hydrophobicity. While initially surprising given the role of hydrophobic interactions in enhancing phase separation and self-assembly,146−148 a lack of hydrophobic residues may contribute to the disorder of IDRs by failing to limit their conformational landscape. Furthermore, our prior FuzDrop analysis indicated a greater propensity for SG proteins to form hydrophobic interactions, meaning the hydrophobicity of amino acids outside of IDRs might be higher.

  SG IDRs were also found to be enriched for hydrophobic G- and RG-rich repeats (Figure 8, padj = 9.4e–12 and 0.04, respectively). These specifically enriched hydrophobic residues might be more effective at forming stabilizing IDR-IDR (G-rich repeats) and IDR-RNA (RG-rich repeats) interactions. Additionally, recent studies show that hydrophobic interactions serve as the dominant driver of phase separation at extreme salt concentrations (1–2 M).149−151 Although our SG proteome is based on experimental evidence tested in various stress conditions, G- and RG-rich IDRs may help SG formation in response to hyperosmotic stress (0.1–0.2 M concentrations), such as that induced by high salt conditions. A clear definition of the SG proteome under hyperosmotic stress conditions would be required to test if G- and RG-rich IDRs are further enriched in the SG proteome induced by these conditions.


#### Specific Features Shared within Subgroups
of SG IDRs
  Next, with the goal of identifying specialized groups of SG IDRs with shared properties, we analyzed features of the SG IDRome against the distribution of the entire human IDRome (see Methods). These groups were visualized using hierarchical clustering (Figure 9A). The most notable result was a large cluster of IDRs harboring glycine-containing repeats (glycine alone or serine-glycine, phenylalanine-glycine, or arginine-glycine dipeptides) (Figure 9B). Notably, this group contained several ALS-associated genes, including FUS, TDP-43, TAF15, and HNRNPA1/2B1. Interestingly, the G-rich regions of FUS, TDP-43, and TIA-1 enable their phase separation and subsequent conversion to reversible aggregate states.152 Similarly, other proteins in this cluster may exploit these G-rich regions to engage in homotypic interactions or heterotypic interactions with other G-rich proteins.

  A second cluster enriched in serine-arginine (SR) repeats and phosphorylation consensus sequences for several kinases (AKT1/PKB, LATS, and GSK3) includes two DEAD-box helicases (DDX3X and DDX3Y), five SR splicing factors (SRSF1/3/5/7/9), and TRA2B (Figure 9C). SRSF proteins are components of the spliceosome and are all capable of shuttling between the nucleus and cytoplasm depending on their post-translational modification status.153−155 Notably, SRSF3 is known to be phosphorylated by AKT1/PKB kinase, whose phosphorylation consensus sequence includes R residues and thus shares similarity to the SR repeats in this cluster.156 Therefore, it is possible that specific repetitive motifs in IDRs can embed potential phosphorylation motifs for kinases, but further testing is needed to confirm this hypothesis. In summary, we used hierarchical clustering of SG IDR features to reveal two classes of IDRs enriched in the SG IDRome, comprising G-rich and SR-repeat-containing IDRs. Future experimental studies on these motifs in IDRs would bring insights to their function.


## Regulation of Stress Granule Dynamics

### Protein-Level Control: Post-translational
Modifications (PTMs)
  A key mechanism to readily control the phase separation of SG proteins is the alteration of their biochemical properties by post-translational modification. PTMs serve as effective molecular switches to modulate various protein properties by influencing a protein’s abundance, physical interactions, localization, or activity. With regard to SGs, PTMs have been shown to regulate their assembly/disassembly and protein composition (Figure 10), often by directly enhancing or attenuating a protein’s interactions with other protein or RNA components. Below, we review the different types of PTMs known to modulate SG dynamics (assembly/disassembly) and selective protein recruitment.


#### Poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation/(PARylation)
  The addition of nucleic acid-like units of ADP-ribose to a protein is known as ADP-ribosylation. ADP-ribose is conjugated to charged amino acids (lysine, arginine, glutamic acid, aspartic acid) as monomers (mono(ADP-ribosyl)ation) or as part of larger linear or branched polymeric chains (poly(ADP-robosyl)ation/PARylation). The latter form, PARylation, is catalyzed by PAR polymerases (PARPs) and reversed by PAR glycohydrolase (PARG) (reviewed in Gupte et al.157) and has repeatedly demonstrated involvement in SG regulation. Interestingly, five PARPs (PARP5A/12/13/14/15) and PARG demonstrate SG localization.158 Indeed, PAR is enriched in SGs and PARylation has been observed on key SG proteins such as G3BP1, TIA1, ZC3HAV1, and AGO1–4.158

  As ADP-ribose units are negatively charged, PAR chains serve as binding sites for folded domains (e.g., PAR-binding zinc fingers; WWE, PIN, and RRM domains; and oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding folds) and sequence motifs (SR/KR repeats and RGG/RG motifs).159,160 PAR chains also act as platforms for multivalent interactions.161 For example, PARP1 is recruited to sites of DNA damage within seconds, generating PAR chains that further recruit PAR-binding proteins that repair DNA.83,162−164 This includes FUS, whose recruitment to DNA damage sites is dependent on its RGG motifs interacting with PAR.162,165 As opposed to RNA that stably associates with FUS, PAR engages in transient interactions and potentiates FUS condensation at far lower concentrations.166 It is important to note that PAR does not compete with RNA in FUS binding, but rather increases FUS’s transient binding to RNA, as shown in a simple in vitro system.166 A similar in vitro reconstituted system testing the effect of PAR on SG formation using the essential nucleators G3BP1/2 and mRNAs would be useful to characterize the relative contribution of PAR in SG formation.

  In cells, PAR modification controls multiple aspects of SG assembly/disassembly dynamics. For example, reducing PARylation delays SG assembly whereas cells with enhanced PARylation exhibit slower SG disassembly during stress recovery.158,167 Similarly, overexpression of PARG inhibits SG assembly through the nuclear relocalization of G3BP1 under DNA damaging conditions.168 Such PARylation-mediated modulation of SG assembly and disassembly is also exploited by viruses. For instance, the nonstructural protein 3 of alphaviruses suppresses the formation of virus-induced SGs by exerting its (ADP-ribosyl) hydrolase activity on G3BP1.169,170

  PARylation also supports the selective recruitment of proteins to SGs, as highlighted by their influence on the SG localization of ALS-associated proteins. Oxidative stress-induced PARylation of the ALS-associated protein hnRNPA1 promotes its translocation to the cytoplasm and subsequent recruitment to SGs.167 Similarly, PAR enhances the phase separation of TDP-43 in vitro, as well as its relocalization from the nucleus to SGs in mammalian cell cultures and neurons.119 This process is dependent upon the PAR-binding motif embedded within TDP-43’s nuclear localization signal sequence, which presumably recognizes PARylated proteins in SGs. These observations indicate a role for PARylation in disease-associated SG phenotypes, particularly in ALS genetic contexts.


#### Phosphorylation
  Numerous experiments have demonstrated the ability of phosphorylation to modulate the formation, number, and size of SGs. The addition of phosphate groups to serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues on SG proteins occurs primarily in response to cellular stresses that activate specific kinase signaling pathways. Most notably, the phosphorylation of eIF2α, described above, is crucial for SG formation through the ISR, though many other SG proteins are substrates for this modification. Phosphorylation has variable consequences for its protein substrates, in some cases enhancing/stimulating SG association and in other cases promoting their exclusion from SGs. For example, phosphorylation of FMR1 and FXR1 by PAK1 enhances their localization to SGs,171 while SRPK-mediated phosphorylation of CIRBP suppresses its phase separation and SG recruitment.172 Phosphorylation of ZFP36 promotes its binding to 14-3-3 proteins and concomitant exclusion from arsenite-induced SGs,173 while the same mechanism of 14-3-3 binding favors the association of phosphorylated MEX3B with PBs rather than SGs.174

  Other experiments reveal more profound influences of phosphorylation on stress granule formation. For example, hyperphosphorylation of GLE1A isoforms by JNK, ERK, and GSK3 during stress recovery impairs its oligomerization and ability to modulate the ATPase activity of the DEAD-box helicase DDX3X, subsequently promoting SG disassembly.175,176 Similarly, the dissolution of SGs following acute heat stress is reliant upon phosphorylation of the ATPase VCP by the serine/threonine protein kinases ULK1/ULK2.100 Finally, phosphorylation on G3BP1 affects SG dynamics, a point recently clarified after a long controversy. Early findings that demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between G3BP1 S149 phosphorylation status and SG assembly177 were later questioned after discovering additional mutations in the phosphomimetic variant used in the study.178 However, recent studies confirm that phosphorylation at S149 and S232, flanking the acidic IDR, diminishes G3BP1’s ability to phase separate,42,67,86 corroborating data supporting the functional role for S149 phosphorylation of G3BP1 in neurons.179 G3BP1 phosphorylation likely increases the strength of the autoinhibitory interaction between its acidic IDR and its positively charged, RG-rich IDR, decreasing its ability to phase separate.

  There is an interesting precedent of kinases (from the evolutionarily conserved DYRK family) being capable of “dissolving” multiple membraneless organelles, including SGs, further implicating phosphorylation as a regulator of SG dynamics. In mammalian cells, DYRK3 accumulates during mitosis, at which point it dissolves splicing speckles, the pericentriolar matrix, and SGs in a kinase-activity-dependent manner.180,181 In C. elegans, DYRK/MBK-2 dissolves perinuclear germ granules by phosphorylating key P-granule components.182 In both cases, spatial and temporal accumulation of DYRK kinase are tightly linked to dispersion of these membraneless organelles. Phosphatases that reverse phosphorylation events have been more elusive, although the phosphatase DUSP12 localizes to SGs and has been implicated in the dispersal of SGs after stress removal.183


#### Arginine Methylation
  The methylation of arginine by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs), particularly within RGG/RG motifs, was recently shown to regulate phase separation.87,184 Generally, arginine methylation within RGG/RG motifs exerts a negative influence on SG assembly, potentially by limiting the capacity of proteins to form interactions with RNA, PAR, or other transient interactors. Indeed, methylating the RGG motif of FMRP reduces its capacity to form π–π interactions, one of the key biophysical forces driving phase separation.185 Given the prevalence of proteins with RGG/RG motifs in the SG proteome, regulation of arginine methylation may provide a flexible mechanism to modulate the assembly of these structures. For example, G3BP1 methylation by PRMT1 and PRMT5 prevents the formation of large SGs,186 while its demethylation by JMJD6187 promotes SG assembly. Similarly, amino acid substitutions that prevent methylation within the RGG motifs of hnRNPA1 moderately stimulate SG formation.188 Methylation can also facilitate SG clearance, as shown in the case of methylated FUS being targeted for autophagic degradation by the Tudor domain-containing protein SMN1.101

  Like phosphorylation, there is growing evidence for the impact of arginine methylation on protein localization to SGs. For instance, asymmetric dimethylation of TOP3B in its RGG motif promotes its SG localization, in part through a moderate increase in its association with its auxiliary factor, TDRD3.189 By contrast, methylation of FUS and CIRBP suppresses their recruitment to SGs.172,184 It is worth noting that 338 of 1449 human proteins containing RGG/RG motifs also include proximal RS/SR sites, suggesting a potential crosstalk between methylation and phosphorylation.172 Indeed, phosphorylation within the RGG/RG motif of CIRBP blocks methylation at that region and vice versa, although both modifications negatively regulate CIRBP’s association with SGs.172


#### Acetylation
  Biochemically, modifications on positively charged lysine residues can affect the net charge of the polypeptide region much like the methylation of arginine residues within RGG/RG motifs. Likewise, lysine acetylation is a means to directly regulate the phase separation tendencies of polypeptides by inhibiting their ability to form weak, multivalent interactions with RNA. Indeed, phase separation of lysine-rich peptides mixed with RNA is readily reversed by acetylation in vitro.89 Lysine acetylation has been extensively characterized in the context of transcriptional and epigenetic control through its post-translational modification of histone proteins (reviewed in Choudhary et al.190). However, its role in regulating SG dynamics is relatively unexplored.

  Once again, G3BP1 serves as a key regulatory target for this modification. CREBBP/EP300-mediated acetylation of G3BP1 interferes with its RNA-binding activity, thereby abolishing its RNA-dependent interaction with PABPC1 and promoting SG disassembly.191 Similarly, acetylation in the RRM domain of ALS-associated FUS mutants impairs their RNA binding ability, while acetylation in the nuclear localization signal of these mutants facilitates their localization to SGs.192 Importantly, acetylation marks are reverted by lysine deacetylases allowing acetylation status to be modulated dynamically during stress. For instance, while the N-terminal IDR of the DEAD-box helicase DDX3X is acetylated during stress onset, its deacetylation by HDAC6 is essential for SG growth and maturation.193 HDAC6, a lysine deacetylase shown to regulate microtubule dynamics and chaperone activity,194,195 localizes to SGs, where its deacetylase activity is important for efficient SG assembly.196 Similarly, the deacetylase SIRT6 facilitates both SG assembly as well as post-stress disassembly.197 Whether additional key acetylation substrates regulate SG dynamics remains to be determined.


#### Ubiquitination
  In contrast to most other PTMs, ubiquitination is notable in that it involves the conjugation of a protein (ubiquitin) to other protein substrates through an enzymatic chain reaction (reviewed in Mattiroli and Sixma198). Much like ADP ribosylation, ubiquitination can occur by single addition of ubiquitin units (monoubiquitination) or in the form of linear or branched polymeric chains (polyubiquitination). The exact contribution of ubiquitination in regulating SG dynamics remains controversial, as inhibition of ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1 ligases) was shown to have little impact on SG assembly and disassembly dynamics.199 However, recent studies have demonstrated ubiquitination as a key regulator of SG dynamics in a stress-specific manner. For example, K63-linked polyubiquitination is vital to an autophagy-mediated SG disassembly mechanism unique to heat stress.105,106 Other studies have shown that ubiquitination and the proper removal of ubiquitin conjugates by ubiquitin peptidases are required for SG disassembly after heat shock.200,201 Understandably, ubiquitin chains are found enriched in a subset of SGs induced by heat shock200,201 but also during overexpression of aggregation-prone proteins.202

  However, the contribution of ubiquitination to SG assembly is unclear. Although two SG proteins carrying ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domains, UBAP2L and UBQLN2, have been found to regulate SG formation,26,203,204 their roles do not depend on the UBA domain. Together, these observations support a role for ubiquitination in regulating SG disassembly, but its impact on SG assembly remains unknown.


#### SUMOylation
  SUMOylation is a PTM involving the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to lysine residues, through an enzymatic cascade akin to ubiquitination (relying on E1–3 ligases). Like ubiquitination, this modification is important for regulating SG disassembly, especially in relation to ALS genetic contexts. For example, SUMO4 mutations identified in ALS patients cause decreased SUMO4 mRNA and protein expression and are associated with significantly more SGs per patient fibroblast in response to oxidative stress.205 These mutations also resulted in reduced SUMOylation of the ALS-associated protein VCP, suggesting a possible causative link, as SUMOylation was previously shown to direct VCP recruitment to SGs, where it serves a key role in SG disassembly.206 In fact, mutations aimed at obstructing SUMO attachment to VCP directly impaired the disassembly of SGs.

  Proteomic analysis of SGs identified a large number of “disassembly-engaged proteins” (DEPs) that are recruited specifically during SG disassembly.25 Among these DEPs are several proteins involved in SUMOylation, including the E1 SUMO ligase SAE1, the E2 ligase UBE2I, and the E3 ligases RANBP2 and TOPORS. Indeed, small inhibitory RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown of UBE2I and RANBP2 caused marked delays in SG disassembly. Interestingly, ALS-associated C9orf72 DPRs directly reduced DEP recruitment to SGs, including the aforementioned SUMO ligases, resulting in impaired SUMOylation and SG clearance. C9orf72 poly-PR DPRs have previously shown the capacity to induce SG assembly with markedly reduced molecular exchange kinetics.50 Whether impaired SUMOylation, or other PTMs, contribute to these less dynamic and possibly disease-related SG varieties demands further investigation.

  SUMOylation status has also been shown to directly influence stress-specific localization or exclusion of SG proteins. For example, while the attachment of SUMO to eIF4A2 promotes its recruitment to SGs in response to arsenite stress, the same is not true for heat shock or hippuristanol-induced SGs.207 Conversely, inhibition of SUMOylation, either by mutations that block SUMO attachment or overexpression of the deSUMOylating enzyme SENP1, causes the ALS-associated protein TDP-43 to be recruited to SGs.208

  SUMOylation may also regulate SG dynamics through the promotion of other PTMs. The SUMO-targeted ubiquitin ligase (StUbL) pathway is one such example,209 where SUMOylation primes protein ubiquitination. Specifically, RNF4 recognizes poly-SUMO chains on SG RNA-binding proteins and ubiquitinates these proteins during heat stress, a process enhanced by the inactivation of SENP family SUMO deconjugases. The StUbL pathway is important for SG clearance during stress recovery, as impairment of SUMO-primed ubiquitination dramatically delays the disassembly of SGs. Additionally, the StUbL pathway limits the SG localization of several ALS-associated proteins, including FUS, TDP-43, and various hnRNPs. Depletion of RNF4 or SUMO2/3 enhances the recruitment of ALS-associated FUS mutants to SGs, suggesting an additional role for SUMOylation in preventing the recruitment of aggregation prone RBPs.


#### NEDDylation
  The conjugation of the ubiquitin-like protein NEDD8 to lysine residues is known as NEDDylation. Unlike ubiquitination, which regulates a myriad of cellular processes, there are far fewer known targets of NEDD8 modification. Nonetheless, both NEDD8 and UBE2M, an E2 NEDD8-conjugating enzyme, localize to SGs under oxidative (arsenite), mitochondrial (clotrimazole), and ER (thapsigargin) stress.153 In fact, siRNA-mediated knockdown of NEDD8 or UBE2M significantly impaired SG assembly across all conditions, a phenotype mirrored by small molecule inhibition of NAE, the E1 NEDD8-activating enzyme. Additionally, proteomic analysis identified several SG-resident proteins as potential substrates for NEDDylation and showed that the NEDDylation of one of these candidates, SRSF3, is important for SG assembly during arsenite stress.

  However, much like ubiquitination, the significance of NEDDylation in regulating SG dynamics has been contested. Using the same pharmacological NAE inhibitor, Markmiller et al.199 completely suppressed NEDDylation without observing any appreciable consequence on the assembly or disassembly of SGs.


#### Glycosylation
  Glycosylation refers to an extensive group of PTMs that are mutually described by the enzyme-catalyzed addition of various carbohydrate groups to specific amino acids. Numerous forms of glycosylation exist, each characterized by different simple or complex carbohydrate groups, their own target amino acids, and corresponding glycosidic bonds. Consequently, these PTMs impact a diverse array of biological functions, including the regulation of SG dynamics. Specifically, the conjugation of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) to the hydroxyl groups of serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues, known as O-GlcNAcylation, has been observed to impact SG assembly210 and disassembly.211 In fact, global increases in O-GlcNAc modification are observed in response to chemical (arsenite)212 and environmental (heat)211 stresses.

  Using an RNA interference screen, Ohn et al.210 identified several components of the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP; responsible for reversible O-GlcNAc modification) that promote SG assembly by the O-GlcNAcylation of ribosomal subunits during stress. Indeed, siRNA knockdown of three HBP-related proteins (SORT1, OGT, and GFAT2) impaired arsenite-induced O-GlcNAcylation and severely reduced SG assembly. By contrast, O-GlcNAcylation of the SG protein eIF4G1 is important for the disassembly of SGs formed under heat stress.211 O-GlcNAc-modified eIF4G1 inhibits PABPC1 binding to mRNA, promoting the efficient translation of stress-responsive mRNAs via cap-independent translation during global translation shutdown, as well as facilitating SG disassembly post-stress. Furthermore, mutations that prevent the O-GlcNAcylation of eIF4G1 show impaired production of stress-responsive proteins and defective SG disassembly.


#### Hypusination
  Currently, the translation elongation factor eIF5A is the only SG protein known to be modified with the spermidine derivative hypusine.213,214 Nonetheless, chemical inhibition of this modification results in significant reductions in the number, size, and density of SGs in stressed cells.215,216 The inhibition of hypusination also reduces the arsenite-induced disassembly of polysomes, suggesting that hypusine-modified eIF5A may facilitate stress-induced translational repression.215 Indeed, arsenite stress-induced eIF5A hypusination promotes efficient SG assembly. Multiple stress conditions stimulate eIF5A accumulation in SGs, and knockdown of eIF5A expression inhibits the assembly of SGs induced by arsenite or thapsigargin, though not the assembly of Pateamine A- or clotrimazole-induced SGs.215 Hypusinated eIF5A also promotes the cytoplasmic retention and localization of TDP-43 to SGs, likely through their physical association.216


#### Arginylation
  The enzyme arginyl-tRNA protein transferase (ATE1) catalyzes the incorporation of arginine into target proteins either at the N-terminus or on the side chains of reactive internal amino acids.182,217−219 Under conditions of heat shock, calreticulin is arginylated as a necessary precursor to its oligomerization and association with SGs.220,221 In the absence of this protein, SGs are markedly reduced in size, suggesting that arginylated calreticulin is an essential scaffolding component of SGs.220 Additional evidence for arginylation-mediated control of SG dynamics has yet to be found.


### Stress Granule Regulation at the RNA Level
  Alongside proteins, RNAs serve as the second key architectural unit of SGs. In fact, even in the absence of stress, exogenous addition of mRNA (with puromycin to prevent its incorporation into polysomes) is sufficient to drive SG assembly in cellulo.222 Any means of disrupting RNA availability, such as enzymatic degradation (e.g., RNaseA, RNaseL)86,223−225 or inhibition of polysome disassembly (e.g., cycloheximide, emetine),20,222,226 rapidly dissolves SGs. The influence of RNA on SG formation occurs largely through its interaction with SG proteins. For example, RNA enhances TIA-1 self-association and phase separation, and the spontaneous assembly of SGs in cells overexpressing TIA-1 relies on its RNA recognition motif.227 Likewise, RNA binding-deficient mutants of G3BP1 no longer condense in the presence of RNA and cannot mediate SG assembly.67

  However, it should be noted that direct RNA–RNA interactions also influence SG formation and organization. Under in vitro conditions that mimic the cytosol, protein-free total RNA from Saccharomyces cerevisiae self-assembles into higher-ordered structures such as droplets or tangles, and these condensates can recruit RBPs.71,72 Further, the SG transcriptome includes a small group of antisense noncoding RNAs, most of which are antisense to SG-enriched long mRNAs.71 This suggests that RNA–RNA interactions may promote the partitioning of specific RNAs into SGs, although in vitro self-assemblies are not limited to complementary base pairing.71

  Both RNA condensates and purified SG cores concentrate additional RNAs at their surfaces.72 This stabilizing “RNA shell” has been speculated to promote further RNA–RNA interactions. This process is negatively regulated by the ATP-dependent DEAD-box helicase eIF4A, which becomes particularly enriched on the surfaces of SGs following oxidative stress.72 Chemical inhibition of eIF4A helicase activity results in enhanced RNA partitioning into SGs, while eIF4A overexpression impairs SG assembly. eIF4A inhibition also enhances SG-PB docking, demonstrating that the modulation of RNA–RNA interactions affects multiple aspects of SG, including their dynamics and contacts with other organelles. Single-molecule tracking experiments using an mRNA reporter derived from the gene KDM5B revealed a relatively rapid bidirectional exchange of mRNAs between SGs and PBs, as well as the capacity of reporter mRNAs protruding from the SG surface to mediate docking with PBs or other SGs.228 However, similar experiments with different mRNA reporters suggested that the frequency of mRNA exchange between SGs and PBs is very low, which may reflect reporter-specific dynamic properties that influence mRNA retention in SG/PBs.36

  SG assembly can also occur through specific RNA-mediated pathways. For example, stress-induced angiogenin-mediated tRNA cleavage produces tRNA-derived stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs)229,230 that contribute to a variety of stress responsive events, including eIF2α-independent SG assembly. Structure–function analysis revealed that it is tiRNAs derived from terminal oligoguanine motif-containing tRNAs that stimulate SG formation, suggesting that their tetramolecular G-quadruplex structures are responsible for their activity. This tiRNA-mediated pathway operates by inhibition of the eIF4F complex, much like chemicals that interfere with eIF4A activity (hippuristanol, rocaglates, Pateamine A), demonstrating a clear example of RNA-induced SG assembly.


#### Characteristics of SG mRNAs
  Several transcriptomic surveys have sought to characterize the RNA composition of SGs. RNA-seq analysis of purified SG cores found that SGs can include the global transcriptome, that no single transcript predominates in these assemblies, and that only a small fraction of the total transcriptome (10–13%) is present in SGs at a given time.23 The transcripts enriched in SGs are characterized by relatively low translation efficiency and a lack of association with ER or mitochondrial membranes. The degree of SG enrichment is strongly correlated with mRNA length, primarily in relation to ORF length, but the size of the 3′ UTR also influences SG association for transcripts less than 3 kb. The relationship between transcript length and SG enrichment has also been observed in experiments using the immunoprecipitation of SG components231 as well as analyses of stress-induced ribonucleoprotein granule assemblies purified by differential centrifugation.232,233 This latter mRNP transcriptomics study also found that AU-rich motifs were enriched in granule-associated transcripts across endoplasmic reticulum, arsenite, and heat stresses.233 Although the G-quadruplex structure in tiRNA plays a key role in SG formation, there is no observable enrichment for specific RNA structures in SG-targeted transcripts. At a dynamic level, ORF length correlates with the duration of mRNA interaction with SGs, and translationally active mRNAs only transiently interact with SGs.228 Using a fusion of eIF4A1 and the ascorbate peroxidase APEX2 to biotinylate and purify SG-associated RNAs, Padron et al.107 found that heat shock enriches for longer RNAs with less efficient translation rates, though this pattern was not observed in cells treated with hippuristanol. This difference may relate to the phospho-eIF2α-independent mechanism through which hippuristanol induces SGs,234 given that phospho-eIF2α-dependent stimuli such as sodium arsenite, thapsigargin, sorbitol, and heat shock yield similar SG transcriptional profiles.23,231

  The partitioning of RNAs into SGs is mediated by additional RNA–protein interactions. The RNA-unwinding enzyme YB-1 binds free cytosolic mRNAs and promotes polysome assembly, thus preventing the recruitment of these transcripts into SGs.222,235 As such, overexpression of YB-1 inhibits SG assembly, although the most notable phenotype arising from siRNA-mediated silencing of YB-1 is a reduced rate of SG disassembly during recovery from stress.222,235,236 Conversely, the ubiquitin segregase VCP releases stalled ribosomes from certain mRNAs via proteasome-mediated degradation and thus promotes their partitioning into SGs.237 The binding of free RNA by SG-resident RBPs provides a mechanism for active recruitment to SGs in which the number of RBP binding sites correlates with the extent of partitioning.231,238 For TIA-1, these binding sites comprise U/C-rich sequences in the UTRs of transcripts.239,240 The presence of multiple proximal TIA-1 binding sites promotes both TIA-1 phase separation227 and the recruitment of associated transcripts to SGs, which tend to harbour long 3' UTRs, as seen in the global SG transcriptome.23,241


#### RNA Modifications
  Aside from intrinsic sequence motifs, RNA methylation can also influence its targeting to SGs. Methylation at carbon 6 of adenosine (N6-methyladenosine, m6A) is the most abundant mRNA modification,242 and its levels increase following heat shock or arsenite stress.243,244 Importantly, m6A modification can promote nuclear export of RNAs,242 which could increase the pool of substrates for SG recruitment. The SG transcriptome is highly enriched for m6A-modified mRNAs relative to total cellular mRNA, with a strong correlation between the extent of m6A modification and partitioning to SGs.117,120,243 However, >97% of transcripts contain four or fewer m6A sites, and these sites are modified at rates of less than 100%.245 More recent bioinformatic analyses suggest that mRNA length has a stronger impact on SG partitioning than the presence of m6A sites.245 In terms of mechanism, m6A modifications are catalyzed by a writer complex comprising METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP and are recognized by the YTH domain-containing proteins YTHDF1/2/3, which variably colocalize with SGs following stress treatments.117,120,242,243,246 It should be noted that genetic depletion of writer complex components significantly reduces the accumulation of m6A-modified mRNAs in SGs, although SG formation is not impaired.120,243 While these data are based on the detection of mRNAs in bulk, a more quantitative evaluation of 11 different mRNAs did not reveal any significant differences in SG partitioning between wild-type cells and a mettl3 knockout.245 Variable observations have also been obtained from analyses of YTHDF genetic knockdowns, ranging from a significant impairment of SG localization by m6A-modified mRNAs to no detectable effect, potentially due to differences in experimental approaches.117,243 Overall, Khong et al.245 posit that RNA targeting to SGs results from the cumulative contribution of multiple factors and that m6A modification plays a relatively minor role. Interestingly, SG RBPs such as G3BP1/2, RBM42, CAPRIN1, and USP10 preferentially bind unmethylated sequences over m6A-modified RNAs,246,247 suggesting distinct mechanisms for methylation-dependent and -independent transcript recruitment into SGs.

  The association of mRNA with SGs is also influenced by the methylation of adenosine at carbon 1 (m1A) by TRMT6 and TRMT61A. TRMT6/61A-binding motifs are enriched in SG-sequestered mRNAs,248 consistent with oxidative stress stimulating the accumulation of both TRMT6/61A proteins and m1A-modified mRNAs into SGs.248 The importance of these events is illustrated by genetic knockdowns of TRMT6/61A, which exhibit impaired arsenite-induced SG formation as well as reduced cell viability following heat shock.248 Mechanistically, m1A methylation appears to enhance the rates at which transcripts are removed from the pool of translatable mRNAs under stress and returned to this pool upon stress removal.248 Whether m1A modification influences RNA recruitment to SGs has yet to be investigated.


## Conclusion and Perspectives
  Where initial efforts to characterize SGs relied primarily on cell biological methods, with the rise of mass spectrometry-based proteomics, the study of subcellular compartments has been approached with newfound vigor. However, due to the fragility of membraneless organelles, they were poorly amenable to the standard purification methods required (e.g., fractionation etc.). The development of proximity-dependent biotinylation techniques 10 years ago, notably BioID and APEX, revolutionized the study of biomolecular condensates by introducing a means to accurately sample their protein composition in native, cellular conditions. Since then, protein proximity labeling has been continually enhanced by techniques that improve spatial (e.g., Split-BioID, Split-APEX, Split-TurboID)249−252 or temporal (e.g., molecularly evolved TurboID/miniTurboID)253 resolution. Due to the transient nature of SGs, many of these tools have been and will continue to be instrumental in mapping the SG proteome. Orthogonal methods, such as live, single-molecule imaging technologies, yield additional information on the dynamics and functions of SGs.

  This review began with a thorough literature curation to generate the most comprehensive database of SG proteins available, enabling the definition of an up-to-date consensus of the SG proteome. As we draw near to a complete characterization of the SG proteome, it is important that we consider how to use this information to further extend our understanding of these organelles. Advancements in machine learning and other computational methods have given rise to new ways to analyze and interpret compositional data. Here, we have demonstrated the use of phase separation prediction tools to nominate proteins that contain intrinsic features with propensities for phase separation. An important caveat, however, is that the analysis of individual proteins in isolation does not provide the complete picture of a protein’s behavior within the complex milieu of in vivo SGs. Additionally, though we analyzed the SG IDRome to identify enriched features that might influence SG dynamics and organization, additional IDR analysis, such as the identification of evolutionarily conserved IDR features by the FAIDR statistical model,254,255 could yield additional information to help improve our understanding of these systems.

  Through our analysis of the SG proteome and review of the SG field, we find unresolved questions. We and others have found pre-existing proximal interactions that remain unchanged and a subgroup of proteins that are recruited upon formation of microscopically visible SGs.24−26What are the roles of pre-seeded and newly recruited SG proteins? Our analysis of the SG IDRome provided an overall view of subgroups of SG proteins sharing similar IDR features. Can we glean distinct nodes of interactions generated by their IDR–IDR interactions and further elucidate potential mechanisms underlying SG organization? We found that SG proteins harbor significantly more aggregation-prone regions, which may promote amyloid formation. What roles do these amyloid promoting regions have when ALS-causing aggregation-prone proteins localize to SGs? We summarized an array of post-translational modifications known to affect SG protein localization or assembly/disassembly dynamics. Do these post-translational modifications impart their effect via modulating percolation and phase separation thresholds in SGs?

  Answering these questions will require the joint effort of teams with different expertise (cell biology, biochemistry, biophysics, transcriptomics, and proteomics) to develop new tools and apply complementary techniques. One way to answer functional questions is to utilize synthetic tools, such as SG Corelets or other simple artificial SG systems built in vitro or from cell lysates, to better control the system. Other avenues of investigation include surveying the variation in SG interaction networks, identifying the key determinants of protein phase separation, and characterizing the modifications and mutations that contribute to the normal vs disease-relevant states of SG-resident proteins. As such, identifying the specific protein–protein, protein–RNA, and RNA–RNA interactions that underlie different phase-separated forms, using methods such as cross-linking coupled to mass spectrometry256 or APEX-seq,257 will help answer these fundamental questions. So too will studying the post-translational modifications that modulate their affinity for the physical interactions driving specific phase transitions. Further, the latest improvements in microscopy-based technologies, such as super-resolution microscopy (e.g., STED, SIM), cryo-electron tomography, fluorescence correlation spectroscopy, lattice sheet microscopy and combined optical systems (e.g., FOB microscopy258), promise new ways of investigating SG structure and organization. This combination of paralleled methodologies has the potential to drive important discoveries regarding SGs and other biomolecular condensates.

  With that said, understanding the intricacy of SGs is far from over. As we identify context-specific plasticity in the SG proteome, interactome, and transcriptome, more opportunities will present themselves to investigate the functions of SGs, both in regard to their response to stress and in relation to disease pathogenesis. These investigations will identify specific proteins and interactions responsible for stress-specific adaptation, as well as those that underlie disease-related forms.
