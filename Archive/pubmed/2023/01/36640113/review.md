## Review setup
- **Input scope** Abstract only
- **Assessment boundary** Claims and conclusions as presented in the abstract; no access to full text, figures, tables, or methods
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report a model liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) system driven by electrostatic interactions between anionic nucleic acids and cationic oligolysine peptides modified with 12 non-ionic amino acids. They determine lower critical peptide concentrations (C(crit)) via turbidimetric titrations and microscopy, and find that eight non-ionic amino acids inhibit LLPS with inhibition increasing with hydrophobicity, while three aromatic amino acids deviate and promote LLPS despite high hydrophobicity. They attribute this deviation primarily to interactions with DNA nucleobases based on comparisons with double-stranded DNA and polyacrylic acid.
- **Visible evidence base** Abstract text only; no experimental details, numerical data, figures, or statistical analyses provided
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Full methods, turbidimetric titration curves, microscopy images, C(crit) values for all peptides, correlation analysis details, amino acid index sources, control experiments, statistical treatment, and any supporting figures or tables

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a potentially interesting approach to quantifying the contribution of non-ionic amino acids to peptide-based LLPS, with a non-obvious finding that hydrophobicity correlates with inhibition for most residues while aromatic amino acids show the opposite effect. However, the evidence base is limited to the abstract, and several claims cannot be evaluated without access to the underlying data. The mechanistic attribution to DNA nucleobase interactions is particularly under-supported from the abstract alone.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying biomolecular condensates, LLPS in cellular biology, sequence determinants of phase behavior, and synthetic biology applications involving protocell models. The proposed "phase-separation propensity" index could be useful for predicting peptide and protein LLPS behavior, which is relevant to understanding cellular compartmentalization and designing biomimetic systems.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a timely and important question about sequence-encoded molecular grammar in LLPS. The systematic modification of oligolysine peptides with 12 different non-ionic amino acids provides a broad survey. The unexpected deviation of aromatic amino acids from the hydrophobicity trend is a potentially novel observation that could reveal specific molecular interactions.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Technical soundness
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "eight non-ionic amino acids inhibit the generation of LLPS, whereby the extent of inhibition increases with increasing hydrophobicity of the amino acids."
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; location not provided
- **Concern** The abstract states that C(crit) values were determined for each peptide, but no numerical values, error estimates, or statistical comparisons are provided. The claim that eight amino acids inhibit LLPS with a hydrophobicity-dependent trend requires quantitative data to support the correlation. Without access to the titration curves, the C(crit) determination method, and the statistical analysis, the robustness of this trend cannot be evaluated.
- **Why it matters** The central finding of the study is the relationship between amino acid hydrophobicity and LLPS inhibition. If this correlation is not rigorously established with appropriate controls and statistics, the proposed "phase-separation propensity" index would lack a solid foundation.
- **Resolution test** Provide the full dataset of C(crit) values for all 12 modified peptides, including replicates and error bars, along with the statistical analysis of the correlation with hydrophobicity indices. Show that the trend is consistent across multiple independent experiments and that the correlation is statistically significant.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Mechanistic interpretation
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "three aromatic amino acids deviate from this trend and rather markedly promote LLPS despite their high hydrophobicity" and that "this is primarily due to interactions with DNA nucleobases."
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; location not provided
- **Concern** The mechanistic attribution to DNA nucleobase interactions is presented as a conclusion, but the abstract only mentions a comparison with double-stranded DNA and polyacrylic acid. No details are given about how this comparison supports the nucleobase interaction mechanism. Alternative explanations, such as pi-pi stacking between aromatic residues or differences in peptide conformation, are not discussed. The abstract does not specify which three aromatic amino acids were studied or how the comparison experiments were designed.
- **Why it matters** The deviation of aromatic amino acids from the hydrophobicity trend is the most novel and potentially significant finding. If the mechanistic interpretation is not well-supported, the broader implications for understanding LLPS molecular grammar would be weakened.
- **Resolution test** Provide detailed experimental evidence showing that the aromatic amino acids interact specifically with DNA nucleobases, such as binding assays, mutagenesis studies, or spectroscopic measurements. Show that the effect is not observed with polyacrylic acid and that alternative mechanisms are ruled out.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Generalizability
- **Claim pointer** The authors claim that their approach "can be expected to help to provide a more accurate description and prediction of the LLPS propensity of peptides/proteins."
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; location not provided
- **Concern** The abstract describes a model system using oligolysine peptides and nucleic acids, but does not demonstrate that the proposed "phase-separation propensity" index applies to other peptide sequences, other polyanions, or full-length proteins. The generalizability of the findings beyond the specific model system is asserted but not shown.
- **Why it matters** The stated significance of the work depends on its applicability to predicting LLPS in more complex biological systems. Without evidence of generalizability, the practical utility of the index remains speculative.
- **Resolution test** Provide additional experiments with different peptide lengths, sequences, or polyanions to show that the propensity index is transferable. Alternatively, demonstrate predictive power by using the index to forecast LLPS behavior in a previously untested system.
