# Biomimetic SARS-CoV-2 Spike Protein Nanoparticles


## Abstract
  COVID-19 is an infectious respiratory disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). This virus contains a crucial coat protein that engages with target cells via a receptor binding domain (RBD) on its spike protein. To better study RBD and its therapeutic opportunities, we genetically engineered a simple fusion with a thermo-responsive elastin-like polypeptide (ELP). These fusions express in E. coli at a high yield in the soluble fraction and were easily purified using ELP-mediated phase separation (79 mg/L culture). Interestingly, they assembled peptide-based nanoparticles (Rh = 71.4 nm), which was attributed to oligomerization of RBDs (25.3 kDa) counterbalanced by steric stabilization by a soluble ELP (73.4 kDa). To investigate their biophysical properties, we explored the size, shape, and binding affinity for the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2) and cellular uptake. Biomimetic nanoparticles such as these may enable future strategies to target the same cells, tissues, and cell-surface receptors as those harnessed by SARS-CoV-2.


## INTRODUCTION
  Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a serious, infectious disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).1 As of March 2023, there have been approximately 760 million cases of COVID-19, including more than 6.8 million COVID-19-related deaths.2 To curb the spread of COVID-19, more than 56 verified vaccine candidates have been produced in North America, Europe, China, and Australia.3 Moreover, FDA-approved COVID-19 treatments, such as remdesivir, baricitinib, and tocilizumab are marginally effective in shortening the recovery time and mortality rate of patients hospitalized with COVID-19.4–8 In turn, the number of COVID-19-related deaths are expected to continue increasing without effective treatments and the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOC).

  The spike protein on the surface of SARS-CoV-2 is critical for cellular entry.9–11 SARS-CoV-2 spike protein consists of two subunits, S1 and S2.12 The receptor binding domain (RBD) of S1 facilitates SARS-CoV-2’s binding to the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2) on the surface of target cells. The virus then uses the host cells’ serine protease TMPRSS2 for spike protein priming.9 Cleavage at the S1/S2 boundary causes S1 to dissociate and S2 to undergo a structural change that allows S2’s fusion machinery to mediate the fusion of viral and cellular membranes.10 In the absence of TMPRSS2 or other membrane-bound proteases that allow fusion, coronaviruses can be internalized into the target cells via clathrin- and non-clathrin-mediated endocytosis.13

  Synthetic nanoparticles carrying the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein can be developed as tools for inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 entry into host cells and for targeted delivery of antiviral therapeutics. Mimicking the size of natural viruses, nanoparticles of optimal size (10–100 nm) enable efficient tissue penetration and facilitate cellular uptake.14 Our group employs elastin-like polypeptides (ELP) as genetically engineered biopolymers that can self-assemble into nanoparticles for drug delivery, tissue engineering, and potential vaccine development.15 ELPs consist of repeated pentameric motifs with the amino acid sequence (Val-Pro-Gly-Xaa-Gly)n where Xaa is the guest residue and n is the number of pentapeptide repeats. For example, A192 represents an ELP with 192 alanine (A) pentamers. When ELPs are heated above their inverse phase transition temperature (Tt), they phase-separate from bulk water phase into a more ordered, secondary structure called a coacervate. This process is reversible – ELPs become soluble in aqueous solution when they are cooled below Tt and their phase diagram depends on the hydrophobicity of the guest residue Xaa. It should be noted that a high substitution density of proline or charged residues (e.g. lysine, glutamic acid, aspartic acid) are usually avoided because they prevent coacervation below the boiling point of water at physiological pH.15, 16 Previously, our team showed that a fusion of knob domain of adenovirus serotype 5 fiber protein to an ELP, S48I48, efficiently exploited the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) internalization pathway to enter the lysosomal compartment of hepatocytes.17 This notable finding was a proof-of-concept that ELPs can be fused with a large protein virus-derived targeting ligand to assemble nanoparticles.

  Due to the need for novel therapeutic strategies against COVID-19 and more technology to elucidate SARS-CoV-2 cellular internalization mechanisms, we developed the first fusion ELP that self-assembles multivalent nanoparticles decorated with the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (spike-A192). The RBD, rather than the full-length spike protein, was expressed because the RBD’s relatively low molecular weight (~25 kDa) enables efficient fusion protein purification. This manuscript reports the ability of the RBD to oligomerize into stable, multivalent spike-A192 nanoparticles. These inhibit interactions between spike RBD and hACE2 protein through a nanomolar-binding affinity. Furthermore, spike-A192 exhibits high cellular uptake into the lysosomal compartment of hACE2-expressing lung epithelial cells. Thus, these protein-based biomimetic nanoparticles are promising tools for modeling SARS-CoV-2-like entry, disrupting the SARS-CoV-2 binding to hACE2, and may facilitate antiviral drug delivery to treat COVID-19.

### Spike-A192 vector design
  The pET-25(+) vector was purchased from Novagen (#69753) and further modified for ELP fusion cloning.18 The plasmid encoding the ELP, A192, was engineered in the same manner as that reported previously.17, 19–22 The spike RBD gene sequence was designed with recognition sites for the restriction enzymes NdeI and BamHI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) at the 5’ and 3’ of the spike RBD gene, respectively. A recognition site for BseRI was also included for recursive directional ligation plasmid reconstruction (PRe-RDL) described previously (Supporting Fig. S1).19 In brief, the plasmids containing genes encoding the spike RBD and A192 were purified from One Shot™ TOP10 Chemically Competent cells (#C404003, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using a QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit and were double digested by BssHII and BseRI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The digested fragments containing the spike RBD and ELP were purified with a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit and ligated together, transformed in TOP10 cells, and extracted using a Miniprep Kit. Successful clones were confirmed by diagnostic DNA digestion using NdeI and BamHI restriction enzymes. The digestion was visualized after electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (Supporting Fig. S1). Sanger sequencing of the plasmids with the primers specific for the T7 promoter (TAATACGACTCACTATAGG) and T7 terminator (GCTAGTTATTGCTCAGCGG) confirmed the insertion into the pET-25b(+) vector.


### Expression and purification of ELPs
  ClearColi® BL21(DE3) Electrocompetent cells (#60810, Lucigen, Middleton, WI) were transformed with the A192 or spike-A192 plasmid via electroporation. This cell line produces an incomplete lipopolysaccharide (lipid IVA) that lacks endotoxic activity in human immune cells. Lipopolysaccharide is an antagonist of the human Toll-like receptor (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation factor 2 (MD-2)-mediated signaling.23 The transformed colonies were cultured in 50 mL of autoclaved TB containing 100 μg/mL of carbenicillin at 37 °C for 16–18 hr. 25 mL of ClearColi® BL21 culture was transferred to an autoclaved 1 L of TB with 100 μg/mL carbenicillin to culture the bacteria at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached between 0.5 and 0.8. Each 1 L flask was then treated with 500 μL of 1 M isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to bring the final concentration to 500 μM IPTG. The IPTG-induced cultures were incubated overnight at room temperature. The following day, the bacteria were sonicated and the ELPs were purified as described previously.22 This purification process uses the ELPs’ phase transition, which is sequentially induced through at least 3 rounds of hot and cold centrifugation steps as necessary to achieve the desired purity.


### Spike-A192 refolding
  To reduce the particle size of the formulation, spike-A192 was first refolded. Spike-A192 was denatured and resolubilized in a 1:1 mixture of an equilibration solution (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM sodium chloride (NaCl), 8 M urea, 500 mM L-arginine, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (dPBS). The mixture (6 mL) was transferred to a 10 kDa MWCO dialysis tubing (#68100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and subjected to stepwise dialysis (1000 mL) at 4 °C. The following buffers were prepared and changed at 24-hr intervals: Buffer 1 (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 3 M urea, 500 mM L-arginine, 2 mM L-glutathione reduced (GSH), 0.4 mM L-glutathione oxidized (GSSG)); Buffer 2 (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 1 M urea, 500 mM L-arginine, 2 mM GSH, 0.4 mM GSSG); Buffer 3 (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 M urea, 250 mM L-arginine); Buffer 4 (20 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM L-arginine, pH 8.0), Buffer 5 (1x dPBS).20


### ELP concentration measurements
  To measure the concentration of the ELPs, the proteins were diluted in 6 M guanidine hydrochloride (1:6) to prevent nanoparticle assembly. The following equation was used to calculate protein concentration:  (1) CELP=A280-A350εl

  In equation 1, A280 is the absorbance at 280 nm, A350 is the absorbance at 350 nm, ε is the molar extinction coefficient, and l is the path length (cm). The NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), which has an l of 0.1 cm, was used to measure the A280 and A350. To calculate ε, the following equation was used24:  (2) ε=125nCysteine+5685nTryptophan+1285nTyrosine

  Using equation 2, the ε of spike RBD was estimated to be 31,770 M−1 cm−1. The ε of A192 was estimated to be 1,285 M−1 cm−1 because it contains a single tyrosine. Consequently, the ε of spike-A192 was calculated to be 33,055 M−1 cm−1.


### Conjugation with NHS-Rhodamine
  A192 and spike-A192 were labeled with NHS-Rhodamine. The labeling protocol was carried out for each protein to achieve an approximately 40% to 80% labeling efficiency. 1 mL of protein was incubated in a 1X molar excess NHS-rhodamine at 4 °C for 1 hr under constant rotation. The mixture was transferred to a 10 kDa MWCO dialysis tubing and unbound, free dye was removed by 24-hr dialysis. The absorbance of rhodamine-A192 (Rho-A192) and rhodamine-spike-A192 (Rho-spike-A192) at 555 nm (A555) was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), which has an l of 0.1 cm. The concentration of rhodamine, CRho, was calculated using the equation below, where the ε of rhodamine is 80,000 (M−1 cm−1) at 555 nm.

  The labeling efficiency was estimated based on the concentration of ELP measured before adding rhodamine:  (4) L%=CRhoCELP100%


### ELP purity
  The purity of the ELPs was analyzed using SDS-PAGE and matrix assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). 10 μg of the ELPs was denatured in 5 μL of β-mercaptoethanol, 95 °C for 5 mins, and loaded onto a 4–20% precast SDS-PAGE gel. The gel was stained with Bio-Safe Coomassie G-250 Stain (#1610786, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Touch Image System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Each lane was then plotted and the area under each peak (A) was calculated using ImageJ v2.0.0 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). The purity of the protein, P%, expressed as a percentage, was obtained from the ratio of the area under the peak of the protein of interest Apeak to the area under all the peaks present in the lane Atotal (Supporting Fig. S2):  (5) P%=ApeakAtotal100%

  To run MALDI-TOF MS (Supporting Fig. S3), 2.5 μL of ELP (~2 mg/mL) was mixed with 2.5 μL of β-mercaptoethanol and diluted in 5 μL of ultrapure water. The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 10 mins, cooled down to room temperature, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm. The protein was then diluted (1:10) in a mixture containing 2,6 dihydroxyacetophenone (DHAP) solution (10 mg/mL in 50% acetonitrile:0.1% formic acid). 0.8 μL of this solution was spotted on a 384-Big Anchor MALDI target and air-dried at room temperature. Crystallized samples were then analyzed using Bruker Rapiflex MALDI-TOF MS (Billerica, MA) equipped with a Smartbeam 3D, 10 kHz, 355 nm Nd:YAG laser. The laser parameters were optimized as follows: scan range = 26 μm; number of shots per sample = 1000; laser frequency = 5000 Hz. The mass spectrometer was calibrated for high-mass range using protein A and trypsinogen standards under the linear mode. The data were analyzed using flexAnalysis software (Bruker, Billerica, MA) and plotted using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA).


### Transition temperature analysis
  To explore the temperature-dependent phase behavior of the ELPs, the inverse phase transition temperature (Tt) was measured using the Beckman Coulter DU 800 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Brea, CA). Four different concentrations of A192 and spike-A192 were prepared using 5-fold serial dilutions (50 μM, 10 μM, 2 μM and 0.4 μM). The samples were heated at a rate of 1 °C/min, starting from 15 °C to 85 °C during which the optical density at 350 nm (OD350) was measured at every 0.3 °C. At 350 nm, neither fusion proteins nor plain ELPs contribute significant absorption. The Tt was determined as the maximum first derivative of the OD350 with respect to temperature.


### Measurements of hydrodynamic radius and colloidal stability
  Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to measure the hydrodynamic radius (Rh) and colloidal stability of spike-A192. 25 μM of spike-A192 was prepared in dPBS and filtered with a sterile Acrodisc® 0.2 μm filter (#4602, Pall, Port Washington, NY). 60 μL of spike-A192 was added to each well of a 384-well plate in triplicates. 15 μL of mineral oil was added to each well to prevent evaporation of the sample. A192 was also prepared in the same manner and its Rh was measured to determine the effect of the spike RBD on Rh. The hydrodynamic radius was measured at 37 °C with a DynaPro Plate Reader II (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA). To measure the stability of the spike-A192, the Rh measurements were taken every 24 hr until 72 hr. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was used to confirm the size and measure the average zeta potential (ζavg) of spike-A192 nanoparticles prior to (‘unfolded’) and following the refolding process (‘refolded’). The ZetaView® instrument (Particle Metrix GmbH, Ammersee, Germany) was first calibrated with a Zeta Potential Control (#70325C, Applied Microspheres, Leusden, the Netherlands). Next, the samples were diluted (1:1000) in a solution of dPBS:water (1:100) and injected into the ZetaView® cells. Measurements were taken at 23 °C (sensitivity: 54; shutter speed: 65). The data were analyzed using the ZetaView 8.05.05 software (Particle Metrix GmbH, Ammersee, Germany) and plotted using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA).


### Measurement of absolute molar mass and weight of spike-A192 nanoparticles
  The absolute molar mass of spike-A192 was measured via size exclusion chromatography-multiangle light scattering (SEC-MALS). Spike-A192 was diluted to 10 μM dPBS and was passed through a sterile Acrodisc® 0.2 μm filter. A Shodex protein KW-803 (8.0 mm ID × 300 mm) (Showa Denko America, New York, NY) column was equilibrated with PBS before introducing a bovine serum albumin (BSA) control at 5 mg/mL and spike-A192. The control and samples were observed on three detectors for each separated fraction: a SYC-LC1200 UV detector (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) at 280 nm, a DAWN HELEOS MALS detector (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA) and an Optilab rEX differential refractometer (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA). Together, these data were used to determine the absolute molar mass and radius of gyration (Rg) on ASTRA 6.1 software (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA). The ratio between Rg and Rh was used to infer shape information for spike-A192.25


### Negative-staining transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
  To visualize the morphology and size of the spike-A192 nanoparticles, negative-staining TEM was performed. 6 μL of spike-A192 (0.2 μM) was applied to a carbon-coated 400-mesh Formvar grid (#01754-F, Ted Pella, Inc., Redding, CA). The samples were wicked off after 3 mins using filter paper, stained with 4% uranyl acetate for 2 mins and then air-dried for 10 mins. Images were taken at 15,000X magnification and processed using a JEOL JEM-2100 LaB6 transmission electron microscope (JEOL USA, Inc., Peabody, MA). The spike-A192 nanoparticles’ length (l) and diameter (d) were measured using ImageJ v2.0.0 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Aspect ratio was calculated as the ratio of l to d.


### Measurement of spike-A192 binding affinity for hACE2
  Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was used to evaluate the binding affinity of A192 and spike-A192 for hACE2. Recombinant hACE2 (10 μg/mL) was immobilized on the surface of a Series S Sensor Chip CM5 (#BR100530, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) via amine coupling using 1-ethyl-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to a final density of 1,000 response units (RU) at pH 4. Residual sites on the dextran were blocked with 1 M ethanolamine hydrochloride; therefore, a control flow cell blocked with ethanolamine was used for reference subtraction. SPR was performed in a running buffer (0.1 M 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 1.5 M NaCl, 30 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% Tween-20, pH 7.4). Dilutions of each ELP were dissolved in running buffer and injected over the chip at 30 μL/min for 2 min. The ELPs were then dissociated from the chip for 5 min with running buffer. The remaining ELPs were removed from the chip by 100 mM hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 30 μL/min for 30 s. Sensorgrams were obtained and analyzed using the Biacore T200 Evaluation (Biacore, Uppsala, Sweden). The SPR RU for each ELP concentration was plotted against time. The association rate (ka), dissociation rate (kd), and equilibrium dissociation (KD) constants were estimated using the steady-state affinity model.


### SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD-hACE2 protein binding assay
  The spike RBD-hACE2 binding assay kit is an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) that uses a 96-well plate coated with recombinant hACE2 and was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Stock solutions of control A192 or spike-A192 were diluted in PBS to obtain the following concentrations: 2, 14.3, 22.8, 28.5, 57, and 114 μM. Briefly, 49 μL of each ELP dilution was incubated with 1 μL of the recombinant SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD-Fc and 50 μL of the assay diluent buffer (1x) for 2.5 hr at room temperature with gentle shaking. A negative control consisting of only assay diluent (1x) for background subtraction and a ‘0 μM positive control’ consisting of 1 μL recombinant SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD-Fc and 99 μL of assay diluent buffer (1x) were also included on each plate. The solutions were then discarded, and the plate was washed four times with 200 μL of wash buffer (1x). 100 μL of HRP-conjugated anti-Fc tag IgG was added to each well and the plate was incubated for 1 hr at room temperature with gentle shaking. Following the same washing step, the wells were treated with 100 μL of 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) one-step substrate and the plate was incubated for 30 mins in the dark with gentle shaking. After addition of 50 μL of stop solution, the absorbance at 450 nm (A450,sample) was obtained using a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT) and subtracted from the background absorbance (A450,background). Consequently, A450,sample-A450,background corresponds to the extent of the interaction between recombinant spike RBD and the hACE2-coated wells. To verify that the HRP-conjugated anti-Fc tag IgG binds exclusively to the recombinant spike-RBD-Fc, the ELISA was performed in which the hACE2-coated plate was treated with the ELP at the same tested concentrations (1 μM to 55 μM) in the absence of the recombinant spike-RBD-Fc. The percent inhibition of spike RBD-hACE2 interaction I% was calculated using the following equation:  (6) I%=A450,positive-A450,sampleA450,positive100%

  In equation 5, A450,positive and A450,sample are the absorbance at 450 nm of the ‘0 μM positive control’ and the wells treated with both the ELP and recombinant spike-RBD-Fc, respectively. The data was fitted through non-linear regression using GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA) and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for the inhibitory activity was estimated. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

After protein separation through gel electrophoresis, the samples were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using an iBlot2 NC stack (#IB23001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and iBlot2 Dry Blotting System.   Immunocytochemistry was performed on Calu-3 cells. 1,000,000 cells Calu-3 cells were cultured to 80% confluency on Poly-D-Lysine-coated 35 mm glass coverslip-bottomed dishes (#P35GC-0-10-C, MatTek Corporation, Ashland, MA) with Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). ### Cellular internalization of spike-A192 into Calu-3 lung epithelial cells
  To determine whether spike-A192 is specifically internalized into lysosomes of hACE2-positive lung epithelial cells, 1,000,000 Calu-3 cells were cultured to 80% confluency on Poly-D-Lysine-coated 35 mm glass coverslip-bottomed dishes with EMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After washing with warm fresh medium, the cells were cultured in medium containing 15 μM of Rho-A192 or Rho-spike-A192 and 75 nM LysoTracker™ Green DND-26 (#L7526, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). After 1 hr incubation at 37 °C, cells were rinsed with warm fresh medium to remove the free Rho-ELPs and were stained with the NucBlue™ Live ReadyProbes™ Reagent (#R37605, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). After 15 mins, the cells were incubated in a chamber mounted on a Zeiss LSM 880 with Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope system (Oberkochen, Germany) and imaged at 37 °C in LSMFast mode with an oil immersion 63X objective lens. Without oversaturating the images, the maximum white image brightness was linearly adjusted on all images to the same level (10,000 for rhodamine channel; 15,000 for GFP channel; 20,000 for DAPI channel) to enable their visualization. After image acquisition, images were analyzed with Fiji using the Coloc2 plugin.31 Briefly, images were split into individual channels for Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (PCC) analysis between Lysotracker Green and A192 or spike-A192 channels. The PCC above background threshold were quantified for images in each condition (A192 vs. Lysotracker Green and spike-A192 vs. Lysotracker Green) and a Mann-Whitney test was used to assess statistical significance.


## RESULTS

### Spike-A192 purification
  The ELPs, A192 and spike-A192 (Table 1), were genetically engineered, expressed in bacteria, and purified using ELP-mediated phase separation.15 Since spike-A192 was produced in the ClearColi® BL21 expression system, these proteins are expected to carry negligible levels of intact E. coli lipopolysaccharide, thus reducing its possible association with the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which has been observed using other expression systems.23, 32 A192 and spike-A192 were purified at yields of 68 and 79 mg/L culture, respectively. As shown by DLS, protein refolding was used to reduce the Rh of spike-A192 by 2-fold from 158.3 nm ± 33.7 (± PD) to 71.4 nm ± 8.0 (± PD) (Supporting Fig. S4A). This was corroborated independently using NTA, which revealed a reduction in Rh from 136.7 nm ± 83.0 (± SD) to 75.8 nm ± 30.5 (± SD) upon refolding (Supporting Fig. S4B). Thus, refolding of proteins with an attached, high M.W. ELP reduces the average particle size and distribution. Furthermore, the ζavg of the unfolded nanoparticles was estimated to be −2.75 mV ± 1.16 (± SD), which is near neutral and suggests that steric hindrance, rather than electrostatic repulsion, underlies their ability to form stable colloids (Supporting Fig. S4C). In contrast, the protein refolding process shifted the ζavg of spike-A192 to −19.14 ± 0.38 mV (± SD), indicating the potential for electrostatic stabilization during refolding of the spike protein. Nevertheless, due to the charge neutrality of A192 and its relatively high molecular weight, steric repulsion is a major factor mediating colloidal stability. Based on their lower hydrodynamic radius, all remaining data in this manuscript was obtained by studying nanoparticles that have been through protein refolding. After three separate rounds of protein expression and refolding, the soluble concentrations of spike-A192 nanoparticles in solution were measured as 108, 96, and 114 μM, while higher concentrations appeared unstable. The purity and molecular weight of the ELPs were analyzed using SDS-PAGE and MALDI-TOF MS (Fig. 1B, Table 1, Supporting Figs. S2, S3). A major band around 75 kDa corresponds to A192, while spike-A192 exhibits a major band around 100 kDa. These observations were consistent with the predicted molecular weights and those determined from MALDI-TOF MS. After achieving a labeling efficiency of 0.55 and 0.48 for A192 and spike-A192, respectively, a fluorescent image of an SDS-PAGE gel with NHS-rhodamine labeled A192 (Rho-A192) and spike-A192 (Rho-spike-A192) indicates efficient labeling of the proteins (Fig. 1B). Based on area under the curve for the major fluorescent band, the purity of A192 and spike-A192 was at least ~99.5% and ~80.3%, respectively (Supporting Fig. S2).


### Spike-A192 nanoparticle characterization
  To characterize the ELP behavior of spike-A192, the Tt was determined using UV-Vis spectrophotometry in which OD350 was measured over a range of temperatures and ELP concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2A, spike-A192 phase separates above 44 °C, while A192 phase separates above 58 °C (50 μM in dPBS). This shows that the attachment of the spike RBD lowers Tt with respect to free A192. For both peptides, the Tt is sensitive to the ELP concentration; however, the extent of Tt sensitivity to ELP concentration is less in spike-A192 than in A192.

  To verify the size, morphology, and formation of refolded nanoparticles, DLS, SEC-MALS and TEM were performed. DLS analysis showed that the Rh of spike-A192 was 71.4 nm ± 8.0 (± PD), representative of large nanoparticles (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the Rh of A192 was 8.0 nm, consistent with the molecular weight of a free polymer, as reported previously.22 The Rh of spike-A192 was also monitored over 72 hrs and did not change significantly, suggesting colloidal stability in solution at 37 °C (Fig. 2C). SEC-MALS was then used to measure the absolute molar mass and Rg of the spike-A192 nanoparticles. The average absolute molecular mass of spike-A192 was 5.4 × 107 Da, which suggests that approximately 550 spike-A192 monomers oligomerize to form a nanoparticle (Fig. 2D). The Rg/Rh ratio was calculated to be 1.2 (Table 1), which is theoretically consistent with an elongated nanostructure.25 This shape factor (Rg/Rh) was confirmed by TEM (Fig. 2E). The average length of these nanoparticles was 115.3 ± 55.1 (± SD) nm and width was 56.5 ± 0.9 (± SD) nm; therefore, the average aspect ratio of the nanoparticles was estimated to be 1.8 ± 0.9 (± SD), which is consistent with a ‘rod-like’ assembly of spherical particles.35


### Binding kinetics between spike-A192 and hACE2
  Since the binding between the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD and the hACE2 is the first crucial step for viral entry into host cells, SPR was performed to quantify the affinity of A192 and spike-A192 for the recombinant hACE2 in vitro.9 Using SPR, the binding properties of the ELPs and hACE2 were analyzed without the need for radioactive or fluorescent labeling. At tested concentrations, the control A192 did not exhibit detectable binding to hACE2 (Fig. 2F) whereas spike-A192 achieved clear concentration-dependent binding (Fig. 2G). The association rate (ka), dissociation rate (kd), and equilibrium dissociation (KD) constants derived from the sensorgram between spike-A192 and hACE2 were estimated from Fig. 2G. Spike-A192 has a nanomolar binding affinity for hACE2 at 25 °C, with a KD of 77.1 nM. Despite assembling a nanostructure, these findings confirm that spike-A192 nanoparticles exhibit specific affinity for the hACE2.


### Inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD-hACE2 binding
  After confirming the high affinity of spike-A192 for hACE2, ELISA was performed to determine if these nanoparticles inhibit the interaction between a recombinant spike RBD fused with the Fc domain of antibody (spike-RBD-Fc) and hACE2. Treatment with spike-A192 at concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 55 μM did inhibit the spike-RBD-hACE2 interaction and this inhibition is concentration-dependent (Fig. 3). A sigmoidal-shaped dose-response curve was generated, and non-linear regression analysis was used to estimate an IC50 of 13.1 μM. In contrast, treatment with A192 (1 μM to 27.5 μM) did not inhibit the spike RBD-hACE2 interaction (Supporting Fig. S5). While A192 at 55 μM yielded a 27.3% inhibition, the magnitude of inhibition was 3-fold less compared to that of spike-A192 at 55 μM, suggesting that A192 alone did not contribute significantly to the spike-RBD-hACE2 interaction. To verify that the HRP-conjugated anti-Fc tag antibody binds exclusively to the recombinant spike-RBD-Fc, the ELISA was performed where hACE2-coated plates were treated with spike-A192 at the same concentrations in the absence of the spike-RBD-Fc (- spike-RBD-Fc). Indeed, the background-subtracted absorbance readings at 450 nm A450,sample-A450,background obtained from all spike-A192 treatments was nearly identical to that of A450,background (Fig. 3A). Despite assembling a multivalent nanostructure, these inhibition studies prove that spike-A192 specifically inhibits the interaction between recombinant spike proteins and their major receptor target, hACE2; furthermore, the spike RBD component of spike-A192 is necessary for this inhibition.


### Cellular internalization of spike-A192
  To test whether spike-A192 can be efficiently trafficked into hACE2-positive lung epithelial cells, confocal microscopy imaging was performed on a human lung epithelial Calu-3 cell line. Prior to this, it was necessary to first confirm hACE2 expression in Calu-3 cells using two methods: western blot and immunocytochemistry. Consistent with previous literature, western blot analysis indicates that Calu-3 cells express glycosylated hACE2 (gly-hACE2), unlike HeLa cells and A549 lung epithelial cells (Fig. 4A).26–30 Confirming the results from the western blot analysis, confocal images show that Calu-3 cells express high levels of hACE2 (Fig. 4B).

  Having confirmed expression of hACE2, live-cell confocal microscopy was used to evaluate the endocytic fate of the Rho-spike-A192 nanoparticles in Calu-3 cells compared with uptake of control Rho-A192. To detect endocytosis of the ELPs, cells were co-incubated with LysoTracker Green, which is a fluorescent marker for low pH lysosomes. After a 1 hr incubation at 37 °C, there was detectable cellular internalization for Rho-spike-A192 (Fig. 5A), which appeared perinuclear. On the other hand, Rho-A192 exhibited lower intensities of cellular uptake, suggesting that the spike RBD enhances internalization into hACE2-postive lung epithelial cells. Moreover, there was notable colocalization of the red fluorescence of Rho-spike-A192 and the green fluorescence of the lysosomal compartments. To quantify the degree of colocalization between the Rho-ELPs and LysoTracker Green, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated. The mean PCC of cells treated with Rho-A192 and Rho-spike-A192 was 0.51 and 0.71 (± SD, p=0.018), respectively (Fig. 5B). While both ELPs are trafficked into lysosomes, spike-A192 is transported into these compartments to a significantly greater extent. These findings suggest that the spike-A192 is trafficked into lysosomes following endocytosis and that the spike RBD promotes this trafficking, indicative of the possible fate of these nanoparticles in tissues that express hACE2.


## DISCUSSION
  There remains much to understand about SARS-CoV-2, but it has been well-established that its spike protein is critical for facilitating its viral entry into host cells after binding to hACE2. Interfering with SARS-CoV-2 internalization mechanism could be a potent therapeutic strategy. In fact, nanoparticles have been developed to disrupt other types of viral binding to host cell receptors. For example, Chang et al. synthesized metal nanoparticle composite called TPNT1 that contains gold, silver, and zinc oxide.36 TPNT1 was shown to inhibit viral entry by disrupting the binding of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to the hACE2 in vitro and syncytium formation. Polymeric nanoparticles have also been developed to inhibit SARS-CoV entry. As another example, Milewska et al. reported that cationic chitosan, a polysaccharide-derived nanoparticle, effectively blocked the binding of the HCoV-NL63 spike protein with hACE2.37 In another study, decoy nanoparticles expressing both the hACE2 and cytokine receptors were developed to neutralize SARS-CoV-2 and inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF).38 These nanodecoys were also shown to effectively protect host cells from SARS-CoV-2 infection, while suppressing immune disorder in an acute pneumonia mouse model.

  Biomimetic SARS-CoV-2-like nanoparticles that incorporate unique structural features of the virus while being noninfectious have also been engineered for several applications such as vaccination and COVID-19 diagnostic tools. Chen et al. developed SARS-CoV-2 nanovaccines by fusing genetically engineered cell membrane expressing RBD with biodegradable mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) that are encapsulated with cytosine-phosphate-guanine oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG) (MSN-CpG@CM).39 Exhibiting spike protein-like morphology and size, MSN-CpG@CM facilitated robust neutralizing antibody responses against SARS-CoV-2 and T cell immunity in immunized mice. Another study reported the production of biomimetic virus-like particles (VLP) encapsulated with SARS-CoV-2 packaged ribonucleic acid (RNA) modules.40 The VLP-based positive controls for RT-PCR detection of SARS-CoV-2 were able to mimic the conditions encountered by the RNA template of SARS-CoV-2 within samples by achieving probe stability over a week and a range of temperatures.

  While a plethora of studies corroborate the promising applications of antiviral nanotechnology towards treating COVID-19, those formulations do not exhibit the therapeutic adaptability of ELP-based nanotechnology. ELP sequences can be attached to other proteins, either on the N-terminus or C-terminus, through simple genetic manipulation, forming a variety of fusion ELP-based nanoparticles beneficial for several applications.41 Furthermore, ELPs’ versatility as a purification tag enables efficient purification, while their biodegradability and biocompatibility minimize adverse effects.42, 43 At the time of this publication, ELP-based biomimetic SARS-CoV-2 nanoparticles have not yet been reported. However, several ELP platforms have been developed that self-assemble into virus-like nanoparticles for different drug delivery and vaccination applications. Van Eldijk et al. engineered a fusion ELP consisting of the capsid protein (CP) of cowpea chlorotic mottle virus (CCMV) and a short-block ELP.44 These CP-ELPs formed virus-like particles through two mechanisms, pH-induced and ELP-induced assembly, leading to distinct well-defined structures. Building on this CCMV variant, Vervoort et al. designed an ELP-CCMV in which the CCMV capsid displays multiple functional entities while being stabilized by the ELP.45 The ELP-CCMV particles were functionalized with a cell-penetrating HIV trans-acting transcriptional activator (TAT) peptide and a fluorophore. These assembled into virus-like particles with structures comparable to the CP-ELPs and were internalized into HeLa cells. As a vaccination option against the porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2), an ELPylated virus-like particle (ELP-VLP) was developed by Li and colleagues.46 The ELP-VLP assembled into particles with a morphology almost identical to that of His-tagged Cap proteins; furthermore, they induced stronger immunogenicity when challenged with PCV2 in mice.

  Considering the evidence demonstrating biomimetic virus-like nanoparticles’ potential applications for treating COVID-19 and the versatility of virus-like ELP-based particles, we developed a fusion ELP (spike-A192) that self-assembles into nanoparticles decorated with the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. The approach of fusing a spike protein RBD to an ELP would be an adaptable platform when developing more effective variants of 2nd-generation spike-ELPs against emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOC). With the wealth of information on the genetic mechanisms underlying VOCs’ higher transmissibility, the spike-ELP technology can be genetically modified to include the same spike protein mutations as a VOC to inhibit entry more effectively into lung cells.

  Similar to spike-A192, several studies by our group have shown a variety of fusion A192 ELPs form stable, random coils from the oligomerization of fusion A192 monomers.20, 22, 47, 48 Moreover, attachment of the fusion domain (e.g. antibody fragment or cytokine) to the soluble A192 led to a lower Tt compared to unconjugated A192. In addition to the decrease in magnitude of Tt, spike-A192’s Tt was less dependent on ELP concentration than A192, a behavior that was observed by previously reported fusion A192 proteins.22, 48, 49 We hypothesize that the spike RBD forms the nanoparticle core that is enveloped by the ELP corona because of the hydrophilicity of A192 at temperatures below the Tt. As with hGMCSF-A192 and scFv-A192, which were previously reported by our group, the high molecular weight of A192 likely provides steric stabilization for the spike-A192 nanoparticle.47, 49 In addition to stabilizing particles for days (Fig. 1B), A192 results in a particle size that differs slightly between nanoparticles purified directly from bacterial lysates and those subjected to protein refolding (Supporting Fig. S4). Moreover, as measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) unfolded nanoparticles have nearly neutral surface zeta potential. As they lack strong surface charge, the colloidal stabilization of these protein-based nanoparticles most likely depends on steric repulsion between a layer of soluble, charge-neutral A192. Another possible factor underlying the nanoparticle assembly is the ability of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to multimerize into trimers.12 The formation spike-A192 nanoparticles was observed using DLS, MALS, NTA, and TEM, the latter method revealing a rod-like morphology (average aspect ratio ~1.8). This flexible shape suggests that the spike RBD remains accessible for biological activity through the loosely-packed A192 corona.

  It should be noted that the DLS and TEM data showed a relatively heterogenous population of nanostructures (PD = 8.0 by DLS). However, structural polydispersity may not significantly hinder the bioactivity of the nanoparticles. For example, our previous work engineered scFv-ELPs that formed multivalent antibody-based, worm-like nanostructures with a PD of 6.5–18.1.20 Despite the heterogeneity, the ‘nanoworms’ still effectively clustered the target cell-surface receptors of non-Hodgkin lymphoma cells and induced apoptosis. Notwithstanding, future studies can be performed to reduce the structural polydispersity and determine if such reduction enhances the bioactivity of spike-A192.

  While the elongated structure was not the result of a deliberate design, it may be still be possible to engineer other morphologies such as spheres or micelles without compromising the binding affinity or cellular uptake of ELP-based nanoparticles. In fact, multivalent micelle-like nanoparticles consisting of an anti-programmed death-1 (PD-1) antibody and an immune-tolerant ELP (αPD-1-iTEP) displayed stronger avidity for PD-1-positive cells and inhibition of programmed death-ligand-1 (PD-L1) binding to those cells compared to an αPD-1 antibody.50 On the other hand, Assal et al. developed fusion spherical ELP-D-helix B nanostructures that were tethered with single-chain vascular epidermal growth factor and were effectively internalized into HeLa cells to induce apoptotic cell death.51

  Indeed, spike-A192 achieved a nanomolar binding affinity for hACE2 (KD = 77.1 nM) and was also shown to effectively inhibit the binding of the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD to hACE2 in a concentration-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 13.1 μM. Both the binding affinity for hACE2 and the spike RBD-hACE2 interaction inhibition required the presence of the spike RBD on the nanoparticles. Moreover, the relatively high IC50 value may be the result of the stronger affinity of SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD for hACE2, compared to spike-A192’s affinity for hACE2. From SPR and biolayer interferometry (BLI) approaches, previous studies have estimated the in vitro KD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein with respect to the hACE2 N-terminal peptidase domain to be 4.7 nM-133.3 nM.52 Notwithstanding, this further demonstrates that the spike RBD remains biologically accessible on the rod-like nanoparticles, which may have applications towards disrupting SARS-CoV-2 binding to host cells.

  The cellular internalization experiments suggested that spike-A192 is endocytosed into hACE2-positive Calu-3 lung epithelial cell at a higher efficiency compared to A192. This suggests that the spike RBD is necessary for endocytosis and lysosomal compartmentalization into the target hACE2-expressing lung epithelial cells. Following endocytosis, the fusion nanoparticles are trafficked into the lysosomal compartment. This endocytic behavior mimics SARS-CoV-2’s in the absence of TMPRSS2; therefore, the spike-A192 may serve as a model for studying SARS-CoV-2 endocytosis. Like many viruses, β-coronaviruses promote infectivity by trafficking to, deacidifying of, and deactivating enzymes in lysosomes.53 This phenomenon opens a potential therapeutic strategy for targeting the lysosomal compartment of infected cells. Thus, spike-A192’s endocytic fate could be manipulated to interfere with SARS-CoV-2’s ability to evade lysosomal degradation and infect other host cells. To further confirm that spike-A192’s endocytic behavior mimics that of the virus, a future study should assess the internalization of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and the replication-restricted SARS-CoV-2 spike pseudotyped lentiviral viruses into hACE2-expressing lung epithelial cells.


## CONCLUSION
  This work demonstrates that the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein can be fused to an ELP to generate stable, biomimetic nanoparticles decorated with the RBD (spike-A192). Similar to the intact virus, these spike-A192 nanoparticles have nanomolar affinity for hACE2 and inhibit the binding interaction between SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD and hACE2. In hACE2-positive lung epithelial cells, these nanoparticles are endocytosed at a high efficiency and trafficked into lysosomes. Thus, potential applications for these fusion proteins are broad, ranging from serving as a model for investigating SARS-CoV-2 endocytosis to a therapeutic option for blocking SARS-CoV-2 entry into host cells.
