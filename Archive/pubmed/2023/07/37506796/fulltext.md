# Intrinsic disorder may drive the interaction of PROS1 and MERTK in uveal melanoma


## Background:
  Class 2 uveal melanomas are associated with the inactivation of the BRCA1 (breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein)-associated protein 1 (BAP1)) gene. Inactivation of BAP1 promotes the upregulation of vitamin K-dependent protein S (PROS1), which interacts with the tyrosine-protein kinase Mer (MERTK) receptor on M2 macrophages to induce an immunosuppressive environment.


## Methods:
  We simulated the interaction of PROS1 with MERTK with ColabFold. We evaluated PROS1 and MERTK for the presence of intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDPRs) and disorder-to-order (DOT) regions to understand their protein-protein interaction (PPI). We first evaluated the structure of each protein with AlphaFold. We then analyzed specific sequence-based features of the PROS1 and MERTK with a suite of bioinformatics tools.


## Results:
  With high-resolution, moderate confidence, we successfully modeled the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK (predicted local distance difference test score (pDLLT) = 70.68). Our structural analysis qualitatively demonstrated IDPRs (i.e., spaghetti-like entities) in PROS1 and MERK. PROS1 was 23.37% disordered, and MERTK was 23.09% disordered, classifying them as moderately disordered and flexible proteins. PROS1 was significantly enriched in cysteine, the most order-promoting residue (p-value < 0.05). Our IUPred analysis demonstrated that there are two disorder-to-order transition (DOT) regions in PROS1. MERTK was significantly enriched in proline, the most disorder-promoting residue (p-value < 0.05), but did not contain DOT regions. Our STRING analysis demonstrated that the PPI network between PROS1 and MERTK is more complex than their assumed one-to-one binding (p-value < 2.0×10−6).


## Conclusion:
  Our findings present a novel prediction for the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK. Our findings show that PROS1 and MERTK contain elements of intrinsic disorder. PROS1 has two DOT regions that are attractive immunotherapy targets. We recommend that IDPRs and DOT regions found in PROS1 and MERTK should be considered when developing immunotherapies targeting this PPI.


## Introduction
  Uveal melanoma (UM) is an ocular malignancy that arises from melanocytes in the choroid, ciliary body, or iris, ultimately accounting for approximately 5% of all melanomas 1,2. Although UM shares the melanoma name, it presents and behaves very differently compared to cutaneous melanoma in its biology, metastatic pattern, and treatment 3. Although there have been advances in the treatment of uveal melanoma, nearly 50% of patients develop metastases within ten years of diagnosis, most commonly to the liver (89%) 4–6.

  Gene expression profiling (GEP) has proved to be a useful prognostic tool for predicting metastatic risk of UM 7,8. GEP categorizes UM into two classes: Class 1, which confers a low metastatic risk, and Class 2, which confers a high metastatic risk 7–9. Class 2 UM is commonly associated with BRCA1 (breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein)-associated protein 1 (BAP1) biallelic inactivation, the strongest genetic risk factor for metastasis and associated with poor prognosis 7,8,10.

  BAP1 loss leads to the upregulation of several genes associated with suppressive immune responses 11. A recent study showed that the loss of BAP1 is associated with a high upregulation of vitamin K-dependent protein S (PROS1) in class 2/BAP1-mutant UMs 10. PROS1 then phosphorylates the Mer Tyrosine Kinase (MERTK) receptor on M2 macrophages, promoting a tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) 10. The PROS1-MERTK interaction is a protein-protein interaction that is believed to dictate the TIME that promotes the development of UM. Therefore, the PROS1-MERTK protein interaction is an attractive target for immunotherapy.

  Our group has been interested in characterizing proteins through the lens of protein intrinsic disorder phenomenon and its association with ophthalmic diseases, focusing particularly on ocular malignancies12–14. While there are several factors to consider when studying the PROS1-MERTK in TIME associated with UM, our group sought to understand this interaction as it relates to the protein intrinsic disorder phenomenon, potentially providing a framework to consider when developing immunotherapies targeting the PROS1-MERTK protein-protein interaction.

  Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDPRs) are functional proteins or segments of proteins that are found in all known proteomes 15–18. These proteins are functional proteins or segments of proteins characterized by a lack of unique 3D structures and have been linked to the development of malignancies 15–22. In this study, we aim to evaluate how intrinsic disorder may drive the interaction of PROS1 and MERTK in uveal melanoma and potentially promote neoplastic development. If IDPRS or disorder-to-order transition (DOT) regions are present in either PROS1 or MERTK, it may provide insights into novel immune therapy targets directed at the protein-protein interaction.


## Methods
  Our study focused on the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK. Kaler et. al. previously noted that this interaction was likely involved in the progression of Class 2 uveal melanoma 10. We aimed to understand further the molecular interaction between these two proteins (Figure 1). We conducted a disorder-based bioinformatics analysis that utilized publicly available databases and computational tools to probe PROS1 and MERTK receptors for intrinsically disordered protein regions that might be related to the interaction between these proteins.


### Protein Sequences
  The Universal Protein Resource (UniProt; available at: https://uniprot.org; date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022) is a protein database that houses comprehensive information related to function, taxonomy, subcellular location, disease associations, post-translation modifications, expression profiles, interaction networks, structure, protein families and sequences 23. The search terms used were “gene: PROS1” and “gene:MERTK”. Each human variant was selected for PROS1 (UniProtID: P07225) and MERTK (UniProtID: Q12866). The default (canonical) amino acid sequence was selected for each protein.


### Structural Assessment
  The UniProt entries for PROS1 and MERTK also contain structural information. We selected the “AlphaFoldDB” entry as AlphaFold is the most up-to-date and accurate computational method to predict protein structure 24,25. For advanced and comprehensive protein structure prediction of the PROS1-MERTK complex, we utilized the University of California San Francisco’s (UCSF) ChimeraX-1.6.1 software, which is an extensible program for interactive visualization and analysis of molecular structures and related data26,27. The software allowed us to model our multimer PROS1-MERTK with ColabFold, a tool that integrates many-against-many searching (MMseqs2) rapid homology search with AlphaFold2’s protein structure prediction capabilities28,29. Leveraging Google Colaboratory’s platform, ColabFold served as a cost-effective and user-friendly resource for our protein folding analysis. To maintain transparency and encourage reproducibility in our approach, we opted for this open-source software.

  The computational hardware used in this analysis was an NVIDIA A100-SXM4–40GB graphics processor, which is a high-performance GPU designed for data centers and artificial intelligence applications. This GPU was particularly suited for handling the large size of the PROS1-MERTK protein complex (1,675 residues) and the computational demands of the prediction process.

  We used the “alphafold2_multimer_v3_model” which is a recent, state-of-the-art model in the AlphaFold series for the protein structure prediction process 29. To increase the accuracy of the prediction, especially considering the limitations associated with running this process in a colab environment, we set the number of recycles to 20. The rationale behind this setting is that the multimer model will continue recycling until the predictions stop changing, up to the limit set here. By setting this to 20, we aimed to achieve higher accuracy at the potential cost of longer inference times.


### Quantification of Intrinsic Disorder

#### a. Compositional Profiler
  We aimed to quantify the elements of intrinsic disorder in PROS1 and MERTK. We used the Compositional Profiler (available at: http://www.cprofiler.org/;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022) to measure the relative proportion of each amino acid in each protein 30. Each amino acid was compared to a background set of proteins (i.e., Protein Data Bank Select (PBD) 25 30) that are a standard set of proteins that are highly ordered (i.e., contains many elements of structure in forms of alpha helices or beta-pleated sheets). We evaluated the normalized enrichment or depletion of each amino acid as Cx − CorderCorder, where Cx is the content of a given residue in a query protein (i.e., PROS1 and MERTK) and Corder is the content of the same residue in the PDB Select 25.


#### b. Per-amino acid residue disorder prediction
  The intrinsic disorder of PROS1 and MERTK, as well as 17 other proteins from the protein-protein interaction network centered on PROS1, were then quantified on a per-amino acid residue basis using a set of commonly used per-residue disorder predictors, including PONDR® VLS2 31,32, PONDR® VL3 33, PONDR® VLXT 34, PONDR® FIT 35, as well as IUPred-Long and IUPred-Short 36. A web application called the Rapid Intrinsic Disorder Analysis Online (RIDAO) was used to gather results from each predictor in bulk 37. In these analyses, 0 is the most ordered measurement, and 1 is the most disordered measurement. We utilized the PONDR server also to generate an overall percent disorder of PROS1 and MERTK. Here, two arbitrary cutoffs for the levels of intrinsic disorder are used to classify proteins as highly ordered (PPDR<10%), moderately disordered (10%≤PPDR<30%), and highly disordered (PPDR≥30%) 38. We also used the average disorder score (ADS; the average of per-residue residue predictors used in calculating PPDR) to quantify the level of intrinsic disorder of a given protein score. Suppose the protein/region/residue has an ADS < 0.15, it is characterized as ordered. When the ADS is between 0.15 and 0.5, the protein/region/residue is characterized as moderately disordered or flexible, whereas ADS≥0.5 corresponds to the disordered protein/region/residue.


#### c. Assessment of disorder-related functionality
  Disorder-related functionality of PROS1 and MERTK was evaluated with the Database of Disordered Protein Prediction (D2P2) platform (http://d2p2.pro/) 39. The D2P2 platform integrates IUPred 40, PONDR® VLXT 34, PONDR® VSL2B 31,32, PrDOS 41, PV2 39, and ESpritz 42, which allows for a consolidated visualization of disorder prediction tools. The D2P2 server also provides the Structural Classification of Proteins (SCOP) 43,44 and SUPERFAMILY predictor for the sequences of interest 45. D2P2 also predicts disorder-based binding sites with the ANCHOR algorithm 46. These ANCHOR-identified regions represent disorder-based binding sites, which are disordered in the unbound form and expected to fold into ordered structures when interacting with specific partners (i.e., molecular recognition features (MoRFs). Lastly, the D2P2 platform also predicts possible posttranslational modifications (PTMs) using the outputs of the PhosphoSitePlus 47.


#### d. Identification of disorder-to-order transition (DOT) regions
  The IUPred3 platform was the final step of quantifying intrinsic disorder within PROS1 and MERTK (available at: https://iupred3.elte.hu/;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022) 36,48,49. This tool can assess where disordered redox-sensitive protein regions are in our proteins by determining the disorder score in their disordered state and ordered state. If the values vary significantly between the redox states, we can define a particular region as a redox-sensitive region (i.e., a disorder-to-order transition (DOT) region).


#### e. Multiple Sequence Alignment
  We performed a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) to evaluate the conservation of disordered regions in the proteins PROS1 and MERTK across multiple species. To find corresponding proteins in other species, we conducted a Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, which is a tool that finds regions of local similarity between sequences50–52. BLAST compares nucleotide or protein sequences to sequence databases and calculates the statistical significance of matches. We used the sequences of human PROS1 and MERTK as queries in the BLAST search and identified their counterparts in other species. We then collected the sequences of the identified proteins in FASTA format for further analysis.

  The collected sequences were aligned using MUltiple Sequence Comparison by Log-Expectation (MUSCLE), a tool for multiple sequence alignment53. The output of MUSCLE is a multiple sequence alignment that shows the positions of similarity and dissimilarity across the protein sequences from different species. This alignment was used as the basis for our conservation analysis. To assess the conservation of each position in the alignment, we calculated conservation scores using a Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM)54. The PSSM represents the frequency of each amino acid at each position in the alignment, providing a quantitative measure of conservation. To reduce noise and smooth the data, we calculated a rolling average conservation score with a window size of 25 amino acids. The conservation scores range from 0 to the number of sequences in the alignment. A score of 0 indicates that no sequences have the same amino acid at that position, while a higher score indicates that more sequences share the same amino acid at that position.


#### f. Binary Prediction of Protein Disorder
  The global disorder analysis of PROS1 and MERTK and their 17 protein partners (see Protein-Protein Interaction Network methods and results for predicted partners outside of one-to-one PROS1-MERTK binding) was conducted using the CH-CDF analysis data, which were generated using RIDAO. The classification of proteins via the CH plot, the CDF plot, and combined the CH-CDF plots are well-established methodologies 55–60. This analysis combines results from charge-hydropathy (CH) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) plots, which are both binary predictors of disorder (i.e., the prediction classifies the whole protein as completely disordered or ordered, rather than a per residue basis).


### Protein-Protein Interaction Network
  To further understand the protein-protein interaction between PROS1 and MERTK, the amino acid sequences were used as inputs for The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) (URL: https://string-db.org/;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022). STRING is a quality-controlled database that uses experimentally and computationally derived data to detail functional interactions between proteins 61. The settings for the minimum required interaction score were set to the highest confidence (0.900), and the maximum number of interactions was assigned the highest possible value of 500.


## Results

### Protein Sequence
  Our disorder-based bioinformatics protein analysis used the canonical amino acid sequence of the PROS1 (Figure S1) and MERTK (Figure S2) in FASTA format. PROS1 contains 676 amino acid residues and includes several functional domains, such as Vitamin K-dependent carboxylation/gamma-carboxy glutamic (GLA) domain (residues 42–87), which is a membrane binding motif which, in the presence of calcium ions, interacts with phospholipid membranes that include phosphatidylserine followed by the thrombin-sensitive region (residues 88–116) that is linked to a series of three EGF-like domains (residues 157–200, 201–242, and 243–283 followed by two laminin G0like domains (residues 299–475 and 484–666) (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/P07225/entry#family_and_domains;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022). On the other hand, MERTK is a 999-residue-long receptor tyrosine kinase that contains two Ig-like C2-type domains (residues 81–186 and 197–273), two fibronectin type-III domains (residues 286–381 and 386–484) and a protein kinase domain (residues 587–585) (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/Q12866/entry#family_and_domains;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022).


### Structural Assessment
  The 3D structure of PROS1 and MERTK modeled by AlphaFold (Figure 2) demonstrates that these proteins have elements of order (structure; α-helices and β-pleated sheets) and disorder (structureless, spaghetti-like regions). The structural elements of the protein are flanked by regions that lack definitive structure. The AlphaFold model confidence is high for a large proportion of the protein’s structure, as indicated by the extensive blue color throughout the model. The terminals of PROS1 appear to be less ordered, as indicated by low model confidence and yellow/orange coloring. MERTK’s AlphaFold model confidence is high, i.e., most of the structure of the protein is shown by blue color. There appear to be large regions of less model confidence (i.e., yellow/orange color), which reflects regions with a less established structure. These regions with less model confidence are the intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDPRs) of interest. We can qualitatively appreciate that there are segments in both proteins that contain little to no structure yet likely retain biological functions as expected for most IDPRs.

  To further analyze the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK, we utilized the University of California San Francisco’s (UCSF) ChimeraX-1.6.1 software to generate a predicted model of the PROS1-MERTK complex using the “alphafold2_multimer_v3_model”. The pLDDT score of our best model was 70.68, indicating a confidence level categorized as “Confident” according to the AlphaFold model confidence ranges. The model provides valuable insights into the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK.

  In our analysis of the PROS1-MERTK protein-protein interaction, we employed several visual and computational methods. Figure 3 illustrates the results of this comprehensive analysis. Figure 3A exhibits the interaction predicted by the local distance difference test (pLDDT). This method indicated a significant degree of interaction between PROS1 and MERTK. As presented in Figure 3B, the ribbon model allows a clear representation of the individual protein chains, with PROS1 highlighted in blue and MERTK in pink. This visualization further underscored the predicted interaction, providing a clearer physical understanding of the interaction sites. The surface model in Figure 3C offers a different perspective on the protein-protein interaction. Colored individually, the proteins reveal a remarkable interface that further corroborates the results from the pLDDT and ribbon model visualizations. Finally, Figure 3D provides a detailed view of the protein contacts at a close distance of 3 angstroms. The proteins are depicted in blue, and their contacts at this short distance are represented in red.


### Quantification of Intrinsic Disorder

#### a. Compositional Profiler
  After visualization of the IDPRs in both proteins in AlphaFold-modeled structures, we aimed to quantify these regions. We first constructed a normalized amino acid composition profile for PROS1 and MERTK (Figure 4). In the composition profile, each amino acid was arranged from the most order-promoting (i.e., cysteine (C), tryptophan (W), isoleucine (I), tyrosine (Y), phenylalanine (F), leucine (L), histidine (H), valine (V), asparagine (N), and methionine (M)) to the most disorder-promoting residues (i.e., arginine (R), threonine (T), aspartate (D), glycine (G), alanine (A), lysine (K), glutamine (Q), serine (S), glutamate (E), and proline (P)). If the amino acid is enriched in the protein, then it receives a positive value. If the amino acid is depleted in the protein, then it receives a negative value. PROS1 is enriched in 3 (C, L, and N) and depleted in 4 (W, F, H, and M) of 10 order-promoting residues. On the other hand, it is enriched in 3 of 10 disorder-promoting residues (K, S, and E). Curiously, PROS1 is most highly enriched in cysteine (C) (p-value < 0.05), the most order- and structure-promoting amino acid. In fact, this protein contains 36 cysteine residues (or 5.3%). This cysteine content of PROS1 is unusual, as the average content of cysteines in a typical UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot protein is 1.37% (https://web.expasy.org/protscale/pscale/A.A.Swiss-Prot.html;date last accessed: Nov 5, 2022). MERTK is depleted in two (Y, F, and N) and enriched in 6 of 10 order-promoting residues (W, I, L, H, V, and M) and is enriched in only 2 or 10 disorder-promoting residues (S and P). While MERTK has higher enrichment in order-promoting compared to the disorder-promoting residues, the protein is most enriched in serine (S) and proline (P) (p-value < 0.05), two of the most disordered-promoting regions.


#### b. Per-amino acid residue disorder prediction
  After we determined the amino acid composition of PROS1 and MERTK, we aimed to quantify the intrinsic disorder on a per-residue basis. We used PONDR® VLXT, PONDR® VL3, and PONDR® VSL2 to predict the disorder score of each amino acid in our two proteins (Figure 5). The PONDR suite allows for the categorization based on the established criteria for the classification of proteins based on their percent of the predicted disordered residues (PPDR scores), where proteins are considered highly ordered (PPDR<10%), moderately disordered (10%≤PPDR<30%), and highly disordered (PPDR≥30%) 38. PROS1 was predicted to be 12.13% disordered by PONDR® VLXT (i.e., moderately disordered), 26.48% disordered by PONDR® VL3 (i.e., moderately disordered), and 31.51% disordered by PONDR® VSL2 (i.e., highly disordered). The average disorder score (ADS) for PROS1 was 23.37%, classifying it as moderately disordered or flexible. MERTK was predicted to be 27.23% disordered by PONDR VLXT (i.e., moderately disordered), 16.72% disordered by PONDR VL3 (i.e., moderately disordered), and 25.33% disordered by PONDR VSL2 (i.e., moderately disordered). The ADS for MERTK was 23.37%, which classifies it as moderately disordered or flexible.


#### c. Assessment of disorder-related functionality
  To broaden our efforts to quantify the intrinsic disorder in PROS1 and MERTK, we used the D2P2 platform (Figure 6). The D2P2 platform allowed us to quantify the functional intrinsic disorder of each protein and assess its relationship with post-translational modifications (PTMs) and disorder-based binding potential. PROS1’s D2P2 output (Figure 6A) demonstrates a relative agreement between the nine disorder-based predictors, where there are scattered regions of disorder predicted throughout the protein. The region of PROS1 that demonstrates the most agreement among the nine per-residue disorder predictors is the Vitamin K-dependent carboxylation/gamma-carboxy glutamic (GLA) domain. PROS1 contains several conserved functional domains, five of which are GLA domain (residues 41–93), Growth factor receptor domain (residues 120–248), EGF/Laminin (residues 241–269), and two Concanavalin A-like lectins/glucanases (residues 266–455 and 468–669) were identified by the Superfamily 1.75 server and six domains (Vitamin K-dependent carboxylation/gamma-carboxyglutamic (GLA) domain (residues 46–87), EGF-like domain (residues 121–153), PF14670.1 domain (residues 161–199), Calcium-binding EGF domain (residues 201–241) and two Laminin G domains (residues 329–459 and 514–647)) identified by the Pfam server. There are no molecular recognition features (MoRFs) in PROS1 as predicated by the D2P2 platform. PROS1 has 16 predicted post-translational modifications (PTMs), 11 carboxylation sites, and 5 phosphorylation sites. 11 of the 16 PTMs are in the GLA domain, the most disordered domain. Figure 7A shows that there are context-dependent interaction sites (i.e., represents regions that can switch between different binding modes and interactions which depend on the cellular milieu, sub-cellular localization, partners, and posttranslational modifications) that are mostly located within or near disordered or flexible regions, further emphasizing the role of disorder in the functionality of this protein.

  MERTK’s D2P2 output (Figure 6B) demonstrates a relative agreement between the nine disorder-based predictors. The most agreement between predictors has localized to the terminals of the protein, as expected. MERTK contains three conserved functional domains identified by the Superfamily 1.75 platform (two immunoglobulin domains (residues 93–195 and 187–282), a Fibronectin type III domain (residues 284–480), and Protein kinase-like (PK-like) domain (residues 578–872) and four Pfams-identified domains (Immunoglobulin I-set domain (residues 94–187), immunoglobulin domain (residues 197–280), Fibronectin type III domain (residues 285–371), and Protein tyrosine kinase domain (residues 587–854). Two short MoRFs are embedded into MERTK, regions 256 to 262 and 980 to 987. Similar to PROS1 (see Figure 7A), Figure 7B shows that the context-dependent interaction sites found in MERKT are mostly located within or in close proximity to the disordered or flexible regions and that both MoRFs overlap with the context-dependent interaction sites. Furthermore, this protein has 22 predicted PTMs, all located after the amino acid 450. There are 12 phosphorylation sites, 9 ubiquitylation sites, and 1 di-methylation site. All these observations further strengthened the hypothesis that intrinsic protein disorder has a role in the functionality of this protein.


#### d. Identification of disorder-to-order transition (DOT) regions
  We next wanted to understand the role of numerous cysteine residues in PROS1. To assess whether this protein can undergo conformational reorganization following changes in the redox state of its environment, we used IUPred’s redox tool. We identified two segments of PROS1 that are predicted to be disordered before binding and undergo a transition to order upon binding (i.e, Disorder to Order Transition (DOT) regions). These two redox-related DOTs of PROS1 were near the N-terminus of the protein (residues 48–72 and 103–261) (Figure 7A). These regions were marked by high levels of disorder, as noted by our per-residue disorder analysis (see Figure 5). On the contrary, MERK does not show any evidence of DOT (i.e,. redox-sensitive regions) (Figure 7B). Therefore, it likely does not undergo disorder-order transitions at the changes in the redox environments.


#### e. Multiple Sequence Alignment
  Through this comprehensive analysis, we were able to evaluate the conservation of disordered regions in the PROS1 and MERTK proteins across the species examined, including PROS_MACMU (monkey), PROS_BOVIN (pig), PROS_RAT (rat), PROS_MOUSE (mouse), and PROS_RABIT (rabbit) for PROS1, and PROS_MOUSE (mouse) and PROS_RAT (rat) for MERTK. This information provides valuable insights into the evolutionary conservation of these proteins and their potential functional importance.

  To assess the conservation of amino acid positions, we performed multiple sequence alignment (MSA) using MUSCLE, a robust and reliable method for evaluating sequence conservation in protein alignments. The conservation score per residue was calculated based on a window of 25 amino acids. In this context, a conservation score of 3.0 (c-terminus of PROS1; Figure 5B and 5D), indicates that three out of the total sequences share the same amino acid at a given position. Similarly, scores of 4.0 or 5.0 indicate that four or five sequences, respectively, have the same amino acid at that position (Figure 5B). By analyzing the conservation score graph generated from the MUSCLE alignment, we observed several well-conserved regions (peaks in the graph) interspersed with less conserved regions (troughs in the graph). This observation suggests that the well-conserved regions correspond to functionally important domains of the protein, while the less conserved regions may be more amenable or subject to varying selective pressures.


#### f. Binary Prediction of Protein Disorder
  Figure 8 illustrates the intrinsic disorder status of all 19 proteins involved in the PROS1-MERTK network and shows that all these proteins are characterized by relatively high disorder content. Here, Figure 8A classifies a protein based on its propensity for intrinsic disorder as determined by the outputs of the per-residue disorder predictor PONDR® VSL2. This classification is based on the accepted practice that groups proteins based on their PPIDR values that are determined by PONDR® VSL2 38, where proteins with PPIDR < 10% are considered as ordered or mostly ordered; proteins with 10% ≤ PPIDR < 30% are considered as moderately disordered; whereas proteins with the PPIDR ≥ 30% are considered as highly disordered 38.

  The level of disorder in proteins can also be classified based on their corresponding average disorder score (ADS) values, with proteins being annotated as highly ordered (ADS < 0.15), moderately disordered, or flexible (0.15 ≤ ADS < 0.5), and highly disordered (ADS ≥ 0.5). Based on these classification criteria, none of the proteins in the analyzed dataset was predicted as ordered. Furthermore, Figure 8A shows that only 5 of these proteins (including MERTK) were predicted as moderately disordered/flexible/containing noticeable IDRs based on their ADS and PPIDR values. Additional 10 proteins (including PROS1) were expected to be moderately disordered based on their ADS values, whereas 4 proteins are expected to be highly disordered.

  We also looked at the global disorder status of proteins using the ΔCH-ΔCDF plot (a tool that combines the outputs of two binary predictors (i.e., tools classifying proteins as mostly ordered or mostly disordered), the charge-hydropathy (CH) plot and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, see Materials and Methods section). Due to the differences between the CH and CDF plot analyses, this approach allows the classification of a query protein as mostly ordered, molten globule-like or hybrid, or highly disordered based on its position within the CH-CDF phase space. Figure 8B shows that most of the proteins (including PROS1) in this network are located within the quadrant Q1 (bottom right corner), which contains proteins predicted to be ordered by both predictors. On the other hand, two proteins (including MERTK) are positioned within the quadrant Q2 (bottom left corner), being predicted to be ordered/compact by the CH-plot and disordered by the CDF analysis (i.e., it contains either molten globular proteins, which are compact, but do not have unique 3D structures, or hybrid proteins containing high levels of ordered and disordered residues). Quadrant Q3 (top left corner) includes one protein, which both predictors predict as disordered. Therefore, they are expected to be highly disordered and behave as native coils or native pre-molten globules in their unbound states. The CH-CDF plot analysis supports our other results and demonstrates that intrinsically disordered proteins and intrinsically protein regions are involved in the PROS1-MERTK protein-protein interaction network.


### Protein-Protein Interaction Network
  Thus far, our analysis has shown that the intrinsic disorder is embedded into PROS1 and MERTK. The final step in our disorder-based bioinformatics analysis was centered on understanding how the intrinsic disorder may expand the binding capabilities of PROS1 and MERTK. Our STRING analysis demonstrated that PROS1 and MERTK have predicted binding partners beyond their own interaction (Figure 9). There are 19 predicted nodes in the 1st shell interaction network between these two proteins. There are 40 predicted edges (i.e., interactions) in the network, and the expected number of edges for a randomly selected set of proteins of the same size is 17, indicating that the network has significantly more interactions than expected (p-value < 2.0×10−6).


## Discussion
  To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to elucidate the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK in the context of intrinsic disorder. Our group was first made aware of this ligand-receptor interaction when it was reported by Kaler et. al. 10. We were able to show that there were intrinsically disordered protein regions (IDPR) and segments lacking stable structure in both the PROS1 and MERTK proteins. The AlphaFold models of each protein qualitatively demonstrated that both proteins had IDPRs (i.e., spaghetti-like regions). In addition, we were able to generate a high-resolution, moderately confident model of the PROS1-MERTK interaction face (pLDDT = 70.68). We also identified points of interaction between PROS1 and MERTK. By zooming in on these points of interaction, we can scrutinize the interface at an atomic level. These close contact points reinforce our predictions and provide a detailed understanding of the interface that might play a crucial role in the functioning of the PROS1-MERTK complex. Our investigation demonstrated high confidence that PROS1 was 23.37% disordered and MERTK was 23.09% disordered, classifying them as moderately disordered and flexible proteins, indicating their interface may rely on structural flexibility (i.e., intrinsic protein disorder).

  While the disorder scores of proteins were similar, they demonstrated different amino acid compositional biases. PROS1 was significantly enriched in two order-promoting residues, especially cysteine, the most order-promoting residue (p-value < 0.05). While PROS1 is enriched with many order-promoting cysteines, our IUPred analysis demonstrated that there are disorder-to-order transition (DOT) regions at amino acids 48 to 72 and 103 to 261. The presence of cysteines (i.e., order-promoting residue) in a protein with DOT regions is counter-intuitive. Yet, these findings are consistent with the ability of cysteines to provide enhanced conformational flexibility when they are flanked by regions of disorder 62. Of the two DOT regions in PROS1 identified by IUPred, the sequence from 48 to 72 is highly enriched in cysteines that undergo post-translational modifications PTMs (see PTMs in Figure 6). MERTK was significantly enriched in two-disorder promoting residues, serine and proline, two highly disorder-promoting residues (p-value < 0.05). There were no DOT regions in MERTK identified by IUPred. Previous studies suggested that the PROS1-MERTK protein-protein interaction cause a tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and is an attractive target for novel immunotherapies. We demonstrate that IDPRs and DOT regions must be considered when developing novel immunotherapies targeting the PROS1-MERTK interaction as they may be present in this TIME-promoting protein-protein interaction (PPI). Lastly, our analysis demonstrated evolutionary conservation of amino acid residues between residues throughout both proteins. Further analysis of the specific amino acid ranges in the conservation score graph will provide detailed information about the conservation patterns and potentially identify functionally relevant regions within PROS1 and MERTK.

  Our study presents an exploration of the intricate process of protein folding, specifically focusing on the transition from disorder to order, a process driven by a confluence of factors such as the physicochemical properties of amino acids, their sequence, and the surrounding environment. The forces guiding this transition, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces, play critical roles in shaping the ordered structure of a protein. Despite advances in understanding this process, predicting the exact driving force behind this transition for a specific protein remains a challenge due to the intricacies of protein folding. It is important to bear in mind that our study, although achieving significant strides using AlphaFold to predict protein structures, has its limitations. AlphaFold employs machine learning algorithms trained on known protein structures, but it does not explicitly simulate the physical process of protein folding. Consequently, it may not always capture the specific driving forces propelling the transition from disorder to order.

  Moreover, the protein structures predicted by AlphaFold represent static snapshots of the most likely conformations of the proteins. In reality, however, proteins exist as dynamic ensembles of conformations. The transition among these states is a process influenced by a multitude of factors, some of which are beyond the current scope of AlphaFold’s predictive capability. This represents a limitation in the current state of protein structure prediction and underscores the need for future research in this area.

  While our study brings new considerations on how to target the PROS1-MERTK PPI, several studies have shown that IDPs and IDPRs are associated with ocular malignancies, including uveal melanoma 13,14,63,64. In addition, IDPs, IDPRs, and DOT regions have been considered attractive drug targets for some time now 64–67. There is potential to apply these same principles to developing immunotherapies directed at the PROS1-MERTK PPI.

  The next best steps could be modeling these disordered segments through molecular dynamic simulations followed by their docking analysis. Suppose these additional efforts are coupled with in vitro disorder-based drug discovery efforts. In that case, a novel immunotherapy targeting the intrinsic disorder in the PROS1-MERTK PPI may be identified and eventually translated to direct care of Class 2 uveal melanoma patients. Our findings are strictly theoretical, yet we believe they establish a necessary biological framework when making efforts to target the PROS1-MERTK interaction.

  As with every study, our study has limitations. We used a set of databases and bioinformatics tools, and this approach does not represent a comprehensive nor exhaustive investigation into the interaction between PROS1 and MERTK. We aimed to analyze the intrinsic disorder qualitatively and quantitatively in each protein and make some conjectures on how intrinsic protein disorder may influence their interaction. In our research, we utilized the Google Colaboratory environment and ChimeraX software to run AlphaFold predictions. However, it’s important to underscore that there may be a small reduction in average accuracy for multimers when using this approach compared to a local AlphaFold installation. For full accuracy in predicting multimeric protein structures, a local AlphaFold installation is preferable. This setup offers greater control over computational resources, allowing for improved optimization and potentially higher accuracy in predicting protein structures, particularly for complex multimeric systems like the PROS1-MERTK interaction. Moreover, we acknowledge that implementing MD simulations with suitable IDP-specific force fields such as ff14IDPSFF, ESFF1 and ff03CMAP can significantly enrich our understanding of the transition from disorder to order and the dynamic behavior of proteins 68–70. Such a refined approach could shed more light on the specific residues involved in the interaction, offering a deeper understanding of their dynamic interplay. In addition, the PROS1 and MERTK do not have mutually exclusive binding and have promiscuous binding abilities, as shown in our STRING analysis. While targeting this interaction may lead to therapeutic benefits, both proteins’ complex protein network may be a limiting factor in these developments.

  While these findings are limited as they are computationally based, we have demonstrated that intrinsically disordered protein regions are likely involved in the PROS-MERTK interaction. Additional work will be needed to translate these findings to direct patient care. The modern drug discovery paradigm and therapeutic target development is grounded in structure and order-based assumptions. We are challenging that paradigm and believe these drug discovery campaigns must also be built on the assumption that proteins have flexibility, altering the solution phase of possible therapeutic development. There is the possibility to develop immunotherapy that alters the intrinsically disordered-based protein-protein interaction between PROS1 and MERTK, thus inhibiting the tumor microenvironment in uveal melanomas associated with this interaction.


## Conclusions
  Our study demonstrates that intrinsically disordered protein regions are present in PROS1 and MERTK. The interaction between these two proteins is associated with tumor immune microenvironments in class 2 (BAP1 loss) uveal melanoma. A previous study by Dr. Harbour’s group recently noted this interaction and suggested that the PROS1-MERTK pathway could be a potential target for immunotherapy in uveal melanoma. We were the first to report a high-resolution, moderately confident prediction of the PROS1-MERTK complex. In conjunction with our other finds, intrinsically disordered protein regions or disorder-to-order transition regions in PROS1 and MERTK could be considered when developing immunotherapies for this protein-protein interaction.
