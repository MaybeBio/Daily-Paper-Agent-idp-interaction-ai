## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (preprint, bioRxiv 2023.05.28.542535)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodology, and claims as presented in the provided text; no external data or supplementary figures were available for inspection beyond what is described in the text
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use coarse-grained (CG) simulations to argue that differences in net charge and charge patterning, particularly in disordered regions, govern the distinct liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) propensities of human HP1α, HP1β, and HP1γ. They further claim that folded domains (CD, CSD) contribute to phase separation, that DNA differentially modulates HP1 paralog condensate stability, and that HP1α acts as a scaffold recruiting HP1β as a client while co-phase-separating cooperatively with HP1γ.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figs. 1–7), supplementary figures (S1–S5), tables (S1–S2), movies (S1–S6), and methods sections for CG and all-atom MD simulations
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures, tables, and movies were not provided for direct inspection; experimental validation data referenced from prior work are not reproduced here; no raw simulation trajectories or analysis scripts are available

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a systematic computational investigation of the sequence determinants of HP1 paralog phase separation, extending prior work on HP1α to a comparative analysis across all three human paralogs and their heterodimers. The study is timely and addresses a question of significant biological interest, namely how subtle sequence differences among highly homologous proteins translate into distinct phase behavior and chromatin localization. The CG simulation framework is well-established and the authors apply it carefully, including controls for finite-size effects and comparisons to atomistic simulations. The central claims regarding the role of charge distribution and the competitive interplay between protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions are plausible and largely supported by the simulation data presented. However, the manuscript would be strengthened by a more direct quantitative link between simulated observables and experimental measurements, and by a clearer discussion of the limitations of the CG model in capturing the full complexity of HP1 behavior in the cellular context. The conclusions regarding biological localization patterns, while reasonable, are somewhat speculative given the simplified in silico system.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of phase separation, chromatin biology, and biophysics of intrinsically disordered proteins would find this study of interest. The work provides a molecular-level framework for understanding how charge distribution dictates differential phase behavior among paralogs, which is relevant for interpreting experimental observations of HP1 localization and function. The methodological approach, including the use of CG simulations to dissect multicomponent condensates, will also appeal to computational biologists studying LLPS.
- **Major strengths** The study is well-designed and systematically executed. The use of a single consistent CG framework to compare all three paralogs and their chimeras is a clear strength, allowing for direct attribution of behavioral differences to sequence features. The domain-swapping approach is particularly informative, isolating the contributions of specific regions. The inclusion of DNA in the simulations adds a relevant layer of biological complexity. The authors are careful to validate their CG results against available experimental data and atomistic simulations where possible. The discussion of scaffold-client versus cooperative co-phase separation is conceptually clear and provides testable predictions.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "DNA can significantly reshape the stability of a minimal condensate formed by HP1 paralogs due to competitive interactions of HP1α with HP1β and HP1γ versus DNA."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "DNA mediates phase separation of HP1α homo- and heterodimers", Figures 5a–j
  - **Concern** The simulations are performed at a single, relatively high temperature (320 K) and a single salt concentration (100 mM). The competitive interaction between HP1 paralogs and DNA is likely highly sensitive to both parameters. The authors do not explore how the balance between protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions changes as a function of temperature or salt concentration, which is critical for assessing the robustness of the claimed competitive mechanism. Furthermore, the DNA model used is a simplified two-bead-per-nucleotide representation; the authors do not discuss the potential limitations of this model in capturing sequence-specific or conformational effects of DNA that could influence binding.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusion regarding DNA's role in reshaping condensate stability and localization depends on the relative strengths of competing interactions. If this balance is not robust to changes in solution conditions, the biological relevance of the finding is diminished. The lack of a systematic parameter sweep weakens the claim that the observed competitive effect is a general property of the system rather than an artifact of the specific simulation conditions.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform simulations at a range of temperatures (e.g., 280–350 K) and salt concentrations (e.g., 50–300 mM) for at least the key systems (HP1α homodimer, HP1α+HP1β, HP1α+HP1γ, with and without DNA) to demonstrate that the qualitative conclusions regarding competitive interactions are unchanged. Alternatively, they could provide a theoretical argument or a simplified analytical model that predicts the observed behavior over a range of conditions.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "the folded domains, particularly the CD domain, also substantially contribute to the multivalent interactions required for the formation of oligomeric networks and phase separation."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Disordered and folded domains cooperatively mediate phase separation of HP1 proteins", Figures 3g–l
  - **Concern** The domain-swapping experiments are performed on chimeric constructs where entire folded domains are replaced. This approach cannot distinguish between effects due to the specific amino acid sequence of the domain and effects due to the domain's overall size, shape, or surface charge distribution. The authors attribute the enhanced phase separation of chimeras with HP1β/HP1γ CDs to the higher negative charge and larger solvent-accessible surface area of these domains. However, swapping a folded domain also changes the connectivity and local environment of the disordered regions, which could independently affect phase behavior. The authors do not perform control simulations where, for example, the charge of the HP1α CD is artificially neutralized or mutated to match the net charge of the HP1β/HP1γ CDs without changing the rest of the sequence.
  - **Why it matters** The claim that the CD domain's charge properties are the primary cause of the observed enhancement is a specific mechanistic statement. Without more targeted perturbations, alternative explanations (e.g., changes in domain dynamics, steric effects, or altered coupling to disordered regions) cannot be ruled out. This weakens the mechanistic insight the authors aim to provide.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should perform additional simulations with more subtle perturbations, such as charge-reversal or charge-neutralization mutations on the surface of the HP1α CD, to directly test the role of surface charge. Alternatively, they could simulate the chimeras with the swapped CD but with the disordered regions held in a conformation identical to the wild-type, to isolate the effect of the domain itself.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scientific importance
  - **Claim pointer** The authors propose a model where "HP1α is central in regulating DNA compaction and initiating protein condensation with DNA or upon phosphorylation. HP1γ preferentially enters and cooperatively enhances HP1α condensates... HP1β creates a phase boundary... and fine-tunes HP1-DNA phase separation in a concentration-dependent manner, potentially limiting heterochromatin spreading."
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section, Figure 7
  - **Concern** The extrapolation from simplified in silico binary and ternary mixtures to the complex nuclear environment is a significant leap. The model does not account for the presence of nucleosomes, histone modifications other than H3K9me, RNA, or a myriad of other nuclear proteins that are known to interact with HP1. The authors acknowledge this limitation but do not discuss how their predictions might be tested experimentally in a way that isolates the proposed charge-based mechanisms from other confounding factors. The biological significance of the proposed "phase boundary" created by HP1β, for example, is unclear without a discussion of how this would manifest in the context of chromatin domains.
  - **Why it matters** The manuscript aims to provide a "molecular framework for understanding their role in chromatin organization." If the proposed model is not readily testable or is likely to be overridden by other interactions in the cell, its impact on the field is limited. The authors should more clearly articulate the specific, testable predictions of their model and how they relate to existing experimental observations of HP1 localization and dynamics.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should strengthen the discussion by proposing specific experimental designs (e.g., in vitro reconstitution assays with defined components, or live-cell imaging experiments with mutant HP1 proteins) that could directly test the predicted scaffold-client and cooperative relationships. They should also discuss how their model might be integrated with existing knowledge of HP1 post-translational modifications and interactions with other chromatin factors.

  - **Affected element** Introduction, first paragraph
  - **Evidence pointer** Introduction, paragraph 1
  - **Issue** The introduction assumes familiarity with the concept of liquid-liquid phase separation and its relevance to chromatin biology. A brief, more general introduction to LLPS and its role in organizing the nucleus would make the manuscript more accessible to a broader readership.

  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Affected element** Methods, CG simulation protocol
  - **Evidence pointer** Materials and Methods, "Coarse-grained MD simulation protocol"
  - **Issue** The description of the CG model for the folded domains is brief. It is stated that they are "kept rigid," but the specific parameters used (e.g., the interaction strengths between folded domain beads and other beads) are not provided. This makes it difficult to reproduce the simulations.
  - **Required correction** Provide a more detailed description of the CG model parameters for the folded domains, including the interaction parameters with the disordered regions and DNA, or refer to a previous publication where these parameters are fully described.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Scientific importance
  - **Affected element** Results, "DNA influences the co-localization of HP1 paralogs"
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Figures 6d–f
  - **Issue** The observation that HP1β localizes to the periphery of HP1α-DNA condensates is interesting, but the authors do not discuss the potential functional consequences of this spatial arrangement. Does this imply a role for HP1β in regulating the surface properties of the condensate or in interacting with other nuclear components at the interface?
  - **Required correction** Add a brief discussion of the potential functional implications of the observed spatial segregation of HP1β at the condensate periphery.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Readability for nonspecialists
  - **Affected element** Discussion, paragraph 3
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion, paragraph 3
  - **Issue** The discussion of the "phase boundary" created by HP1β is somewhat abstract. A more concrete explanation of what this means physically and biologically would be helpful.
  - **Required correction** Clarify the concept of a "phase boundary" in this context, perhaps by relating it to the idea of a barrier to mixing or a region of distinct material properties, and discuss its potential implications for chromatin organization.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** The primary technical concern is the lack of a systematic exploration of simulation conditions (temperature, salt) to establish the robustness of the competitive interaction mechanism between HP1 paralogs and DNA (R1-M1). The mechanistic attribution of the CD domain's role to its charge properties is also not fully established without more targeted mutations (R1-M2). These issues need to be addressed to solidify the central claims of the manuscript.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The work is original in its systematic comparison of all three HP1 paralogs and their heterodimers within a unified CG framework, and in its explicit focus on the competitive interplay between protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions. This goes beyond previous studies that focused on individual paralogs.
  - **Scientific importance** The question of how sequence differences among paralogs dictate distinct phase behavior and biological function is of high importance for understanding chromatin organization and gene regulation. The proposed model, if validated, could have broad implications.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The topic is at the interface of biophysics, computational biology, and chromatin biology. The findings are likely to be of interest to researchers in all three areas, though the technical nature of the simulations may be a barrier for some experimental biologists.
  - **Technical soundness** The methodology is generally sound and well-executed, but the concerns raised above regarding parameter sensitivity and mechanistic attribution need to be addressed to fully establish the technical robustness of the conclusions.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is clearly written but assumes a certain level of familiarity with LLPS and simulation techniques. The main findings are presented in an accessible manner, but the discussion could be more explicit in translating the simulation results into testable biological predictions.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The manuscript presents a valuable and timely computational analysis with the potential to significantly advance our understanding of HP1 paralog phase behavior. However, the central claims regarding the competitive role of DNA and the specific contribution of the CD domain require additional simulation data to be fully convincing. Addressing these concerns would substantially strengthen the manuscript and make it suitable for publication in a high-impact venue.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the CD domain's higher negative charge and larger SASA are the direct cause of enhanced phase separation in chimeras is not fully supported, as the domain-swapping experiments do not isolate these specific properties from other potential effects.
- The extrapolation of the in silico model to the complex in vivo nuclear environment, particularly the proposed roles of HP1β in creating a "phase boundary" and limiting heterochromatin spreading, is speculative and not directly supported by the simulation data presented.
- The statement that "DNA can significantly reshape the stability of a minimal condensate" is based on simulations at a single temperature and salt concentration; the generality of this finding is not established.
- The biological relevance of the simulated heterodimer phase separation is inferred from co-immunoprecipitation experiments, but the simulations do not directly model the cellular context where such heterodimers would form and function.
