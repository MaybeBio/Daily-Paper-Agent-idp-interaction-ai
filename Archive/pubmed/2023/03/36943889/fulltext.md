# Mechanomemory in protein diffusivity of chromatin and nucleoplasm after force cessation


## Significance
  Mechanomemory is a response to force long after force cessation. Nuclear mechanomemory has been largely unknown. This research shows that nuclear mechanomemory is regulated by nuclear pore complexes by elevating nuclear protein diffusivity. We report that externally applied force via integrins elevated chromatin and nucleoplasm protein diffusivity tens of minutes after cessation of force. Additionally, external force dissociated clusters of heterochromatin protein 1 alpha (HP1α), likely by reversing protein–protein condensation in phase separation via unjamming of the chromatin. The current finding suggests that force may regulate nuclear processes and activities in a previously unknown manner.


## Results

### Mechanomemory in Nucleoplasm Diffusivity after Force Cessation.
  Our recent work has shown that a local surface stress via integrins directly stretches (within 1 to 10 ms of stress application) the chromatin domains to induce rapid (<15 s after stress onset) upregulation of the transgene DHFR and endogenous genes egr-1 and Cav-1 (1–4). Since the external sinusoidal cyclic stress applies mechanical energy on the nucleus via the LINC (linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton) to lamins to deform chromatin via nuclear proteins lamina-associated polypeptide 2 beta (LAP2β) and barrier-to-autointegration factor (BAF) (4), we hypothesized that this energy would alter the protein molecular dynamics (e.g., diffusivity) of single proteins inside the nucleoplasm even after termination of the force. To test this hypothesis, we applied a local stress to a living cell via integrins using RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) peptide-coated magnetic bead (4 μm in diameter) (Fig. 1A). A local stress of 15 Pa was applied at 0.3 Hz for either 2 min or 10 min, and fluorescent imaging was performed in living CHO (Chinese Hamster Ovary) cells 5 min after the cessation of stress (Fig. 1B). The 2-min or 10-min stress application was chosen because the stress duration causes upregulation of the transgene dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) (1–3) (a house-keeping gene for synthesis of thymine) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) but is too short for the translocation of cytoplasmic molecules such as yes-associated protein (YAP) into the nucleus to activate transcription factors (4). The protocol to image single molecules 5 min after stress termination was chosen based on the observation that gene transcription in these CHO cells remains up-regulated up to ~30 min post stress application (2).

  Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) was used to evaluate the diffusion of single molecules with a confocal scanning time-resolved imaging system. The system was calibrated with rhodamine 110 (488-nm excitation) on a rigid glass dish to obtain the excitation volume of the confocal profile (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) (13). Mean square displacement (MSD) was used to calculate the diffusion of the green fluorescent proteins (GFPs) that attach to the transgene DHFR via the tight 256 interactions of GFP-LacI and LacO (DHFR-GFP) (1), whereas FCS was used to calculate free GFPs near DHFR gene locus and single free GFPs in the nucleoplasm (Fig. 1C). The diffusion coefficient of DHFR-GFP spots on the chromatin increased from 6.54 × 10−8 µm2/s to 9.11 × 10−8 µm2/s after 2-min stress and to 13.1 × 10−8 µm2/s after a 10-min stress (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), suggesting that the duration of mechanical stress exerted on the chromatin increased protein mobility. Typical FCS curve is shown in Fig. 1E, and the apparent diffusion coefficient was fitted with a three-dimensional (3D) one-component anomalous diffusion model. The sample volume was based on FCS measurements and data analysis was estimated to be 0.739 ± 0.149 (mean ± SEM) femtoliter (13). The autocorrelation function (G) versus the lag time provides an estimate of the time spent by the individual molecules in traversing the focal distance, with a shorter lag time denoting less time spent, hence a faster diffusivity (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, diffusivity of free GFPs near the DHFR gene locus (<0.5 μm in distance) was also elevated 5 min after the application of stress, which increased from 1.07 µm2/s at zero stress to 2.20 µm2/s after 2 min of stress and to 4.01 µm2/s after 10-min stress (Fig. 1F). However, the diffusivity of free GFPs near the DHFR gene locus was ~7-orders of magnitude higher than that of DHFR-GFPs (comparing Fig. 1F with Fig. 1D), consistent with the notion that the DHFR-GFPs were tightly anchored onto the chromatin and thus were less mobile than the unanchored proteins. After 1-h post stress application, the diffusion coefficient of free GFPs near DHFR returned to the prior-stress level, for both the 2-min stress and 10-min stress (Fig. 1F) conditions. The diffusion coefficient of free GFPs in the nucleoplasm that were not near the DHFR gene locus (>0.5 μm in distance) was elevated 5 min post stress application, and the extent of increase was found to be dependent on the duration of stress application (Fig. 1G). Diffusion in the nucleoplasm at the nuclear center (>1.5 μm from the envelope) was elevated to a higher degree than at the nuclear periphery (<1.5 μm from the nuclear envelope) post 10-min stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusivity in the absence of stress did not change with time (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), suggesting that the observed elevation of the diffusivity was a result of the applied stress. This observation is notable, because it suggests that a local cell surface stress via integrins can cause changes in single protein molecular dynamics throughout the nucleoplasm, likely via alteration of the chromatin structures in the nucleus, consistent with the published observation that the strain on the nucleus induced by the magnetic bead stress on the cell surface, measured via H2B-GFP, is substantial throughout the nucleus (1, 14). The elevated diffusivity of nucleoplasm-free GFPs returned to the baseline level 1 h post stress (Fig. 1G). Together our results show that diffusivity of single protein molecules is elevated at least 5 min after stress cessation, and the stress effect on protein diffusivity becomes negligible after 60 min.


### Force Reverses Chemically Induced Chromatin and Nucleoplasm Condensation.
  Next, we investigated whether the applied stress could reverse the change in diffusivity induced by histone modifiers. JIB-04 is a known histone H3K9 demethylase inhibitor (15) and treating the CHO cells with JIB-04 increases H3K9me3 levels, condenses the chromatin, and blocks stress-induced DHFR upregulation (2). Treating cells with JIB-04 decreased the diffusivity of DHFR-GFP spots but a 10-min stress in the presence of JIB-04 returned the diffusivity back to the control level (Fig. 2A). Similarly, nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusivity was lowered by JIB-04, and the 10-min stress overrode the effect of chromatin condenser and even elevated the diffusivity higher than that before stress (Fig. 2B). Chaetocin is a known inhibitor of H3K9me3 methyltransferase (16) and treating with chaetocin decreases H3K9me3, decondenses chromatin, and enhances stress-induced DHFR upregulation (2). Treating the cells with chaetocin alone slightly increased diffusivity of both DHFR-GFP spots and nucleoplasm-free GFP. Applying a 10-min stress in the presence of chaetocin further increased the diffusivity (Fig. 2 C and D). These results suggest that the applied force is potent enough to reverse the chemically induced reduction in single protein diffusivity in the nucleoplasm, possibly via force propagation along the chromatin network to alter chromatin dynamics and thus the nucleoplasm.


### HP1-Mediated Heterochromatin Diffusivity Elevates after Force Cessation.
  Heterochromatin Protein 1 (HP1) is implicated in gene silencing and it binds to H3K9 methyltransferase SUV39h1 (17) and mediates force transmission from nuclear lamina to chromatin (1). We investigated how stress might influence the diffusivity of HP1 in the nucleus of a MCF7 cell, a breast cancer cell line. The diffusivity of HP1α-GFP increased 5 min after a 2-min stress or a 10-min stress but returned to the prestress baseline 1 h after stress cessation (Fig. 3 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). There was elevation in diffusivity after 2-min and 10-min stress at the nuclear center region and at the nuclear periphery, although the diffusion coefficient at zero-stress was lower at the nuclear periphery than at the nuclear center (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). The temporal controls show that without stress, HP1α-GFP diffusivity did not change (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). To examine in detail how HP1α-GFP clusters respond to applied stress, we quantified HP1α-GFP cluster size and number. The diameter of HP1α-GFP clusters decreased after stress cessation (Fig. 3D). However, the number of HP1α-GFP clusters increased after stress cessation (Fig. 3E) and this increase occurred only at the nuclear periphery but not near the center of the nucleus (Fig. 3F). From Fig. 3 D and F, we find that the total area (area times the number of clusters) of all HP1α-GFP clusters in a cell did not change before and after stress, suggesting that the applied stress dispersed HP1α-GFP clusters into smaller sized clusters and thus countered the process of phase separation of HP1α from the nucleoplasm liquid.

  To further investigate the effect of applied stress on the dynamic behavior of HP1α, we cotransfected HP1α-GFP and HP1α-mCherry in MCF7 cells and quantified changes in FRET signal in live cells after the application of stress. A lower HP1α FRET efficiency indicates that HP1α molecules are more separated, suggesting decondensation of the chromatin that is recruited by HP1. A 10-min stress decreased HP1α FRET and thus decondensed the HP1α-dependent chromatin, and the FRET levels returned to the baseline 1 h after stress termination (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The control cells transfected with HP1α-GFP alone exhibited no temporal changes in FRET in the absence of stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). The reduction in FRET efficiency from 5 to 1% in response to force was similar to the decrease (in positive controls) induced by the histone deacetylase inhibitor Trichostatin A (TSA) or Chaetocin (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Both chemicals are known to decondense the chromatin in living cells, observed by electron microscopy (18, 19). In contrast, JIB-04, which condenses the chromatin, did not change the FRET efficiency of HP1α (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), suggesting that the FRET efficiency of HP1α-GFP and HP1α-mCherry was already at the high level and could not be further increased by additional chromatin condensation. Together these results suggest that the applied stress alters the diffusivity at least in part by altering the condensation levels of chromatin.


### MBD1-Mediated DNA Diffusivity Elevates after Force Cessation.
  To further explore the after-effects of force application, we employed a FRET biosensor based on MBD1 (Methyl-CpG Binding Domain Protein 1)-GFP and MBD1-mCherry interaction. MBD1 binds to methylated sequences in the DNA and likely mediates gene repression (20). A lower MBD1 FRET efficiency indicates that the MBD1 molecules are more separated, suggesting DNA/chromatin decondensation. An applied 10-min stress lowered MBD1 FRET efficiency of MBD1 and the effect of the applied forces sustained 5 min after force cessation and disappeared 1 h after stress termination (Fig. 4 C and D); as a control, MBD1-GFP alone did not change with stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 C and D). These results further suggest that after stress cessation the DNA diffusivity remained high tens of minutes after stress termination.


### Diffusivity Mechanomemory Is Regulated by Nuclear Pore Complex.
  Because mechanomemory in protein diffusivity after force cessation is dependent on the duration of force application and thus the energy input, we hypothesized that the transport via the nuclear pore complex (NPC) (21, 22) could regulate the duration of the mechanomemory. To test our hypothesis, we pretreated CHO cells with Pitstop 2, a known NPC permeability barrier disrupter (4, 23), to maximize transport via the NPC, or with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) that is a specific nuclear pore blocker (4, 24) to block all the transport via the NPC, or with both reagents. Compared with the control cells whose chromatin DHFR-GFP diffusivity and nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusivity remained high 5 min after stress cessation, both diffusivity in Pitstop 2-treated cells were the same as the preloading values (Fig. 5 A and B); in other words, the mechanomemory was lost after NPC transport was maximized. Pretreating the cells with WGA resulted in the opposite effect: Both chromatin DHFR-GFP diffusivity and nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusivity stayed high 1 h after stress cessation (Fig. 5 A and B), suggesting that blocking the transport of NPC extended the duration of mechanomemory. Pretreating with both WGA and Pitstop 2 led to the similar results as treating with WGA alone, suggesting that WGA interfered with the action of Pitstop 2 (Fig. 5 A and B) such that the ultimate effect was the same as WGA alone.

  To modulate the nuclear envelope tension to change the size of the NPCs (60 to 120 nm) (21, 22) without changing the number of the NPCs, we used the established protocols to increase the nuclear envelope tension and thus increase the nuclear pore size with extracellular hypo-osmotic pressure (~220 mOsm) by the addition of 30% pure water (−80 mOsm from the isotonic medium of ~300 mOsm) or to decrease the nuclear envelope tension with extracellular hyperosmotic pressure (~410 mOsm) with the addition of 0.1 M PEG 300 (+110 mOsm) (25, 26). Hypo-osmotic or hyperosmotic pressure alone in the absence of the applied local stress had no effect on diffusivity of nucleoplasm GFPs (Fig. 6). In contrast, after cessation of the applied force, increasing the nuclear pore size by hypo-osmotic pressure led to a similar time course of diffusion coefficient of the nucleoplasm-free GFPs as the isotonic medium control. Decreasing the nuclear pore size by hyperosmotic pressure resulted in the diffusion coefficient being staying elevated 1 h after force cessation (Fig. 6), which is consistent with the data of blocking the nuclear pore complexes with WGA. Treating the cells with lower hypo-osmotic pressure (+40% water, ~193 mOsm) or with higher hyperosmotic pressure (+0.26 M PEG 300, ~586 mOsm) resulted in similar results as the 30% water or the 0.1 M PEG 300 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), suggesting that these perturbations did not further alter the diffusion coefficient of the nucleoplasm. Together, these results suggest that modulating the size of NPCs can alter the duration of mechanomemory in nucleoplasm protein diffusivity after force cessation.

  To elucidate the mechanism of the mechanomemory, we developed a dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) model using a mesoscopic particle-based coarse-graining simulation to simulate the live-cell experimental conditions. All relevant parameters used in the model were estimated from those of the cell nucleus (see Materials and Methods for details) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Interestingly, the simulations showed that the chromatin diffusivity and nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusivity were elevated for a short duration compared with those of prior-stress values under conditions of normal nuclear pore numbers (equivalent to 3 nuclear pores per nanosheet) but decreased and reverted back to the prior-stress values after force cessation (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A–D), as observed in Fig. 5 of live cells. In contrast, when the NPC number in the simulation model was increased to 196 pores per nanosheet (a 65-fold increase), similar to the condition of Pitstop 2-treated cells, the short-duration increase in diffusivity was lost for both chromatin and nucleoplasm-free GFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D), suggesting that maximizing NPC transport diminished the short-duration mechanomemory. When the NPC number was set to zero to inhibit NPC transport, similar to the condition of WGA-treated cells, both chromatin and nucleoplasm-free GFP diffusion coefficients stayed high long after stress cessation (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D), suggesting that mechanomemory was maintained for a long time after force cessation. Furthermore, the elevation of both chromatin and nucleoplasm diffusivity originated from the 10% tensile strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S13F), which was similar to the strain magnitude in the chromatin in live cells (4). Together with live-cell experiments, results from our simulation suggest that regulating transport via the NPCs can substantially regulate the duration of mechanomemory of chromatin and nucleoplasm protein diffusivity after stress cessation.

  To understand the underpinning mechanism of the NPC transport-controlled mechanomemory, we further calculated the particle density inside the nucleus (the region between the two nanosheets in SI Appendix, Fig. S13C) for different numbers of NPCs. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S14E, the particle density inside the nucleus decreased under condition of 3 and 0 NPCs under the tensile strain because of the confinement of nuclear envelope on the exchange of the particles (i.e., the small molecules that can pass through the NPCs) with extra-nucleus environment. As a result, the diffusion coefficient of the substances inside the nucleus increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D). After a period of time (~30 ms in our DPD simulation), the slow diffusing particles that exchanged through NPCs allowed the particle density inside the nucleus to be restored, so the elevated diffusion coefficient returned back to the prior-stress value under the condition of 3 NPCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D). In contrast, when the particle exchange via NPC was blocked under the condition of 0 NPC, the decreased particle density could not be restored, thus the diffusion coefficient of the substances inside the nucleus remained at a high value (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D). However, when the confinement on particles exchange was released (196 NPCs), the particle density inside the nuclear envelope remained constant during the tensile strain, thus the diffusion coefficients of the substances inside the nucleus did not change (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B and D). In addition, SI Appendix, Fig. S14E suggests that the duration of elevated particle density in cell nucleus increased with the decrease in the number of nuclear pores. This result indicates that the rate of particle exchange via the nuclear pores regulates the duration of the elevated diffusion coefficients. The DPD simulations suggest a putative mechanism for the NPCs transport-controlled mechanomemory: The decrease in particle density inside the nucleus induced by the tensile strain leads to the elevation in mobility and diffusion coefficients of chromatin and nucleoplasm protein, and as a result, the “effective temperature” is elevated; in addition, nuclear pores regulate the duration of the elevated diffusion coefficients.


## Discussion
  In the current study using single-molecule spectroscopy and imaging, we have shown that exogenous local forces via integrins decondense the chromatin and increase the apparent diffusivity of the chromatin structure, leading to elevated diffusivity of single protein molecules in the nucleoplasm and thus ‘fluidizing’ the “condensates” in the nucleus.

  The published observation of jamming (solidification) and unjamming (fluidization) of the cytoskeleton at the cellular and supracellular levels appears to depend on the balance of cytoskeletal prestress and the externally applied large mechanical stretch over the whole-cell surface (8). It is shown that the large shear stretching forces, generally much higher than thermal energy, can fluidize the cytoskeleton and lead to an unjammed state of the cell. On the other hand, the observed LLPS behaviors in the nucleus are generally thought to be driven primarily by the multivalent electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions to form condensates in the nucleus. Our current study shows that the applied local stress of physiological amplitudes, while not being large enough to fluidize the cytoskeleton, is sufficient to overcome the condensation process and decondensate the clusters of HP1α in the nucleus to decrease the size of the clusters and increase the diffusivity of HP1α. This finding could potentially connect the two ostensibly distinct processes-cellular jamming/unjamming and molecular LLPS processes. This conclusion is based on the observation that an applied stress that is much higher than the multivalent electrostatic and hydrophobic forces decondenses chromatin structure, elevates its apparent diffusivity, reverses the LLPS condensation process, and increases single protein diffusivity in the nucleoplasm via unjamming the chromatin.

  Mechanomemory is a feature of living cells that undergo persistent structural and phenotypic changes long after the cessation of an external force. In general, after hours to days of placement of the cells on stiff or soft substrates, mechanomemory could be observed hours to days post load cessation, due to the irreversible changes in cytoplasmic and nuclear structures (5–7). In our current study, we show that application of force for much shorter periods (2 to 10 min) is too short for the cell to synthesize proteins to exhibit biological and phenotypic changes observed in the previous studies (5–7) but can result in mechanical memory of tens of minutes in the nucleus due to alteration of the structures and molecule–molecule interactions in the nucleoplasm. The persistent physical effect of force, highlighted in its extended impact on the nucleus such as the mobility of molecules on the chromatin and single proteins in the nucleoplasm to at least 15 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S15), is independent of the translocation of cytoplasmic molecules such as YAP and other molecules that happens only after 60 min of continued stress application (4). Because it takes only ~10 min to complete a DHFR transcript (1) (of 34 kb), the continued transcriptional activity might be partially explained by the finding of the continued chromatin and nucleoplasm mobility 15 to 30 min after cessation of the applied force in the current study. Therefore, our observation that the chromatin diffusivity is elevated many minutes after force cessation is of physiological significance because it can explain our previous observation that the chromatin-stretch-dependent gene upregulation persist for up to 30 min after cessation of the applied force (2). Additional studies are needed to explore the other effects of this “memory” on other nuclear structures and functions.

  Our dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) model has qualitatively recapitulated the experimental results from living cells that mechanomemory persists in the chromatin, and nucleoplasm diffusivity for tens of minutes after stress cessation and this memory is regulated by the NPCs. Recent reports show that the size of and the transport via the NPCs that is tethered to the cytoskeleton is regulated by the tension in the nuclear envelope (21, 27), and transport of RNA via NPCs is unidirectional (28). Our current finding that the mechanomemory of chromatin diffusivity and nucleoplasm protein diffusivity extends the current understanding of the role of the NPCs and highlights the importance of the molecular exchange between the nucleus and the cytoplasm in regulating external force-induced nuclear activities and functions. Currently we do not have data from measurements of nuclear pore size in response to osmotic shock, which could be obtained using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), but high-resolution images of cryo-EM can only be collected statically in isolated nuclear membrane. The nuclear pore sizes vary from 60 nm to 120 nm in a cell (22), and therefore these experiments need to be performed carefully using increased cell numbers in the future. Furthermore, the effects of osmotic swelling/shrinking are not confined to effects on the pore size alone. We cannot rule out the possibility that the osmotic shock may have effects on other cytoplasmic and nuclear structures that, in turn, have effects on protein diffusivity in the nucleoplasm. However, the experimental results with Pitstop 2 that disrupts the nuclear pores and WGA that blocks all nuclear pores (Fig. 5) support the data from osmotic challenges (Fig. 6). Together these data from osmotic challenges and pharmacological perturbations suggest that nuclear pores might be important in regulating the duration of mechanomemory in protein diffusivity of the nucleoplasm.

  Although we do not fully understand why the nucleus exhibits this tens-of-minutes physical change, i.e., elevated protein diffusivity in the nucleoplasm, we hypothesize that it is dependent on the input energy at the nucleus and the semi-enclosed environment of the nucleus via the nuclear pores such that the effective temperature in the nucleus is elevated as a result of the input tensile stress (input mechanical energy per volume). This mechanical energy per volume appears to be large enough to dissociate protein–protein aggregations to reverse the LLPS (liquid–liquid phase separation) process via unjamming the chromatin. The unjammed chromatin by the tensile strains, in turn, might elevate protein mobility and diffusivity of the nucleoplasm by increasing the local volume of the nucleoplasm and thus decreasing the density of the molecules, and as a result, the effective temperature is elevated. The duration of the physical memory can be regulated by the transport via the NPCs, as shown by our DPD model (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

  In the present study, JIB-04 is used as a histone H3K9 demethylase inhibitor but this molecule is also a Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway inhibitor (29). In addition, Pitstop 2 not only affects NPCs but also affects clathrins and clathrin-independent endocytosis (30, 31). Because of the pleiotropic effects of these molecules, we cannot completely rule out potential additional effects of these molecules on the protein diffusivity in the nucleoplasm.

  When a local surface stress is applied to the cell surface via integrins, the stress is directly transmitted to the chromatin via the cytoskeleton and the LINC and the intranuclear protein LAP2β; in addition, it is known that the applied stress via the magnetic bead deforms the nuclear envelope and nuclear lamins (32). Therefore, it is possible that the applied stress can directly regulate nuclear transport by deforming the NPCs. Currently, we are not able to examine this possibility experimentally due to the inability to quantify the size of and/or the transport via the NPCs while applying local stresses. However, the viscoelasticity of the cytoplasm (2) and of the nucleus and lamins (33) exhibits a time constant of 1 to 10 s, which is too short to explain the mechanomemory of many minutes after force cessation. In the current study, the amplitude of the applied sinusoidal-wave stress was kept at 15 Pa and frequency at 0.3 Hz (comparable to shear stress magnitudes in blood vessels during exercise and breathing frequency). In future studies, the amplitude, frequency, waveform, and duration could be varied to examine their influence on the extent of mechanomemory. The DPD model, while simple, was able to recapitulate some essential features observed in the experiments but has some limitations. One limitation is that the model shows that the chromatin diffusion coefficient is only ~2 to 3 orders lower than that of the nucleoplasm-free GFP, much higher than those observed in the cell experiments. The discrepancy between the diffusion coefficient from the DPD model and that from the experimental results in cells is likely due to the fact that the chromatin is a highly folded and condensed structure with a compaction ratio of ~1,000 relative to the 2-nm DNA (34) and thus is highly immobile. In contrast, in the DPD model, the folded chromatin is only assumed to be slightly folded from the nucleosome (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B), much less than the actual condensation state of the chromatin in living cells. A hydrodynamic-scaling model (35) might be used to explain this size effect on the diffusion coefficient, namely, DD0=exp−kRRN , where k is a scaling constant, R is the nanoparticle radius, RN is average polymer mesh size, D is the diffusion coefficient of the nanoparticle in the obstructed environment, and D0 is the diffusion coefficient of the nanoparticle in water. This model suggests that both the particle size and the characteristic size of the polymer network influence the diffusion coefficients of the particles. In our DPD model, the sizes of the folded chromatin and that of the polymer network were both substantially smaller than those of the actual chromatin in living cells, leading to the discrepancy between the diffusion coefficients from the DPD model and those from the experimental result in cells. In the DPD simulation, the degree of chromatin folding was determined by the DPD potential parameter of folded chromatin beads. Calculations based on the above equation show that increasing the degree of chromatin folding can substantially reduce the discrepancy between the diffusion coefficient from the DPD model and that from the experimental result in cells. Another limitation is that the DPD model can only compute the duration for up to ~100 ms, 3 to 4 orders of magnitude shorter than those in the cell experiments. In the future, the DPD model should be improved to quantitatively recapitulate the experimental findings.

  A recent study using microinjected magnetic nanoparticles to probe nuclear mechanics indicated that the chromatin behaves like a fluid (36), and the authors use this finding to challenge the view that interphase chromatin is a gel-like material (18). The results from our current study suggest, however, that the probe size is critical for sensing its microenvironment or nanoenvironment. If the probe size is on the order of tens of nanometers or less, then, one will observe a fluid-like behavior of the probed material because the probe size is much smaller than the pore sizes of the network in the nucleoplasm. On the other hand, if the probe size is greater than 1μm, then the probe perturbs the nucleoplasm network and detects a gel-like structure of chromatin. This interpretation is supported by our results that the chromatin DHFR-GFP spots themselves are less mobile and nonfluid-like. The apparent diffusivity of DHFR GFP spots is seven orders of magnitude lower than the free GFP proteins in the nucleoplasm that are <0.5 μm in distance from the chromatin DHFR gene locus. These results are consistent with our recent finding that the chromatin deformation is independent of the loading frequency for frequencies between 0.3 and 6 Hz, i.e., the chromatin behaves like an elastic material in this frequency range. In short, we caution the conclusion drawn from nanoparticle experiments because these nanoparticles that are ~25 nm in diameter are too small to detect the coarse chromatin structural network to probe the mechanical features of the chromatin structure at appropriate length scales.

  In summary, our current study reveals that a short duration (2 to 10 min) of applied forces can impact the mobility of the chromatin and single proteins in the nucleoplasm tens of minutes after the cessation of force. This prolonged effect after force termination may have implications in our understanding of the overall impact of force on living cells and tissues.


## Materials and Methods

### Cell Culture.
  Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) and human breast cancer MCF7 cells (American Type Culture Collection) were used in this study. In the CHO DG44 cell genome, DHFR transgene, LacO, and multiple copies of a bacterial artificial chromosome were artificially inserted after knocking out the endogenous DHFR (1). CHO DG44 cells were cultured after EGFP-LacI stable expression in Ham’s F12 medium without hypoxanthine (Hyclone) and thymidine with 10% dialysed FBS (fetal bovine serum) (Gibco) by following the processes mentioned previously (1, 4). MCF7 cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, Corning, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS, streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and penicillin (100 IU/mL). CHO DG44 and MCF7 cells were cultured every 2 to 3 d using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid), respectively, and kept in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C. For the two cell lines, mycoplasma contamination was regularly monitored using DAPI stain as well as visual inspection during the single-molecule imaging (SMI) experiments. There was no mycoplasma contamination for any of the two cell lines.


### Magnetic Twisting Cytometry (1D-MTC).
  Magnetic twisting cytometry (MTC) is based on the principle of magnetizing a ferromagnetic micron-sized structure in one axis direction and applying the twisting magnetic field in an orthogonal axis direction in such a way that the resulting twisting torque generates the rotation of the magnetized ferromagnetic microstructure in the plane of these two axes (3). After seeding around 100,000 cells and overnight incubation of CHO DG44 and MCF7 cells in a fibronectin-coated (5 μg/mL, 1-h incubation in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) 35-mm glass-bottomed petri dish (GBD00001-200, Cell E&G, San Diego, CA), approximately 2 × 105 Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads were added on each dish (30 μL bead, 1 mg bead/mL cell culture medium) so that approximately one ferromagnetic bead was attached per cell. Cells with ferromagnetic beads were incubated for 15 min in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After that, cells were washed once with a cell culture medium to remove the unbounded ferromagnetic beads and added 2-mL fresh cell culture medium in 10 mM HEPES buffer for CHO DG44 cells and FluoroBrite DMEM (ThermoFisher) with 10% FBS (Gibco) in 10 mM HEPES buffer for MCF7 cells. Cell dishes were transferred in a stage top incubator (ONICS, Tokai, Japan) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 to start the SMI experiments for 0-min stress duration condition. After the 0-min stress SMI experiments, the glass-bottomed petri dish was placed into a MTC chamber surrounded by magnetic field coils. Cells were magnetized using a strong magnetizing pulse of ~1,000 Gauss for less than 1 ms along the Y direction and applied sinusoidal twisting field (50 Gauss, corresponding to 15 Pa stress, quantified using the magnetic moment and contact area of bead) (2) at a frequency of 0.3 Hz along the Z direction. The resulting twisted magnetic torque in the Z direction caused the in-plane rotation of the ~4-µm ferromagnetic bead attached to the integrins on a single-cell surface. The sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied either for 2 min or 10 min for the two cell lines.


### Single-Molecule Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging System.
  Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS), Förster Resonance Energy Transfer and Fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM-FRET) experiments were performed with an ISS Alba (Champaign, USA) confocal scanning time-resolved imaging system. A detailed description of the instruments and setup was described previously (37). In brief, a 488/561/640-nm picosecond (ps) pulsed laser with a 50-MHz repetition rate was used to excite EGFP/Atto-565/Atto-647N through apochromatic water immersion objective (60X, NA = 1.2, Olympus) or apochromatic silicone oil immersion objective (100X, NA = 1.35, Olympus). The same 60X/100X objectives were used to collect photons that were reflected by a dichroic filter (Chroma) and finally, photons were passed through a pinhole of 50 µm and a band-pass filter (Chroma) before reaching an avalanche photodiode (SPCM-AQRH-15, Excelitas). Detected photons were stored in TTTR (Time-Tagged-Time-Resolved) format.


### Fluorescence Correlation Spectroscopy (FCS) in Living Cells.
  CHO DG44 and MCF7 cells were seeded on a 35-mm glass-bottomed petri dish (GBD00001-200, Cell E&G). For CHO DG44 cells, after 24-h incubation, cell dishes were transferred to a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C. First, FCS data for 0-min stress condition were collected; next, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for either 2 min or 10 min after the addition of Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. For each stress condition, single-molecule imaging (SMI) for the FCS measurement was initiated in living cells 5 min after stress application was ceased. After the 2 min or 10-min SMI FCS measurement, CHO DG44 cell dishes were kept in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 1 h. After 1 h, SMI measurement for FCS was performed for 1 h post 2- or 10-min stress.

  However, for the drug (JIB-04 and chaetocin) (2)-treated CHO DG44 cells, after 24 h of incubation, cell dishes were transferred to a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C for FCS measurement in a cell culture medium. Cells were then treated with 3 µM JIB-04 or 0.3 µM chaetocin with a fresh cell culture medium for 4 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 4 h, cells were rinsed two times with a fresh medium, and 2 mL fresh cell culture medium was added for FCS measurements. After FCS measurements in drug-treated cells, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for 10 min after the addition of Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. FCS measurements were then performed in living cells, 5 min after stress application was ceased.

  For drug (Pitstop 2 or WGA) (4)-treated CHO DG44 cells, after 24 h of culture, the cells were treated with 30 µM Pitstop 2, or 50 µg/mL WGA, or both Pitstop 2 and WGA, or 0.1% DMSO for 30 min, or fresh medium at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell dishes treated with 0.1% DMSO and fresh medium were the controls for the drug-treated cells. After 30 min, cells were rinsed with fresh medium, and sinusoidal cyclic stress was applied (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) using MTC for 10 min after adding Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. FCS measurements were then performed on a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C in living cells 5 min and 60 min after stress application was ceased.

  FCS measurement for nucleoplasm-free GFP with an altered extracellular osmotic pressure (hyperosmotic or hypoosmotic pressure) was performed with two different hypertonic media, the addition of 0.1 M PEG 300 or the addition of 0.26 M PEG 300, two different hypotonic media, the addition of 30% DI water or the addition of 40% DI water, in CHO cell culture medium (25, 26). The control was the isotonic cell culture medium at an osmotic pressure of ~300 mOsm. Addition of 0.1 M PEG 300 to the cell culture medium applied an additional osmotic pressure of +110 mOsm, whereas adding 30% DI water to the medium created a hypotonic medium of −80 mOsm to swell CHO cells (25, 26). In a similar manner, the addition of 0.26 M PEG 300 to the cell culture medium applied an additional osmotic pressure of about +286 mOsm, whereas adding 40% DI water to the medium created a hypotonic medium of about −107 mOsm to swell CHO cells. After 24 h of culture, Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads were added to cells, and cells were rinsed two times with a fresh medium. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with isotonic, 0.1 M PEG 300, 0.26 M PEG 300, 30% DI water, and 40% DI water, for 30 min. After 30 min, cell dishes were transferred to a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C and FCS was measured for 0-min stress (no stress). Next, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for 10 min and FCS was measured in living cells for 5 min and 60 min after stress application was ceased.

  After seeding and incubating for 24 h, the modified cell culture medium with MCF7 cells was replaced with a fresh medium and transfected overnight with Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Around 550 ng plasmid [HP1α-GFP, Addgene (17652)] DNA was used to culture MCF7 cells on the glass-bottomed petri dish. After overnight transient transfection, the cell culture medium was removed and Arg-Gly-Asp-coated (~4 µm in diameter) ferromagnetic beads were added. After 15 min, the cell culture medium was removed and rinsed once with a fresh cell culture medium to remove the unbounded ferromagnetic bead. Finally, before SMI, cells were incubated with FluoroBrite DMEM (ThermoFisher) with 10% FBS (Gibco) in 10 mM HEPES buffer for 30 min. After 30 min, cell dishes were transferred to a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C. After FCS measurements at 0-min stress condition, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for either 2 min or 10 min. In a similar way to CHO DG44 cells, FCS measurements of MCF7 cells were conducted in living cells 5 min after stress application was ceased. After the 2 min or 10 min of FCS data acquisition, MCF7 cell dishes were kept in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 1 h. After 1 h, SMI measurement for FCS was performed for 1 h post 2- or 10-min stress.

  For FCS measurement in the two cell lines, at each position (measurements at 2 to 4 different positions were collected for each cell nucleus), data were collected for 100 s (five 20-s segments) to minimize the effect of photo-bleaching with ~0.2 µW at the back aperture of the 60X water immersion objective. The measurements in each cell nucleus at the periphery (<1.5 μm from the nuclear envelope) and the center (>1.5 μm from the of the nuclear envelope) were quantified using VistaVision Software (38).

  For FCS measurement, the autocorrelation function G(t) is defined as:[1]Gτ=<δFt·δFt+τ><δFt>2,

  where <F> is the average fluorescence intensity and δF(t)=F(t)-<F(t)>.

  For solution measurements, G(τ) was fitted with the following equation:[2]Gτ=γ<N>·11+ττD·11+1κ2·ττD,

  where  κ=z0ω0 , w0 is the lateral radii of the FCS detection volume, z0 is the axial radii of the FCS detection volume. τD=w02/4D  is the diffusion time of fluorescent molecules, where D is the diffusion coefficient, which reflects the transit dwell time in the effective detection volume ( V=π32z0w02 ) (39). The excitation volume in the femtoliter of the confocal profile was calibrated using a known concentration of rhodamine110 solution for 488-nm excitations and Atto565 for 561-nm excitations.

  For FCS measurement, kappa ( κ ) was calibrated and estimated using the known concentration of rhodamine 110 solutions at the beginning of each experiment day. <N> is the average number of fluorescent particles in the excitation–detection volume. Gamma ( γ ) is 0.3536 for 1-photon FCS under 488-nm excitation, N= 1, and diffusion coefficient (D) was estimated by fitting G ( τ ), with the input of kappa (κ). For FCS experiments with CHO DG44 and MCF7 cells, the acquired G(τ) was fitted with the standard equation for the free, 3D anomalous diffusion model:[3]Gτ=γ<N>·11+ττDα·11+1κ2·ττDα,

  where α is the degree of anomalous behavior and 0.5 ~ 0.8 indicates anomalous diffusion (10).


### Mean Square Displacement (MSD) in Living Cells.
  To measure the diffusivity of DHFR-GFP in CHO DG44 cells, each GFP spot was tracked with a time interval of ~0.3 s and overlapped the time interval to measure MSD and diffusion coefficient (D) for a total time interval of about 10 to 40 s to avoid photobleaching. After that, the diffusion coefficient D was computed from a best fit to the MSD versus Δt curves for the total time interval of DHFR-GFP. For MSD, the spontaneous movement of the GFP in the focal volume of CHO cells was measured in living cells 5 min after 0-min stress. Then sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for either 2 min or 10 min after adding Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. Again, diffusion in this location was measured in living cells 5 min after the application of stress ceased. After the 2-or 10-min GFP spot's spontaneous movement measurement, CHO DG44 cell dishes were kept in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 1 h. After 1 h, spontaneous movement of GFP was measured for 1 h post 2- or 10-min stress.

  For the drug (JIB-04 or chaetocin) (2)-treated CHO DG44 cells, after the GFP spot’s spontaneous movement measurement in cell culture medium only, cells were treated with 3 µM JIB-04 or 0.3 µM chaetocin with a fresh cell culture medium for 4 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 4 h, cells were rinsed two times with a fresh medium and 2 mL fresh cell culture medium was added to initiate GFP diffusion measurement. After the diffusion measurement in drug-treated cells, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for 10 min after the addition of Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. Diffusion measurement of the GFP in the same focal volume was measured in living cells 5 min after stress application was ceased.

  For drug (Pitstop 2 or WGA) (4)-treated CHO DG44 cells, after 24 h of culturing, the cells were treated with 30 µM Pitstop 2, or 50 µg/mL WGA, or both Pitstop 2 and WGA, or 0.1% DMSO, or fresh medium for 30 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell dishes treated with 0.1% DMSO and fresh medium were the controls for the drug-treated cells. After 30 min, cells were rinsed with fresh medium and sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied using MTC for 10 min after adding Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads. Diffusivity of the DHFR GFP in the same focal volume was measured in living cells 5 min and 60 min after stress application was ceased.


### Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging (FLIM)–Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET).
  For FLIM-FRET measurement, MCF7 cells were seeded on a 35-mm imprinted 50-µm cell location grid glass-bottomed petri dish (81148, Ibidi USA Incorporated, Madison, WI). After seeding and 24-h incubation, the modified DMEM cell culture medium was replaced with a fresh medium and transfected overnight with Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For FLIM-FRET measurement, around 500 ng plasmid DNA of HP1α-mCherry/MBD1-mCherry was used with 100 ng plasmid DNA of HP1α-GFP/MBD1EGFP to culture MCF7 cells on the glass-bottomed petri dish. HP1α-GFP (Addgene, 17652) and HP1α-mCherry (Addgene, 179703) were purchased from Addgene, while Dyche Mullins’s lab gifted MBD1-mCherry/MBD1E-GFP (Addgene, 58468). After overnight transient cotransfection, the cell culture medium was removed and Arg-Gly-Asp-coated ~4-µm ferromagnetic beads were added to the fresh cell culture medium. After 15 min, the cell culture medium was removed and rinsed once with a fresh cell culture medium to remove the unbounded ferromagnetic bead. Finally, before FLIM-FRET imaging, cells were incubated with FluoroBrite DMEM (ThermoFisher) with 10% FBS (Gibco) in 10 mM HEPES buffer for 30 min. After 30 min, cell dishes were transferred in a stage top incubator (ONICS, tokai, Japan) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Each cell was tracked using a 35-mm imprinted 50-µm cell location-grided glass-bottomed petri dish for 0-min stress conditions. After measurement of lifetime due to FRET in live cells (HP1α-GFP/mCherry and MBD1-EGFP/ mCherry) for 0-min stress condition, sinusoidal cyclic stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz) was applied for 10 min using MTC. Lifetime (ns) for the same live cells was measured 5 min after stress application ceased. After the FLIM-FRET measurement, cell dishes were kept in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Similarly, after 1 h post 10-min stress, lifetime (ns) measurements for the same cells in FRET conditions were quantified. For FRET measurement, the negative control was cells transfected only with HP1α-GFP or MBD1E-GFP with a 0-min stress (no stress), 5 min after 10-min stress (15 Pa at 0.3 Hz), and 1 h post 10-min stress. For positive control FRET quantification, lifetime (ns) of HP1α-GFP/mCherry cotransfected cells was measured, and then cells were treated with 0.5 µM Trichostatin A, 3.0 µM JIB-04, or 0.3 µM Chaetocin for 4 h in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and measured the lifetime (ns) of the same tracked MCF7 cells.

  FRET was obtained to assess the heterochromatin condensation state between HP1α-GFP/mCherry and MBD1-EGFP/mCherry (40). The FRET efficiency (η) was quantified using the following equation (27):[4]η=1−τdaτd,

  where τda is the lifetime (ns) of dual transient transfected HP1α-GFP/mCherry or MBD1-EGFP/mCherry cells, and τd  is the lifetime (ns) of transient transfected HP1α-GFP or MBD1-EGFP cells.


### Statistical Analysis.
  Two-tailed Student’s t test and the Mann–Whitney test were used for statistical analysis between two independent groups, and multiple comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni corrections when appropriate.
