# Dissecting the structural heterogeneity of proteins by native mass spectrometry


## Abstract
  A single gene yields many forms of proteins via combinations of posttranscriptional/posttranslational modifications. Proteins also fold into higher‐order structures and interact with other molecules. The combined molecular diversity leads to the heterogeneity of proteins that manifests as distinct phenotypes. Structural biology has generated vast amounts of data, effectively enabling accurate structural prediction by computational methods. However, structures are often obtained heterologously under homogeneous states in vitro. The lack of native heterogeneity under cellular context creates challenges in precisely connecting the structural data to phenotypes. Mass spectrometry (MS) based proteomics methods can profile proteome composition of complex biological samples. Most MS methods follow the “bottom‐up” approach, which denatures and digests proteins into short peptide fragments for ease of detection. Coupled with chemical biology approaches, higher‐order structures can be probed via incorporation of covalent labels on native proteins that are maintained at the peptide level. Alternatively, native MS follows the “top‐down” approach and directly analyzes intact proteins under nondenaturing conditions. Various tandem MS activation methods can dissect the intact proteins for in‐depth structural elucidation. Herein, we review recent native MS applications for characterizing heterogeneous samples, including proteins binding to mixtures of ligands, homo/hetero‐complexes with varying stoichiometry, intrinsically disordered proteins with dynamic conformations, glycoprotein complexes with mixed modification states, and active membrane protein complexes in near‐native membrane environments. We summarize the benefits, challenges, and ongoing developments in native MS, with the hope to demonstrate an emerging technology that complements other tools by filling the knowledge gaps in understanding the molecular heterogeneity of proteins.


## INTRODUCTION
  Biological systems are inherently complex networks of interconnected pathways involving different types of molecules. Among these molecules, proteins are key players that carry out important biological functions ranging from performing biochemical reactions to serving as physical scaffolds. Characterizing their molecular structures is essential for understanding their functions. Protein structural studies have enabled the bioengineering of desired protein products for pharmaceutical, food, and cosmetic industries (Li et al., 2020), and exciting developments in computational methods such as AlphaFold have enabled the prediction of structures from protein sequences (Jumper et al., 2021). These advances are critical to describe biological systems more precisely and to support the well‐informed design of traits for engineered organisms.

  Yet despite these advancements, technological gaps still exist in measurement science for heterogeneous “real‐world” systems. Structural determination methods such as x‐ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and cryo‐electron microscopy (cryo‐EM) often require highly purified and homogeneous proteins to obtain high‐resolution structural information. Proteins produced under “artificial” conditions may also lack native traits such as posttranslational modifications (PTMs) or interacting molecules. Although various global proteome‐level “omics” measurements under native biological context are possible, most methods offer minimal information on higher‐order molecular structure and instead primarily focus on detection and quantitation, regardless of functional states.

  Advances in mass spectrometry (MS) have addressed some of these shortcomings through significantly improved throughput and accessibility, and facile integration with chemical biology tools has expanded its applications. Chemical crosslinking, footprinting, limited proteolysis, hydrogen‐deuterium exchange, and other related methods that were once almost exclusively used for purified protein samples have been successfully applied to studies of protein structure and function at the proteome scale (Chavez et al., 2022; James et al., 2022; Mateus et al., 2016; McKenzie‐Coe et al., 2022; Petrotchenko & Borchers, 2022; Piersimoni et al., 2022; Schopper et al., 2017; Wheat et al., 2021). These “structural proteomics” methods introduce structure‐dependent changes in proteins in their native states (e.g., via chemical labeling or proteolysis) that can then be measured by MS (Figure 1). Most workflows are based on the “bottom‐up” proteomics approach, which involves the enzymatic digestion of proteins into smaller peptides for the ease of detection. Although this approach is robust and commonly employed, the measurement of proteins at the peptide level has limitations. For example, stoichiometry of protomers and co‐occurring PTMs can be difficult to define because proteins are indirectly inferred from small peptide fragments after proteolysis. Not detecting a peptide fragment does not necessarily confirm its absence due to possible missing data in the analysis. Furthermore, differentiating protein homologs and variants with high sequence identity is also challenging at the peptide level, especially for complex mixtures.

  The “top‐down” approach, on the other hand, bypasses the proteolytic step and directly measures intact proteins and their proteoforms. Herein, we use the term proteoform (Smith et al., 2013) to describe all the different forms that a protein produced from a single gene may take, including PTMs and genetic variations. Furthermore, instrument hardware improvements have expanded the upper mass range to MDa, novel ionization techniques and improvements to mass analyzers have improved sensitivity, and nondenaturing conditions enable noncovalent protein complexes to be directly transferred into the gas phase for “native MS.” Native MS is well suited to the analysis of protein complexes and can characterize stoichiometry, protein–ligand interactions, and conformation, especially if coupled to an orthogonal method such as ion mobility (IM). Native MS also complements computational biology and can be used to elucidate and confirm structural models and mechanisms. Finally, with additional tandem MS activation methods, both known and unknown protein complexes can be dissected for more in‐depth structural interrogation (Lermyte et al., 2019). For example, collision‐induced dissociation (CID), which is the most common activation method widely available in many commercial mass spectrometers, is capable of both disrupting noncovalent interactions and breaking covalent backbones (Lantz et al., 2022). Alternative methods include surface‐induced dissociation (SID) which breaks noncovalent bonds by accelerating analytes toward a surface (Snyder et al., 2022), electron‐based techniques such as electron capture dissociation (ECD) and electron transfer dissociation (ETD) which generate backbone cleavage through the transfer of electrons (Lermyte et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018a), and photodissociation techniques such as infrared multiphoton dissociation (IRMPD) and ultraviolet photodissociation (UVPD) which generate fragments through the absorption of photons (Brodbelt et al., 2020; Li et al., 2018a). Each method has its own selectivity in generating structure‐informative fragments from protein complexes and can be used individually or in combination. Not all methods are readily accessible in commercial instruments and may require custom modification. For further reading on technical and instrumental advances in all areas of native MS, we refer the reader to the recent special issue of Chemical Reviews (Bennett et al., 2022; Jarrold, 2022; Largy et al., 2022; Rogawski & Sharon, 2022; Rolland & Prell, 2022; Snyder et al., 2022; Tamara et al., 2022; Vallejo et al., 2022). Here, we focus on the contributions and prospects of native MS for protein science in resolving heterogeneity that a single analytical method struggles to accomplish.


## BENEFITS AND CHALLENGES OF NATIVE MS FOR PROTEIN STRUCTURAL CHARACTERIZATION
  During a typical native MS experiment, proteins are electrosprayed from aqueous solution to yield desolvated protein ions into the mass spectrometer (Fenn et al., 1989). Because gas‐phase ions are analyzed in the absence of bulk solvent, it may seem nonintuitive to use native MS for structural characterization of intact proteins and complexes. However, the solvent evaporation process is endothermic, and proteins are believed to be kinetically trapped in their native‐like states without significant activation, that is, the proteins remain intact, folded, and still associated with any noncovalent interacting molecules (Raab et al., 2021). Indeed, strong experimental evidence supporting this idea has been provided by IM and soft‐landing experiments. IM is a gas‐phase separation technique based on ion size and charge (Clemmer & Jarrold, 1997) that is used to calculate the collisional cross‐section (CCS), which is essentially a measure of the shape of the ions in the gas phase and provides a second dimension of separation based on conformation. IM permits the separation of isomers, which is difficult to achieve by MS alone. Many reports have shown that CCSs from IM correlate highly to calculated values using known high‐resolution structures, with minor structural rearrangement and/or collapse of side chains expected (Breuker & McLafferty, 2008). Furthermore, changing solution conditions induces changes that can be measured using native MS, suggesting that gas‐phase protein ions retain the memory of their solution structures (El‐Baba et al., 2017; Hudgins et al., 1997). In soft‐landing experiments, protein ions generated under native MS conditions are gently deposited onto surface targets for further investigation. Early studies demonstrated that virus particles and proteins could be soft‐landed and still retain their activities (Gologan et al., 2004; Mazzei et al., 2008; Siuzdak et al., 1996). More recent results from electron microscopy, atomic force microscopy, and electron holography showed that soft‐landed proteins largely retain their native structures after going through the ionization, desolvation, and landing process (Benesch et al., 2010; Esser, Bohning, Fremdling, Agasid, et al., 2022; Esser, Bohning, Fremdling, Bharat, et al., 2022; Fremdling et al., 2022; Mikhailov et al., 2014; Ochner et al., 2021; Westphall et al., 2022). These data provide strong evidence that the native‐like structures of proteins are preserved in native MS experiments.

  The preservation and measurement of proteins in their native‐like states is a significant advantage of native MS, but another attractive benefit to biochemists is the high resolution that can be used to confidently determine stoichiometry and PTMs, especially for samples that are heterogeneous or not highly purified. Whereas most traditional bulk measurements rely on the combined signal from the whole ensemble—which can be distorted by interference in the sample—native MS can be used to readily define and quantify the stoichiometry and PTMs of mixtures of complexes at the proteoform level, given sufficient differences in mass. Additionally, native MS can be performed with reasonably low analyte concentrations of μM or lower. Isolation of target species by their masses also opens up new avenues for tandem MS experiments for the identification of multiprotein complexes (Gault et al., 2020; Skinner et al., 2018) and structural elucidation (Li et al., 2018b; Zhou et al., 2020). The resolving power of native MS can be further increased by incorporation of online orthogonal separation techniques such as IM, capillary electrophoresis, and chromatography.

  However, like every technique, native MS is not without limitations. Complete removal of solvent can cause significant structural rearrangements for certain proteins. This effect has been observed for the “Y” shaped IgG, which adopts a collapsed non‐native structure in the gas phase (Hansen et al., 2018). Nevertheless, IgGs with minor structural differences have displayed different gas‐phase unfolding behaviors (Dixit et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2015), implying an indirect link between solution and gas‐phase structures. Notably, hydrophobic interactions are believed to be weakened in the absence of bulk solvent, whereas electrostatic interactions are strengthened (Bich et al., 2010; Breuker et al., 2011; Sharon & Robinson, 2011). However, this does not mean that hydrophobic interactions cannot be detected in native MS. As demonstrated by numerous studies, native MS has been used successfully for the analysis of membrane protein complexes (Keener et al., 2020; Laganowsky et al., 2013; Sharon & Robinson, 2011), although special care must be taken when correlating observations in the gas phase to solution phase structure. Other sample preparation conditions and instrument settings such as desolvation and ion transfer parameters must also be carefully considered because suboptimal conditions can induce unfolding and structural rearrangement. Structural disruptions can also arise from buffer exchange into MS‐compatible volatile salt solutions like ammonium acetate (Konermann, 2017), which are often required to decrease nonspecific adducts to achieve the highest possible mass resolution. For instance, protein‐nucleotide complexes and membrane protein complexes require high salt and lipid content to stabilize the complexes, respectively.

  Fortunately, advances and innovation continue to expand the possibilities for native MS analysis of complex and biologically relevant analytes. Complexes can be stabilized with additives, metal ions, cofactors, and ligands. To minimize the interference of nonvolatile solution components, smaller emitters that reduce adducts in electrospray ionization (ESI) have been developed (Susa et al., 2018; Xia et al., 2019). New ionization methods under active development (Li, Allen, et al., 2022; Peetz et al., 2019) may also bring new possibilities for studying samples under more physiological conditions. In summary, sample and experimental conditions must be carefully optimized to best preserve native‐like structures, and ongoing developments in the native MS community are tackling many of the remaining challenges, as demonstrated by the examples discussed below.


## COMPLEMENTING EXISTING STRUCTURAL BIOLOGY TOOLS WITH FAST SPEED
  Native MS has been making critical contributions to structural biology methods since its early development. Protein complexes (~50‐200 kDa) that are recalcitrant to crystallography, too big for NMR, or too small/heterogeneous for cryo‐EM have been characterized by native MS (Romano et al., 2017; Song et al., 2015). Native MS is also well suited for identifying bound ligands/lipids that cannot be easily confirmed in atomic structures with limited resolution. Even for complexes that are compatible with classical structural biology methods, the speed of native MS makes it an ideal prescreening tool before high‐resolution determination. For example, high‐quality sample preparation remains a limiting step in single‐particle cryo‐EM, despite its tremendous power in structural biology (Passmore, 2016). Early‐stage screening is critical to ensure sample purity and homogeneity. The high resolving power of native MS serves as an effective time‐saving strategy for streamlined assessment of sample quality for cryo‐EM (Olinares et al., 2021), including membrane protein complexes (Kuhlen et al., 2018) and heterogeneous/dynamic assemblies (Snijder et al., 2017). More excitingly, the mass selectivity of native MS can potentially be used for cryo‐EM sample preparation by isolating highly homogeneous populations and soft‐landing them directly on cryo‐EM grids for imaging, as demonstrated by several proof‐of‐principle studies mentioned earlier (Benesch et al., 2010; Esser, Bohning, Fremdling, Agasid, et al., 2022; Esser, Bohning, Fremdling, Bharat, et al., 2022; Fremdling et al., 2022; Mikhailov et al., 2014; Westphall et al., 2022).

  In addition to screening proteins by themselves, native MS has been used to determine binding partners (e.g., ligands, lipids, metals) for selected proteins (Bennett et al., 2022), and especially for screening inhibitors for enzymes in applications such as drug discovery. High throughput screening (HTS) methods such as fluorescence spectroscopy, isothermal titration calorimetry, thermal shift assay, and surface plasmon resonance are often used for large‐volume screening of protein‐ligand interactions (Gavriilidou et al., 2022), but some artifacts exist (Yang et al., 2020). For example, aggregation, nonspecific binding, and compound reactivity complicate many assay results (Jadhav et al., 2010). In fluorescence‐based methods, autofluorescence can lead to false positives (Imbert et al., 2007; Jadhav et al., 2010), while other methods such as reporter gene assays are prone to off‐target responses (Crisman et al., 2007). Additionally, most HTS methods do not directly provide stoichiometry measurement. Native MS thus offers a fast, orthogonal approach that not only provides information on stoichiometry, but can verify the physical interaction between protein and ligand without extra sample requirements. Binding affinity (K d) can also be estimated based on the relative intensities of the apo versus holo peak in the spectrum if the ligand concentration is known. In addition to confirming the physical binding of ligands, native MS can guide the selection of optimal conditions for desired binding stoichiometry to generate homogeneous populations for high‐resolution structure determination. A classic example is the ammonia channel AmtB, which undergoes a conformational change in response to lipid binding. Native MS was used to determine the optimal detergent and lipid composition required to obtain the first high‐resolution x‐ray crystallography structure of AmtB bound to lipids (Laganowsky et al., 2014). Recent applications of native MS have also supported the selection of COVID‐19 therapeutics (Choi et al., 2021; Littler et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2020; Sacco et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2022), and the evaluation of allosteric modulation of G‐protein coupled receptors, which are highly important membrane protein drug targets (Yen et al., 2022).

  A benefit unique to native MS is the ability to screen protein binding against a mixture of ligands simultaneously, which is possible because each bound ligand can be resolved by their different masses and further identified via tandem MS (Figure 2). This approach has been demonstrated for screening protein binding to natural products (Gu et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2021), glycan libraries (Park, Jung, et al., 2020), and peptide substrates of ubiquitin ligases (Canzani et al., 2019). Alternatively, the holo protein complexes can be isolated first and then activated to release the bound ligands for identification. This tandem MS method has different implementations referred to as “catch and release” (Zhang et al., 2012) or “collision‐induced affinity selection” (Mak et al., 2022). Detecting interactions between metabolites and proteins in a heterogeneous system opens avenues to functionally annotate unknown metabolites. A recent study demonstrated this by separating a crude metabolite extract from a cyanobacterial biofilm using liquid chromatography, followed by online mixing with a target protein, chymotrypsin, post‐column. The protein–metabolite interactions were then analyzed using native MS, which was used to identify nanomolar affinity chymotrypsin inhibitors (Reher et al., 2022). Currently, protein–metabolite interactions are often identified indirectly at the metabolite level after affinity‐based enrichment using target proteins (Hou et al., 2020). In contrast, native MS offers the direct detection of the interaction and stoichiometry of binding without the need for laborious enrichment steps. Additionally, more information regarding higher‐order structure and oligomeric states can be obtained for improved mechanistic understanding of the interactions, especially for heterocomplexes. For example, Native MS and IM were used to demonstrate that the decarboxylase enzyme Fdc1 exists as a mixture of monomer and dimer in the absence of its cofactor, prFMN. However, upon binding prFMN, Fdc1 exists solely as a dimer, and adopts a more compact structure than its apo form (Beveridge et al., 2016). Although some advanced experiments highlighted in this review still involve significant manual steps and/or long data acquisition times, recent developments have established automated native MS workflows (Busch et al., 2021; Hecht, Obiorah, et al., 2022; Orton et al., 2018; Park, Winton, et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2019; VanAernum et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2003), some of which are commercialized, for high throughput stoichiometry measurement that will complement existing structural biology tools.


## RESOLVING COMPOSITIONAL HETEROGENEITY CHALLENGING FOR OTHER METHODS
  Compositional heterogeneity poses a problem that traditional structural biology methods struggle to overcome. Whether this heterogeneity stems from a complex's variable stoichiometry or the presence of heterocomplexes composed of homologous subunits, the ability to accurately resolve small structural differences is critical for understanding their contributions to protein function. Thanks to improvements in MS instrumentation, various groups have used ultrahigh mass range Orbitrap instruments to push the limits of accurate high‐resolution mass analysis up to 50,000 m/z (Tamara et al., 2022). Native MS routinely achieves high resolving power for large analytes, enabling stoichiometry measurements of mixed populations of protein–ligand, and protein–protein complexes. One example is tryptophan binding to the tryptophan RNA‐binding attenuation protein (TRAP) complex from Bacillus. Different TRAP homologs form stable ring structures of homo‐11mers, 12mers, or a mixture of both (Li, Norris, et al., 2022; McCammon et al., 2004). Bulk solution measurements could not resolve the heterogeneous distribution of the ligand binding stoichiometry to the homo‐protein complexes, but native MS accomplished this task and revealed the allosteric cooperativity mechanism (Holmquist et al., 2020; Li, Norris, et al., 2022). As with tryptophan binding, heterogeneous stoichiometry is also observed when TRAP is complexed with its regulator, anti‐TRAP. Early x‐ray structures only captured the subpopulation, and ultracentrifugation did not confidently resolve the mixture of species (Akashi et al., 2009; Ihms et al., 2014). However, native MS was able to resolve the different stoichiometries of TRAP: anti‐TRAP complexes (e.g., 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and also substoichiometric ratios 2:1, 3:1, etc.) to help define the molecular mechanism of interaction. A similar native MS application to resolve a mixed population of protein complexes was reported for the E. coli chaperonin GroEL–GroES complex. GroEL is a tetradecameric complex that binds to the co‐chaperonin GroES to ensure the proper folding of substrate proteins. Data obtained using variable‐temperature ESI (vT‐ESI) native MS led to the discovery of previously unknown stoichiometries for the GroEL–GroES complex in response to variations in temperature and ATP and Mg2+ concentrations (Walker et al., 2022).

  Another example of a homocomplex with varying stoichiometry is the mammalian chaperone α‐crystallin (Aquilina et al., 2003). The homocomplex consists mainly of 24–33 subunits, but also shows oligomers with as few as 10 subunits and as many as 40. The high heterogeneity initially made it impossible to fully resolve the different stoichiometry, even by native MS directly. However, by activating all the unresolved complexes via collision with gas, the monomers were dissociated from the mixed population of n‐mer homocomplexes (n = 24–33), yielding a distribution of stripped complexes (n − 1, n − 2, n − 3 mers) over a much broader range of m/z with decreased spectral overlap. This then allowed for the accurate quantitation of each species in the mixture. Other follow‐up studies demonstrated that polydispersity was driven by the dynamics and modifications of monomers (Baldwin et al., 2011; Benesch et al., 2008). The conclusions were largely supported by the thermodynamic and kinetic parameters extracted from native MS data, where the assembly states could be defined under different solution conditions. The same methodology was also applied to resolve polydisperse complexes between small heat‐shock proteins and their targets, where the dissociation by CID was essential to resolve the overlapping species in the mixture (Benesch, 2009; Stengel et al., 2010).

  Heterocomplexes of homologous subunits represent another type of compositional heterogeneity. Isoforms or close homologs of proteins may be exchangeable while maintaining an overall conserved scaffold. These homologs can substitute their canonical counterparts to carry out unique functions in specific cells, tissue types, and conditions. Well‐known examples include histone variants in chromosomes/nucleosomes (Espiritu et al., 2021), proteasomes (Kahne & Darwin, 2021; Vimer, Ben‐Nissan, Morgenstern, et al., 2020), and Caseinolytic protease complex (Bouchnak & van Wijk, 2021; Hall et al., 2017; Kahne & Darwin, 2021). Recently discovered “pseudoenzymes”—inactive enzyme homologs that evolved to take regulatory roles and other diverted functions—are another example (Ribeiro et al., 2019). Human epidermal growth factor receptor is an important drug target for cancer therapeutics, and its pseudoenzyme homologs have noncanonical functions that contribute to resistance to treatment (Eyers & Murphy, 2016; Sigismund et al., 2018). The structural similarity of these close homologs makes them difficult to differentiate when present in heterocomplexes.

  The plant pseudoenzyme—enzyme complex PDX1.2‐PDX1.3 is an example where neither X‐ray crystallography nor cryo‐EM was able to resolve the high‐resolution structure of the heterocomplexes (Novikova et al., 2021; Robinson et al., 2019). However, native MS of the heterocomplexes readily revealed that the stoichiometry is tunable depending on the availability of each subunit during protein synthesis. The high resolving power of MS allowed complexes with a single stoichiometry to be isolated and dissected into constituent subcomplexes, which revealed symmetry in the assembly (Figure 3). Similar experiments have been demonstrated on proteasome complexes, which contain multiple homologs and thus high complexity (Vimer, Ben‐Nissan, Morgenstern, et al., 2020). The ability to mass‐resolve structurally similar subunits offers the unique opportunity to study hetero‐assemblies that are challenging to resolve by other techniques.


## QUANTIFYING THE CONFORMATIONAL HETEROGENEITY OF “STRUCTURELESS” PROTEINS
  Protein dynamics is critical for protein function, but due to conformational heterogeneity, transient interactions and structures are difficult to capture with conventional structural biology methods. However, even more difficult to capture are the structures and interactions of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs; Shea et al., 2021) which lack uniquely defined structures. Nevertheless, a recent estimation showed that approximately 30% of sequences in the human proteome are likely IDP/IDRs (Ruff & Pappu, 2021) that are involved in a variety of crucial biological functions. IDPs are of great interest in the neurodegenerative disease field due to their connection with amyloid formation (Bleiholder et al., 2011; Mitra, 2019), but also have roles extending beyond human health research. It was recently discovered that IDPs participate in hydration‐dependent phase separation during plant seed germination (Dorone et al., 2021). Methods for investigating IDP structures and interactions are therefore necessary to gain a more complete understanding of their functions. For example, physics‐based models have linked the disordered nature of IDPs to potentially tunable behaviors including dynamic binding modes, formatting of hydrogels, and liquid–liquid phase separation leading to membraneless organelles (Shea et al., 2021; Uversky, 2019). Additionally, because IDP/IDRs are better defined as ensembles of structures instead of single structures, quantitative measures of the mixed population of conformational states are also important. Native MS, often coupled with IM, provides unique ways to define the full conformational landscape (Mitra, 2019; Santambrogio et al., 2022; Stuchfield & Barran, 2018).

  A simple measure of the degree of disorder is obtained from the charge state distribution of a native MS spectrum. During the ESI process, charges are deposited on analyte ions as they are transferred from the liquid phase to the gas phase. Because the analyte may pick up several different charges, the resulting m/z spectrum is a series of peaks, referred to as the charge state distribution. Generally, a folded protein will pick up fewer charges than when it is denatured, therefore IDPs generally show broader (or even multimodal) distributions than well‐structured proteins, and the addition of IM provides direct measurement of conformations. These experimental data provide descriptors like CCSs and solvent‐accessible surface areas (SASAs) that can be compared to experimental or predicted structural models to quantify the contribution of different states in the sample (Figure 4a). As an example, IM–MS elucidated the ligand‐induced conformational changes of the complex formed between the leucine zipper domains from the transcription factor c‐MYC‐Zip, an IDP, and its cofactor MAX‐Zip (Harvey et al., 2012). In the absence of the ligand, the heterodimeric c‐MYC‐Zip: MAX‐Zip complex exists in two conformations, one extended and one compact. In the presence of the ligand, however, the formation of the compact complex is inhibited. The experimental CCSs were then compared with those calculated from predicted or NMR structures for assignments (Figure 4b). An earlier study used a similar approach to individually resolve the CCSs of soluble intermediates of amyloid fibril‐forming peptides, revealing the mechanism on the path to aggregation (Bleiholder et al., 2011). Native MS and IM also detect conformational changes in response to solution conditions like ionic strength, offering critical insights to the mechanisms that control their structures (Beveridge et al., 2019). However, it is cautioned that CCS and SASA data from native MS are also sensitive to solution and ESI conditions (Lermyte et al., 2021; Natalello et al., 2017). Just like all native MS experiments, careful optimization as informed by fundamental studies is necessary. Recent studies have also utilized native top‐down to identify metal/ligand binding sites of α‐synuclein and tau by the measurable changes in covalent backbone fragmentation induced by binding (Nshanian et al., 2019; Wongkongkathep et al., 2018). Likewise, changes in the dissociation behavior of noncovalent interactions revealed how the IDR in a bacterial RNA‐binding protein Hfq regulated the stability of the complex in response to substrate binding (Sarni et al., 2022). Such structural information, albeit with low resolution, can be extremely valuable to support computational models to elucidate the mechanisms.

  Another area native MS could contribute to IDPs/IDRs is the ability to probe evolving structures in real‐time under stress (e.g., heat, denaturant) or during a reaction. Native MS data have already been used to study detailed kinetics and thermodynamics of protein unfolding for various systems such as proteins (Benesch et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2011; Woodall et al., 2020), protein–ligand/metal complexes (Daneshfar et al., 2004; Thangaraj et al., 2019), and DNA complexes (Marchand et al., 2018). Solution heating is typically achieved via temperature control in the ESI source (Laganowsky et al., 2022), and gas phase heating can be done via ion activation (Dixit et al., 2018; Donor et al., 2019). Native MS, among other structural MS methods such as crosslinking, HDX, foot‐printing, and limited proteolysis, will undoubtedly contribute to the structural dynamics of IDPs/IDRs that are difficult for conventional methods (Beveridge & Calabrese, 2021; Mitra, 2021).


## INTERROGATING THE HETEROGENEITY OF PTMS ON ENDOGENOUS PROTEINS
  The examples discussed so far have almost exclusively used highly homogeneous recombinant proteins, which are ideal samples for structural biology studies. However, endogenous proteins in vivo are in a complex and crowded environment, interacting with many other molecules, and can be in different states from when they are in isolation. Many proteins have PTMs (phosphorylation, acetylation, glycosylation, etc.) present at varying degrees, leading to heterogeneous proteoforms with distinct functions, but unfortunately, these are not directly accessible by genomics and transcriptomics data. Homogeneous samples with site‐specific PTMs are also not trivial to produce for classical structural biology analysis. Fortunately, native MS can be used to study proteins with endogenous PTMs, and MS‐based proteomics methods have made significant contributions for PTM studies.

  Glycosylation is a very important type of PTM in molecular recognition. For example, glycosylation on the spike protein in SARS‐CoV‐2 is related to how the virus escapes the host immune system, and has been a popular area of research for glycoproteomics on both the peptide and intact protein level (Grant et al., 2020; Gstottner et al., 2021; Miller, Barnes, et al., 2021; Roberts et al., 2021; Shajahan et al., 2020; Watanabe et al., 2020; Wilson et al., 2022). Glycosylation on monoclonal antibodies and protein therapeutics affects how they interact with the host immune system, and needs to be carefully controlled for minimizing adverse effects on patients (Sha et al., 2016). However, glycosylation is challenging to study due to the high diversity of chemical structures, and often cannot be experimentally defined in high‐resolution structures. Instead, glycosylation must be modeled computationally (Fadda, 2022). Glycan heterogeneity comes from multiple levels, including site localization in the protein sequence and the type of glycan on each site. Such information can be captured using current peptide‐based proteomics methods with reasonably high confidence and coverage, and individual glycoforms of a glycoprotein can be mass resolved and quantified at the intact protein level (Yang et al., 2016). However, different methods can have unique detection biases, thus integrated analysis using multiple methods is beneficial (Caval et al., 2021).

  Native MS can also study binding between glycoproteins. Native MS and glycoproteomics methods were recently used to show the different selectivity of N‐glycans using two different lectins (Wu et al., 2019). The technique is also being used to directly study glycoprotein complexes to resolve the contribution of individual glycoforms to binding affinities (Hecht, Mehta, et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2022; Wu & Robinson, 2022). Figure 5 describes an application to define the composition of the biopharmaceutical follitropin, a heterodimer of two glycosylated subunits. Sialic acids on the native proteins were first removed enzymatically to simplify the spectra. Multi‐stage activation was used to profile the glycosylation content on each subunit, and also the glycan structures. Combined with bottom‐up proteomics data and molecular dynamics, the N‐glycan at Asn52 of the α subunit was proposed to be important for the dimer assembly.

  Currently, a major limitation for native MS and MS‐based methods is the lack of information on glycan structural isomers, which may improve via incorporation of a structural based separation such as IM (Manz & Pagel, 2018). Deglycosylation enzymes have also been used to selectively cleave certain glycan moieties to study their specific structural and functional contributions (Chen et al., 2021). The controlled deglycosylation approach has been commonly performed for quality control applications in biopharma (Liu & Schulz, 2021). One may wonder if there is a limit for resolving the heterogeneity of PTMs on intact proteins/complexes by native MS methods, as complexity rapidly expands by multiplying the heterogeneity at different levels. Recent improvements to instrumentation have allowed easier access for high‐resolution measurement of high‐mass ions. On the mass analyzer side, charge detection MS (CDMS) is an attractive new development. CDMS can be used to determine the masses of large, heterogeneous samples that cannot be analyzed via traditional MS techniques. CDMS simultaneously measures the charge and the mass‐to‐charge ratio of single ions, therefore alleviating the frequent problem of overlapping charge states in heterogeneous samples that can prevent accurate deconvolution of m/z spectra (Jarrold, 2022), especially for macromolecules/assemblies such as the SARS‐Cov‐2 spike glycoprotein complex (Miller, Barnes, et al., 2021), intact viral particles (e.g., adeno‐associated virus as gene‐therapy vectors) (Ebberink et al., 2022; Miller, Bond, et al., 2021), exosomes (Brown et al., 2020), and lipoproteins (Lutomski et al., 2018). However, intact mass measurement alone provides limited information. For example, synthetic nucleosomes of highly homogenous recombinant histones and DNAs produced well‐resolved native MS spectra (Ma, 2014; Saikusa et al., 2018), but the mass distribution of endogenously purified intact nucleosomes by CDMS was too broad to precisely define its composition, and multi‐stage activation was necessary to dissociate the constituents for structural details (Schachner et al., 2021). A similar situation applies to heavily glycosylated complexes. As the molecular weight increases, individual glycoforms become harder to resolve. Native top‐down is one solution to dissect the intact complexes into smaller constituents that can be better resolved by MS, and orthogonal separation techniques (Cramer et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2018) are also essential to reduce the heterogeneity before MS. Other methods to deal with heterogeneity utilize gas‐phase chemistry to further fractionate the subpopulations, or dilute the heterogeneity by increasing their separation in m/z space (Chen, Dai, et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2021). While many of these methods are still in the research stage, they will undoubtedly become more accessible as the techniques mature.


## PROBING ENDOGENOUS PROTEIN COMPLEXES IN (NEAR) NATIVE ENVIRONMENTS
  The ability of native MS to resolve heterogeneity introduces perhaps the most exciting direction for probing protein complexes closer to their cellular states providing critical information to complement structural biology data in isolated states. Direct native MS on overproduced proteins in crude mixtures without extensive purification was shown to provide rapid feedback to optimize protein expression and harvest time (Vimer, Ben‐Nissan, & Sharon, 2020). Microfluidics‐based devices have been used to directly sample and clean up proteins/peptides from cell lysate to study metal binding (Hou et al., 2022). In addition, in situ sampling methods such as desorption electrospray ionization (DESI; Ambrose et al., 2017; Sisley et al., 2022), liquid extraction surface analysis (Griffiths et al., 2019; Martin et al., 2015), solid‐substrate ESI (Hu & Yao, 2018), and rapid evaporative ionization mass spectrometry allow direct extraction of proteins and protein complexes under nondenaturing conditions in a spatially resolved manner. Although the resolution is currently limited to hundreds of μm, future development of integrated instrumentation with cryo‐electron microscopy and tomography may push the limit for visualizing proteins in the cellular context (Klykov et al., 2022; Su et al., 2021).

  Another inspirational direction is the investigation of membrane protein interactions in native or near‐native environments—a task that has been traditionally difficult to accomplish. Unlike soluble proteins, the lipid environment plays a critical role in maintaining membrane protein structure and function, yet conventional techniques require the removal of the protein from this environment. Detergents are often used to solubilize these proteins, but they can disrupt critical protein–lipid interactions and even destabilize complexes. However, detergent‐free, native MS‐compatible cassettes such as amphipols, nanodiscs, and styrene‐maleic acid‐lipid particles have enabled the preservation of these noncovalent interactions, thus permitting the analysis of drug and ligand binding, lipid and protein interactions, and the determination of oligomeric state and stoichiometry of membrane proteins (Chorev et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2022; Marty et al., 2016; Townsend et al., 2021).

  These methodological advances have enabled new ways to “visualize” molecular pathways. For example, native MS was employed to study the rhodopsin receptor signaling cascade directly from native membranes in a mass spectrometer (Figure 6) (Chen, Getter, et al., 2022). Essentially, native membranes were homogenized into vesicles, directly transferred into the mass spectrometer via ESI, and then proteins were released by activation for characterizing binding among different proteins and lipids. An earlier study also showed how complexes directly released from native membranes could present different stoichiometry from isolated structures reported by crystal structures (Chorev et al., 2018). The ability to determine lipid binding on membrane complexes can be achieved using multi‐stage activation that releases bound lipids from proteins to be further fragmented for identification (Gault et al., 2020). Continued improvements to instrumental methods that activate organelles and large heterogeneous structures into smaller, resolvable complexes will be essential to increase the complexity native MS can handle.


## OUTLOOK FOR MS‐BASED “NATIVE PROTEOMICS”
  Native MS has evolved significantly from the early demonstrations that used ESI to investigate model protein complexes nearly 30 years ago (Katta & Chait, 1991; Light‐Wahl et al., 2002; Smith et al., 1992). At the time, native MS was largely restricted to a small set of MS systems due to the special hardware requirements for high‐mass transmission, most of which were performed on custom‐modified instruments. Now, many commercial instruments can be directly used for native MS without significant tuning. New MS instrumentation, activation methods, and sampling and separation processes have emerged rapidly, allowing more complex and heterogeneous samples to be studied as discussed in this review. The success of proof‐of‐principal, proteome‐level native MS now signals the emerging “native proteomics” on the horizon (Shen et al., 2018; Skinner et al., 2018). In addition to the protein identification and quantitation currently offered by proteomics analysis, noncovalent molecular interactions and stoichiometry can be extracted from native proteomics experiments. Such experiments can not only capture protein–protein interactions, but also protein–ligand, protein–metal, protein–DNA/RNA, protein–polymer, and many other types of interactions. The high versatility of native MS has the prospects to transform omics analysis by connecting different molecule types which are often studied separately and integrated post hoc with assumptions. Native MS's high speed also complements computational biology, and as the predictive power and accuracy of machine learning increases, low‐resolution structural constraints will likely be sufficient for preliminary confirmation of the models prior to more labor‐intensive characterization. Compositional and structural heterogeneity of endogenous protein complexes remains a major challenge for all analytical methods, with many proteins still lacking known functions and/or well‐defined structures. Native MS has demonstrated the unique potential to address these challenges and will continue contributing to the improved understanding of molecular mechanisms.
