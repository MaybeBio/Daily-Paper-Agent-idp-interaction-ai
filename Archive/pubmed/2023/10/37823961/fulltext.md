# Docosahexaenoic acid promotes vesicle clustering mediated by alpha-Synuclein via electrostatic interaction


## Abstract
  α-Synuclein (α-Syn) is an intrinsically disordered protein whose aggregation is associated with Parkinson’s disease, dementia, and other neurodegenerative diseases known as synucleinopathies. However, the functional role of α-Syn is still unclear, although it has been shown to be involved in the regulation of neurotransmitter release via the interaction with synaptic vesicles (SVs), vesicle clustering, and SNARE complex assembly. Fatty acids have significant occupancy in synaptic vesicles; and recent studies suggest the interaction of fatty acids with α-Syn affect the formation of (pathological) aggregates, but it is less clear how fatty acids affects the functional role of α-Syn including α-Syn-membrane interactions, in particular with (SV-like) vesicles. Here, we report the concentration dependent effect of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) in synaptic-like vesicle clustering via α-Syn interaction. Through molecular dynamics simulation, we revealed that DHA promoted vesicle clustering is due to the electrostatic interaction between DHA in the membrane and the N-terminal region of α-Syn. Moreover, this increased electrostatic interaction arises from a change in the macroscopic properties of the protein-membrane interface induced by (preferential solvation of) DHA. Our results provide insight as to how DHA regulates vesicle clustering mediated by α-Syn, and may further be useful to understand its physiological as well as pathological role.


## Introduction
  α-Synuclein (α-Syn) is a protein consisting of 140 amino acid residues, and is intrinsically disordered in solution. α-Syn is expressed throughout the central nervous system, which in vivo is found primarily in neurons at presynaptic termini, with concentrations around 20 μM in cells, and up to 50 μM in neuronal synapses. 1 The aggregation of α-Syn is strongly linked with Parkinson’s disease (PD) and other neurodegenerative disorders, including dementia and multiple system atrophy. 2 3 Aggregates of α-Syn are major components of intraneuronal inclusions known as Lewy bodies. 4 Recent studies suggest nucleation-dependent aggregation, where α-Syn forms cytotoxic amyloid oligomers and fibrils. 5 6 7

  There have been several studies on the role of α-Syn in the regulation of the homoeostasis of synaptic vesicles (SVs) during neurotransmitter release, and its prevalence at the presynaptic termini indicates that it may be involved in synaptic plasticity. 8 α-Syn shows binding affinity with SVs in vitro and strongly colocalises with SVs in synaptosomes in the presence of calcium ions. 9 Upon the binding of SVs, α-Syn has a tendency to promote their clustering. 10 11 12 α-Syn has also been shown to influence the regulation of vesicle trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus, 13 and to localize at mitochondrial membranes, where it has been proposed to mitigate effects of oxidative stress. 14 15 Each of these functions by α-Syn require its binding to biological membranes, an interaction that defines the conformation of α-Syn in vivo and influences the kinetics of its aggregation, 16 17 and the toxicity of its aggregates. 5 6

  Synaptic vesicles are asymmetric, approximately 40-50 nm in size, and composed of proteins, phospholipids, sphingolipids, cholesterol, fatty acids and other different lipids. The PC, PE, PI-PE lipids represent the major fraction of phospholipids composing the membrane bilayer, while PS lipids represent roughly 12% by mole. 18 Fatty acids are the building block of phospholipids and have significant presence in synaptic vesicle membranes. The binding of α-Syn to anionic lipid membranes in an amphipathic helical conformation is driven primarily by electrostatic interaction. 19 However, hydrophobicity, membrane defects, pH of medium, ionic strength, curvature, protein-lipid ratio, and lipid composition all have a role in the interaction of membranes and α-Syn. 19 20 21

  Fatty acids are biomolecules known for their long carbon chain geometry, and are broadly classified into two categories: saturated (SFAs), and unsaturated (monounsaturated (MUFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), characterized by none and one or more double bonds respectively. The functional relevance of fatty acids in the membrane include regulating the rigidity, permeability, curvature of the membrane and more; 9 22 several studies have shown α-Syn aggregation being effected in the presence of fatty acids. 23 24 25 Oligomerization of α-Syn is enhanced in the presence of PUFAs whereas inhibitory in the presence of SFAs. 25 However, studies have shown that at least one unsaturation is required for the interaction between α-Syn and fatty acids. For example, several free SFAs such as myristic acid, palmitic acid and arachidic acid have been shown to have no effect on the structural properties of the α-Syn; while polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) have been shown induce an alpha-helical conformation of α-Syn upon interaction. Moreover, prolonged exposure to DHA gradually converts alpha-synuclein into amyloid-like fibrils. 26 Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is an essential Omega-3 fatty acid (PUFA with 22:6 n-3). Protein/fatty acid ratio dependent structure formation of α-Syn driven by fatty acid with different morphology have been reported where α-Syn/DHA molar ratios of 1/10 and 1/50 for instance lead to amyloid-like fibrils with different conformational properties from those formed by α-Syn alone and the stable oligomers respectively. 27 Thus formed stable oligomers interact with membranes leading to undermining membrane integrity by increasing permeability. 28 Moreover, M. Manna et al use MD simulation to show how DHA modulates monomeric α-Syn-membrane interaction by affecting the local properties of the membrane, including variations in membrane thickness and distribution of membrane packing defects. 22 Moreover, fatty acids such as arachidonic acid (AA) inclusion on the vesicles has been shown to lead to the rapid multimerization of α-Syn. 30 Furthermore, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) such as oleic acid has been shown to lead to conformational changes in α-Syn. 28 29

  G. D. Franceschi et al (2009) reports the mechanism of acquiring α-helical conformation of α-Syn driven by fatty acid through two-state transition via binding of up to all of the first 70 residues and transitionally exposed 71-100 NAC region to promote fibril formation. 31 It has been suggested that DHA-induced α-Syn oligomers can undergo further fibrilizations upon interaction with additional DHA or vesicles with negatively charged lipids and further suggests the possibility of stabilization of oligomers through the interaction of the non-amyloid-beta component (NAC) region of α-Syn. 24 So while there is a growing body of work on the effect of fatty acids on the oligomerization of α-Syn, there is less known about the effect of fatty acids on the functional role of (monomeric) α-Syn in general; in particular the effect of additional fatty acids (in membrane) and their effect on vesicle clustering mediated by α-Syn.


## Results

### Vesicle clustering caused by α-Syn as measured by TIRF microscopy
  Here, we report the role of DHA in vesicles composed of phospholipids including PC, PE and PS to study the interaction mechanism of SV-like vesicles and α-Syn. For this we considered three variations in concentration of PUFAs, fixing 12% PS in all cases to produce SV-like vesicles (see Table 1), and imaging was conducted by using TIRF microscopy. We use 12% PS vesicles (0% mole DHA) as a control group, as clustering caused by initial electrostatic interaction between protein and vesicles described in our previous work; 21 33 34 whereas, 5% mole and 20% serve as the representative small and large concentrations of PUFAs respectively. The comparison of enhancement caused by α-Syn on the basis of channel-to-channel may not be likely, however, as long as we use the similar concentration of vesicles and protein, preparing the negative control group of corresponding species and keeping the same intensity as well as background set up during the imaging process, the comparison may be reasonable.

  Figure 1B shows the representative TIRF images of the effect in vesicle clustering caused by α-Syn in all three cases of concentration for DHA. The negative control channel corresponding to those species for all cases are also included for the reference. In case of 12% PS + 0% DHA DiI-vesicles, vesicle clustering is enhanced via α-Syn binding as expected. The vesicle clustering count in the case of 5% DHA is slightly increased, and significantly increased in the case of 20% DHA in the presence of α-Syn, as compared to 0% DHA. We quantify this change by measuring the vesicles count in all cases with appropriate background subtraction. Control groups (12% PS + 0% DHA) were considered when the change caused by α-Syn was significant (p<0.001%) as compared to the background (without α-Syn). Now, we subtract the corresponding background (without α-Syn) and calculate the enhancement caused by the protein. Finally we combined the data from the slides obtained in different days where each slide contains all three group samples for comparison and hence presented in a box and whisker plots.

  For all vesicle compositions, 12% PS was maintained. Furthermore, for the enhancements in clustering caused by α-Syn for 5% DHA and 20% DHA (compared to 0 DHA), we have p<0.01% and p<0.00001 as shown in Figure 2C. Thus, our results quantify the role of concentration dependence of DHA in vesicle clustering; that is, DHA enhances vesicle clustering mediated by α-Syn.


### DHA effects helical structure of α-Syn bound to lipid membranes
  Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy is based on the difference in absorption of left-hand circular (LHC) and right-hand circular (RHC) polarized light in the absorption bands of optically active chiral molecules. CD spectroscopy has a wide range of applications in many different fields; in particular UV CD is widely used to investigate the secondary structure of proteins. 35 CD spectra can be readily used to estimate the fraction of a molecule that is in the alpha-helix conformation, the beta-sheet conformation, the beta-turn conformation, or some other (e.g. random coil) conformation. 36 37

  We use CD spectroscopy to probe the secondary structure of α-Syn upon interaction with anionic vesicles of varying lipid composition, in particular DHA. Having observed the the promotional effect with increasing concentrations of DHA, we recorded the circular dichroism (CD) spectra of α-Syn in CD buffer (20 mM of Na2HPO4/ NaH2PO4 and 100 mM of NaF, pH 7.4), as shown in Figure 2. WT α-Syn has a minimum ellipticity of approximately 198 nm, 21 which suggests that free α-Syn possesses a high percentage of random coil structures. After α-Syn was mixed with control lipid vesicles containing 12% PS, we find a characteristic minimum at 203–205 nm without any inverse peak in the vicinity of 198 nm, which demonstrates that highly unstructured α-Syn undergoes a conformational change into a typical secondary structure (i.e., α-helix) thought to be the stable conformation of membrane-bound α-Syn. After α-Syn was mixed with vesicles containing 12% PS and DHA, characteristic minimum shifted to 207–210 nm. Our CD spectrum experiments thus suggest that DHA promotes the interaction between α-Syn and negatively charged lipid membranes, by enhancing the distribution of hydrogen bonds, and thus increasing the helical content of (vesicle-bound) α-Syn (see Fig. 2).


### DHA results in electrostatic enhancement in α-Syn-membrane interaction
  To confirm the change in interaction between α-Syn and lipid vesicles in the presence of DHA, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to study the interaction of α-Syn and lipid membranes with and without DHA. Previous studies have used MD simulations to study the conformational and structural properties of α-Syn upon membrane binding, 38 the role of calcium ions in the water environment and charge screening effects, 21 and the influence of membrane curvature on α-Syn binding. In particular we found that the preferential association is attributed to smaller, curved membranes containing more packing defects that expose hydrophobic acyl tails, which may favorably interact with hydrophobic residues of α-Syn. Furthermore, the number of membrane defects is proportional to the curvature and the size can be regulated by cholesterol. 39 Furthermore,the nature and initial abundance of packing defects modulate adsorption of α-Syn to model membranes and are dependent on the lipid composition. 22 39

  We tested the binding of α-Syn’s N-terminal domain (amino acids 1–60) to a model anionic membrane of similar SV-like composition with no DHA and 20%DHA (since we expect little enhancement for 5%). Figures 3-5 show that the total number of hydrogen bonds and total interaction energy between α-Syn’s N-terminus and lipid membranes increased in the presence of DHA. Figure 3A-C illustrates the interaction energies between α-Syn’s N-terminus and lipid membranes, confirming that electrostatic interaction is the primary driving force of their binding. Consistent with the CD experiments (Fig. 2), the MD results showed that DHA enhances α-Syn binding to (anionic lipid) membranes.

  Thus, the simulations showed that the presence of DHA significantly enhances protein binding The mechanism can be understood as follows. The fatty acid DHA enhances the protein binding through increasing the H-bond interactions between the protein and lipids. The protein firstly diffuses to the membrane surface anchored by the formation of hydrogen bonds. Manna et al showed DHA localizes around α-Syn’s N-terminal region upon insertion (see Fig. 4 from Manna et al), which leads to filling the void underneath the protein by adjusting conformation of the flexible hydrocarbon chains and are thus more likely to form H-bonds. Moreover, their work suggests the preferential solvation of DHA around α-Syn essentially lubricates the protein for insertion, and furthermore reduces changes in membrane environment including thickness, defects, and local curvature. Our results below also show that there are more hydrogen bonds available at the surface for a membrane only system (without α-Syn) with DHA when compared with an identical membrane system without DHA - see Figure 6. As a result, the DHA molecules are more likely to form H-bonds directly with α-syn, thereby increasing Coulomb energy. Hence, DHA enhances the protein binding by increasing the hydrogen bond interactions between the proteins and DHA, but the preferential solvation also leads to a reduced interaction with other lipids - see Figures 3-5. The results suggest a more significant effect due to interaction of the protein with the membrane, i.e., α-synuclein interaction effects distribution of lipids and in particular fatty acid components in the membrane, but membrane properties including lipid packing, diffusion, rigidity (modulated by lipid composition which also determines membrane rigidity, charge and other properties) affect the binding and conformational properties of α-syn.

  Even though our definition and calculation of packing defects is different from PackMem, our results are consistent with the results from (Manna et al 22). In particular, Figure 7 shows DHA reduced membrane thinning and had a negligible impact on defect distribution (consistent with preferential solvation), and it is primarily the addition and interaction of α-Syn that affects the distribution of lipid packing defects. Just like α-Syn interacts more strongly with membranes of higher curvature 40, but conversely α-Syn modulates membrane curvature 39, similarly α-Syn more strongly interacts with membranes with defects (dependent on lipid composition), but also α-Syn modulates the defects in the membrane by changing the local membrane environment.


## Discussion
  Recent work suggests that α-Syn senses and regulates the curvature of the membranes, and may be involved in both synaptic vesicle trafficking and the exocytosis and endocytosis of synaptic vesicles 7 12 41. Furthermore, other research has proposed that α-Syn can act as a bridge between membranes in which two helices bind to two synaptic vesicles and, in turn, cause vesicles to cluster. 21 42 Similarly, another recent study showed that α-Syn amyloid oligomers, acting as multivalent nanoparticles, can cause close contact and even hemifusion in negatively charged vesicles. 43

  Using solid-state nanopores to study the time-dependent kinetics of α-Syn oligomerization through a label-free and single molecule approach, recent studies suggests that the oligomers formed at the initial oligomerization stage may be the root cause of cytotoxicity, and imply oligomerization of α-Syn is dependent on membrane lipid composition. 44 Furthermore, by using a solid-state nanopore system, it has been shown that N-terminal acetylation can significantly decrease α-Syn oligomerization by disrupting intermolecular hydrogen bonds. 45 Moreover, silicon nitride solid-state nanopores have also been used to study the time-dependent kinetics of early oligomerization of wild-type α-Syn, A30P, and E46K mutants with different incubation times, the results imply that the distinct aggregation pathways and kinetics featured by wild-type α-Syn and mutations may account for their distinct cytotoxicity and pathology. 46

  Recent studies use all-atom molecular dynamics simulations to show O-GlcNAc modifications can suppress the process of oligomerization of α-Syn aggregates via a steric effect, where the additional O-linked glycosyl group disrupts the formation of hydrogen bonds between α-Syn monomers.47 Monte Carlo simulations have also been used to study α-Syn aggregation, using a coarse-grained model for amyloidogenic proteins and a hard sphere acting as steric hindrance were used to investigate how steric hindrance on proteins can affect amyloid formation, and reveal an inhibitory role in oligomerization based on the competition between steric hindrance and nonspecific attractions of soluble proteins. Moreover, the equimolar mixture of proteins with and without steric hindrance amplified the sterically inhibitory effect by increasing the energy barrier of protein's conformational conversion. 48

  It has been shown PUFA levels can lead to aggregation of α-Syn and subsequent deposition into potentially cytotoxic oligomers that precede inclusions in dopaminergic cells.49 Furthermore Fecchio et al showed α-Synuclein oligomers induced by DHA affect membrane integrity. 24 Because researchers have proposed that fatty acids can regulate the interaction between αSyn and lipid membranes by affecting the membrane microenvironment 22, we performed single-vesicle imaging to study vesicle clustering mediated by α-Syn and the effect of varying concentrations of DHA.

  A single-vesicle clustering assay revealed that the effect of DHA in vesicle clustering with α-Syn varies by concentration of fatty acid. DHA enhances vesicle clustering with α-Syn (Fig. 1), with increasing concentration, which can be attributed to the mechanism by which the preferential solvation of DHA to α-Syn enhances the interaction of α-Syn with vesicles, such that α-Syn can more effectively mediate vesicle clustering. This result is consistent with Manna et al’s finding that DHA assists the membrane insertion of α-Syn by modulating the macroscopic membrane properties and topology of the protein-membrane interface. 22

  Furthermore, CD spectroscopy experiments and MD simulations also indicated that DHA enhanced the interaction of α-Syn’s N terminus with vesicles with increasing DHA concentration (Figs. 3A and 3B). The promotional role of increasing concentrations of DHA in vesicle clustering may occur via an enhancement in hydrogen bonds and hence electrostatic interaction of DHA and the N-terminal region of α-Syn, which would corroborate previous findings that fatty acids can affect local properties of the membrane (24 22 39). Our results also suggest that the mechanism of DHA in regulating vesicle clustering varies with the DHA concentration, and that increasing concentration of DHA in an ensemble of anionic SUVs leads to the preferential solvation of DHA among multiple vesicles in the vicinity of vesicle-bound α-Syn, which drives them into close contact and, in turn, form clusters.


## Conclusion
  In this study, we quantify the effect of increasing concentrations of DHA on the functional role of α-Syn by employing several biophysical techniques to examine synaptic-like vesicle clustering mediated by α-Syn. By using single-vesicle clustering assay, our TIRF measurement shows concentration dependent enhanced clustering with DHA. Similarly, the results from CD spectroscopy and MD simulation show that increasing DHA enhances α-Syn binding to (anionic lipid) membranes through hydrogen bonds in the protein-membrane system. Hence, our results suggest that α-Syn’s N-terminal region membrane interaction with phospholipid membranes containing DHA results in enhanced vesicles clustering via electrostatic interaction.


## Experimental Methods

### Materials
  1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-(phospho-L-serine) (DOPS), and 1,2-dioleoly-sn-glycero-3-phospholethanolamine-N-(biotinyl) (Biotin-PE) were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). Fluorescent dyes i.e. DiD (DiIC18(5)) and DiI (DiIC18(3)) were purchased from Life Technologies Corporation (USA). Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and NeutrAvidin were provided by Thermo Scientific (USA). Recombinant human α-Syn protein was obtained by Alexotech (Sweden). Chromatography and SDS-PAGE measurements as performed by vendor shows that its purity was > 95%.


### Vesicle preparation
  Phospholipid vesicles such as small unilamellar vesicles (SUV) or liposomes that mimic the synaptic vesicles have been used as model membranes for in vitro studies of interactions in protein-lipid systems. 50 51 52 53 54 We use similar methods to prepare SUVs, as described in previous work. 34 55 Briefly, lipid compositions were prepared by mixing the lipid stock in a glass tube with proportions listed in Table 1. The mixtures were vortexed and sonicated until homogeneous. The organic solvents (chloroform/ethanol) were removed by placing the sample in a vacuum system (Sigma-Aldrich (USA)) for ~5 hours. The resulting dry film of lipid was hydrated with HEPES buffer (25 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 (as mentioned by NaOH)). The lipid solution was further vortexed and sonicated until the film was fully dissolved. The obtained suspension was then annealed by submerging in a heat bath at 55°C and then storing in contact with dry ice (−80°C), repeating 9 times to obtain the poly-dispersed unilamellar vesicle solution. The solution thus obtained was extruded through 50 nm size polycarbonate membrane (Avanti Lipids, USA) at least 20 times to obtain a more uniform distribution of SUVs. The collected SUVs were used immediately.


### Microfluidic chamber assembly
  Pre-drilled quartz glass slides and intact glass coverslips were cleaned by sonicating in acetone (25 min), 1 M KOH (25 min) and methanol (30 min) sequentially followed by rinsing with Milli-Q water right after each step, to remove non-specifically interacting molecules. One side of the quartz slides was burned for at least one minute and dipped immediately into the Milli-Q water, to oxidize the surface pre-PEG. To prevent the non-specific adsorption through PEGylating the quartz slide known as surface passivation, which is a multi-step process. First, the cleaned slides and coverslips from previous steps were incubated in amino saline solution (mixture of 100 mL methanol, 5 mL acetic acid, and 1 mL amino saline (3-(2-aminoethylamino) propyl trimethoxylinane)) for 10 minutes, sonicated for one minute and then incubated again for 10 minutes. Second, we rinse both slides and coverslips with Milli-Q water followed by methanol and drying. Finally, a freshly prepared 100 μL reaction solution (prepared by mixing 120 mg mPEG, 4 mg biotin-PEG and 700 μL sodium bicarbonate (0.1 M)) was coated on each glass slide using 200 μL pipet and hence covered by coverslip to set. This assembly was incubated overnight in the dark at room temperature (biotinylation). After ~24 hours, slides and coverslips were rinsed with water and dried. All these collective steps supplied the PEGylated slides which can be used immediately or in future if stored at −20°C. Microfluidic channels on glass slides were prepared as follows: small (<0.5 cm) strips of double-sided tape were placed on PEGylated side of slide keeping the hole position at center. Coverslip was placed on top of it and pressed gently. The assembly was sealed using the Epoxy glue (kept at STP for 30 minutes to be set) along the edge (lengthwise). The assembly thus formed creates the microfluidic channels on PEGylated slides.


### Sample preparation
  After obtaining the fresh vesicles as well as PEGylated slides with microfluidic channels, slide preparation steps for TIRF imaging were as follows: NeutrAvidin of 0.1 mg/mL (in 10 mM Tris-HCL, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) was injected (2 shots: 20 μL/shot) per microfluidic channel and incubated for 20 min. This creates a binding surface for Biotin-PE labeled samples (i.e DiD-vesicles) as a result of interaction with biotin. With appropriate dilution, two shots of DiD-vesicles (100 μL/shot) were injected and let them incubated for 35 min to form a homogeneously distributed DiD-vesicles’s generated planer surface. HEPES buffer was injected (~1 mL) to remove unbound DiD-vesicles. 50 μL/per channel in one shot of α-Syn was added and incubated for 30 min for effected channel whereas buffer only for controlled channel. Again, buffer exchange was done by HEPES buffer to remove the unbound proteins. Channels were further filled by injecting 100 μL DiI-vesicles with appropriate dilution and incubate for 30 min. Finally, the buffer exchanged was conducted by injecting 1 mL of HEPES to remove unbound vesicles.


### TIRF microscopy and analysis
  The schematic of TIRF microscopy setup for vesicle clustering assay is shown in Figure 1A. Sample imaging was conducted by exciting with green laser (532 nm) for DiI-vesicles and red laser (633 nm) for DiD-vesicles, captured by CCD camera and analyzed by smCamera program provided by Dr. Taekjip Ha’s group (John Hopkins University, USA). First, the coverage of DiD-vesicles upon the substrate was imaged. After confirming the quality and surface distribution of the substrate with the red laser, ten (20 frames total including red channel) images were obtained at various (random) positions per channel by using green laser to measure the DiI-vesicles clustering. Vesicle clustering was quantified by counting the number of fluorescent sources, with an algorithm counting the amount of Gaussian peaks within a given threshold in brightness (determined by height of Gaussian distribution) and size (given by width of Gaussian distribution). Student’s t-test was used to test the significance of protein induced changes. If p<0.001, change is considered as highly significant.


### Preparation of MD simulation models
  The lipid bilayers were constructed using the CHARMM-GUI input generator. 56 57 Here, the lipid composition corresponding to 0% DHA is given by the composition 12%DOPS + 20%DOPE + 68%DOPC, and 20%DHA given by 12%DOPS +20%DOPE + 48%DOPC + 20%DHA. While the curved vesicle was approximated as a flat membrane, we simulated the curved membrane by controlling the packing density of lipids in a curved membrane, by applying tension to the flat membrane given by σ = <Lz/2(pzz-(pxx+pyy)/2)>, where σ is the membrane tension, pij is the ij component of the pressure, Lz is the box length along the z direction, and <> represents the time average. 58 59 To mimic the packing density of lipids in vesicles, e.g. 1.19–1.35 lipids per square nanometer for a vesicle with approximately 20 nm diameter, 60 61 we applied pzz = 1 bar, pxx = pyy = −28 bar to achieve a packing density of approximately 1.28 lipids per square nanometer of the membrane. The system was solvated in an (approximately) 20x20x12 nm3 TIP3 water box, 62 with ~80,000 water molecules and an appropriate number of sodium ions added to neutralize the system. The structure of α-Syn was obtained from the RCSB protein data bank, PDB ID: 1XQ8. 38 To reduce the computational demand, only the N-terminus of α-Syn (i.e., residues 1–60) was used in the simulations. The protein is initially positioned parallel to the membrane surface, a distance from the membrane sufficiently larger than the radius of gyration of the protein, for us a typical separation distance is 5 nm.


### MD simulation method
  All simulations were performed using GROMACS 63 with the CHARMM36 force field. 64 Periodic boundary conditions were applied, and the temperature was coupled to 310 K with the V-rescale algorithm. 65 To maintain the packing density of lipids, the pressure was coupled via the Parrinello–Rahman method with pzz = 1 bar, pxx = pyy = −28 bar to apply membrane tension. 66 The LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain the covalent bonds with H atoms. 67 The time step of the simulations was 2.0 fs. The particle-mesh Ewald summation method was used to calculate the long-range electrostatic interactions. 68 The cutoff of the non-bonded interactions was set to 12 Å. All graphics and visualization analyses were processed using the VMD package. 69 Hydrogen bonds were determined based on default angle and distance cutoffs for H-Donor-Acceptor atoms in GROMACS. Energies (Coulomb and VDW) calculated by specifying groups (protein, lipid, etc.) and the positions of the atoms in those groups allows calculation of potential energies by specifying what atoms and hence positions are being used for calculation. Both hydrogen bond and energy analyses were performed averaging over an ensemble of protein-lipid systems, in our case 4 simulations each (with or without DHA). Unless otherwise stated, error bars are given by standard deviations.


### Method for detection of defects, calculation of membrane thickness
  Following the approach from previous paper 39, we selected the hydrophilic head groups of lipids and projected the head group atoms onto a grid meshed with a grid size of 0.1 nm. For each grid, we calculated 9 the distance L between the center of the grid and the mass center of the projected atom. An overlap of the grid and the projected atom is assigned when L is shorter than the critical distance, dc = rvdw + d1/2 where rvdw is the van der Waals radius of corresponding atoms and d1/2 = 0.07 nm is the half diagonal length of the grid. Otherwise, there is no overlap between the grid and the projected atom. Following the above method, if there is no overlap between a grid and any atoms, the grid is identified as a defect grid with the area of 0.01 nm2. The defect grids within distance 03 nm are identified as in the same defect. The area of one defect thus defined as the total area of its defect grids. The calculation of number of defects. According to previous work 39, we identified the defect grids belonging to two different defects when the two regions are not connected with a distance larger than 0.3 nm. Membrane thickness was calculated using GridMAT-MD, a free program developed (by Justin Lemkul et al) to aid in the analysis of lipid bilayers from molecular dynamics simulations. It reads a GROMACS coordinate file and generates two types of data: a two-dimensional contour plot depicting membrane thickness, and a polygon-based tessellation of the individual lipid headgroups. 70
