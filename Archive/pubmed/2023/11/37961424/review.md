## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, supplementary material references)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, methodological rigor, internal consistency, and support of claims by presented evidence. No assessment of formatting or editorial style.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors investigate the N-terminal low complexity domain of EWS (EWSLCD), a FET family protein, to characterize its conformational ensemble, self-association, and phase separation behavior. They combine NMR (relaxation, PRE), hydrodynamic methods (AUC, SEC, DOSY), microscopy, and atomistic and coarse-grained simulations to propose that tyrosine residues, particularly in the N- and C-terminal regions, drive intramolecular and intermolecular interactions that underpin phase separation. They further suggest that the patterning and local environment of tyrosines, rather than simply their abundance, are critical determinants of phase separation propensity, distinguishing EWS from FUS.
- **Visible evidence base** Full text including methods, results, figure legends (referenced but figures not visible), supplementary figure and table references, and discussion.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures and supplementary figures/tables are referenced but not provided. Raw NMR data, simulation trajectories, and analysis scripts are not available. Specific numerical values for key measurements (e.g., Csat values, PRE magnitudes, Rg distributions) are described qualitatively in the text but not presented in tabulated form.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a comprehensive and technically ambitious study of the EWS low complexity domain, addressing a significant gap in the FET protein field where FUS has been the primary focus. The combination of experimental and computational approaches is appropriate and the conclusions regarding the role of tyrosine residues in driving phase separation are plausible and generally supported by the described data. However, the manuscript has several weaknesses that need to be addressed. The claim that intramolecular interactions in the dilute phase directly inform on intermolecular interactions driving phase separation is based on correlative evidence from simulations, and the experimental validation of this link is limited. The presentation of key quantitative data is often vague, relying on qualitative descriptions ("~10-15 s-1", "slightly increase") rather than precise values with uncertainties. The discussion of the relationship between the observed conformational preferences and the oncogenic function of EWS::FLI1, while interesting, is speculative and not directly tested. The manuscript would be strengthened by clearer presentation of quantitative results, a more critical discussion of the limitations of the simulation-to-experiment comparison, and a more careful distinction between established findings and hypotheses.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying biomolecular condensates and liquid-liquid phase separation, particularly those focused on the molecular determinants of phase separation in intrinsically disordered proteins. The FET protein community, especially those interested in the differences between FUS, EWS, and TAF15, will find the comparative analysis valuable. The study is also relevant to cancer biologists studying Ewing sarcoma, as it provides molecular-level insights into the EWS LCD that is part of the oncogenic EWS::FLI1 fusion. The methodological approach, combining NMR, hydrodynamics, and simulations, will be of interest to biophysicists and computational biologists working on IDPs.
- **Major strengths** 1. The study addresses a significant knowledge gap by focusing on EWS, which is understudied compared to FUS. 2. The multi-technique approach (NMR, AUC, SEC, DOSY, microscopy, CG and AA simulations) is comprehensive and provides complementary views of the system. 3. The use of both experimental and computational methods to probe the role of tyrosine residues, including specific mutants, is a rigorous approach. 4. The comparison between dilute and condensed phase behavior, particularly the correlation of interaction patterns, is a valuable contribution. 5. The study provides a plausible molecular explanation for the differences in phase separation behavior between EWS and FUS, which is an important conceptual advance.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence strength
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that "interactions observed in the dilute phase inform on atomic interactions driving phase separation" and that the condensed phase simulations show that "the same residue types responsible for compaction in a dilute phase also serve to stabilize the condensed phase."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Peptide Chain Dynamics and Self-Association of the EWSLCD" and "Condensed Phase Intermolecular Contacts Mimic Intramolecular Interactions in the Dilute Phase", Figures 3, 6, Supp. Fig. 7
  - **Concern** The central claim that dilute phase intramolecular interactions are directly predictive of condensed phase intermolecular interactions is supported primarily by a correlation between simulation-derived contact maps from single-chain and multichain simulations. The experimental evidence for this link is indirect. The PRE data are collected in the dilute phase, and the authors explicitly state that attempts to study the condensed phase experimentally were challenging. The correlation between dilute and condensed phase contacts, while suggestive, does not establish a causal or mechanistic link. The authors should either provide direct experimental evidence for the condensed phase interaction network or temper the strength of this claim. The statement that condensed phase simulations "strongly suggest" this link is an overstatement given the indirect nature of the evidence.
  - **Why it matters** This is the central conceptual claim of the paper. If the dilute-to-condensed phase transferability of interaction motifs is not firmly established, the relevance of the extensive dilute phase analysis to understanding phase separation is weakened. The claim has implications for how the field uses dilute phase data to infer condensed phase behavior.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) provide experimental data on condensed phase interactions (e.g., solid-state NMR, or measurements in the condensed phase that report on specific contacts), (b) perform simulations that directly test the predictive power of dilute phase contacts for condensed phase behavior (e.g., by mutating residues predicted to be important in the dilute phase and showing a corresponding effect in the condensed phase simulation), or (c) revise the claim to clearly state that the condensed phase interaction network is inferred from simulations and that the correlation with dilute phase data is suggestive but not definitive.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Quantitative rigor
  - **Claim pointer** The authors report various quantitative measurements, including PRE rates, relaxation rates, Csat values, and Rg distributions, but the presentation is largely qualitative.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Peptide Chain Dynamics and Self-Association of the EWSLCD" and "Tyrosine Residues Mediate Phase Separation of the EWSLCD", Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, Supp. Figs. 2, 3, 4, 6
  - **Concern** Throughout the results, quantitative values are described with vague terms such as "~10-15 s-1", "~5-8 s-1", "slightly increase", "more compact", and "similar trend". Exact values with appropriate uncertainties (standard deviations, confidence intervals) are not provided in the text or in tables. For example, the Csat values extracted from simulations are described as showing a "similar trend" to pelleting assays, but the actual values are not given. This lack of quantitative detail makes it difficult for the reader to assess the magnitude of the reported effects and the statistical significance of the differences between WT and mutants.
  - **Why it matters** The conclusions regarding the differential effects of tyrosine mutations on phase separation propensity rely on quantitative comparisons. Without precise values and error estimates, the reader cannot evaluate the robustness of these conclusions. This is particularly important for the claim that Y170S/Y172S has a greater effect than single mutations, which requires a quantitative comparison.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a table or supplementary table with all key quantitative values (PRE rates, relaxation rates, Csat values, Rg values, etc.) including means, standard deviations, and the number of replicates. For simulation data, the number of independent simulations and the standard error of the mean should be reported. The text should refer to these specific values rather than using qualitative descriptors.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Experimental validation
  - **Claim pointer** The authors state that "tyrosine residues primarily drive the interactions vital for EWSLCD phase separation" and that "a higher density and proximity of tyrosine residues amplify the likelihood of condensate formation."
  - **Evidence pointer** Results sections "Tyrosine Residues Mediate Phase Separation of the EWSLCD", Figures 4, 5, Table 1
  - **Concern** The conclusion that tyrosine residues are the primary drivers of phase separation is based on turbidity measurements, pelleting assays, and simulations of a limited set of mutants. While the data are consistent with this conclusion, the manuscript does not provide a systematic analysis of the contribution of other residue types. The authors note that Tyr-Gln, Tyr-Ser, and Tyr-Pro interactions are also significant, but the relative importance of these compared to Tyr-Tyr is not quantified. Furthermore, the authors do not test whether mutations that disrupt other types of interactions (e.g., polar-polar) have comparable effects on phase separation. The claim that tyrosine is the "primary" driver is therefore not fully supported by the presented data.
  - **Why it matters** The central mechanistic claim of the paper is that tyrosine residues are the key drivers of phase separation. If other residue types contribute comparably, the proposed molecular grammar for EWS would need to be revised. A more complete mutational analysis would strengthen the conclusion.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should either (a) perform additional mutations targeting other residue types (e.g., glutamine, serine) and compare their effects on phase separation to the tyrosine mutants, (b) provide a quantitative decomposition of the interaction energies from simulations that shows the relative contribution of Tyr-Tyr versus other interaction types, or (c) revise the claim to state that tyrosine residues are "a major driver" rather than "the primary driver" of phase separation.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M4
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Reproducibility
  - **Claim pointer** The authors describe the use of "all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations" and "coarse-grained (CG) simulations" to study EWSLCD behavior.
  - **Evidence pointer** Methods sections "Coarse-Grained (CG) Simulation" and "All-Atom (AA) Simulation"
  - **Concern** The methods section for the simulations lacks sufficient detail for reproduction. For the CG simulations, the specific parameters of the HPS-Urry model are not fully described, and the method for constructing the mutant models is not specified. For the AA simulations, the force field parameters are mentioned (Amber99SBws-STQ), but the water model, ion parameters, and the specific protocol for building the initial structures from CG simulations are not fully detailed. The criteria for determining equilibration (e.g., "based on the Rg autocorrelation function") are described qualitatively. The number of independent simulations and the total simulation time for each system are not clearly stated for all systems.
  - **Why it matters** Reproducibility is a core requirement for scientific publication. The simulation results are central to the conclusions of the paper, and insufficient methodological detail prevents other researchers from reproducing or building upon this work.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide a complete description of the simulation protocols, including all force field parameters, water model, ion concentrations, temperature and pressure coupling methods, simulation box sizes, number of replicas, total simulation times, and the specific criteria used to determine equilibration. This information could be provided in the main text or as a supplementary methods section.
- **Minor Comments**
  - **Concern ID** R1-m1
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Clarity
  - **Affected element** Figure 1 and associated text
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "EWS Domain Architecture and Differences between FUS and EWS", Figure 1
  - **Issue** The description of the domain architecture comparison between EWS, FUS, and TAF15 is clear, but the figure is not visible. The text states that "the distribution of alanine and serine are predominantly clustered in the N- and C-terminus of EWSLCD respectively" but does not provide a quantitative measure of this clustering. The statement that "tyrosine appears to be relatively well distributed in FUSLCD, while in EWSLCD there is a patch between residues 128 to 157 that is devoid of tyrosine" would benefit from a quantitative analysis of tyrosine spacing or density.
  - **Required correction** Provide a quantitative analysis of residue distribution (e.g., a plot of residue frequency along the sequence for each protein) or refer to a supplementary figure that shows this analysis. This would strengthen the claim of differential residue patterning between EWS and FUS.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m2
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Interpretation
  - **Affected element** Discussion of FRAP results
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Physiochemical Properties of EWSLCD", Figure 2I, Supp. Fig. 2G
  - **Issue** The FRAP data are described as showing "rapid recovery" for fresh droplets and incomplete recovery after aging. However, the text does not provide quantitative FRAP recovery percentages or recovery half-times. The statement that aged droplets are "less liquid-like" is based on incomplete recovery, but the text does not discuss alternative interpretations, such as increased viscosity or network formation that might not strictly correspond to a loss of liquid character.
  - **Required correction** Provide quantitative FRAP parameters (e.g., mobile fraction, half-time of recovery) for fresh and aged droplets. Briefly discuss the interpretation of incomplete recovery in the context of material properties of condensates.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m3
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Completeness
  - **Affected element** Discussion of EWS::FLI1 implications
  - **Evidence pointer** Discussion section
  - **Issue** The discussion of the implications for EWS::FLI1 oncogenic function is interesting but brief. The authors mention the yin-yang hypothesis and the importance of the LCD in the fusion protein, but do not elaborate on how their findings specifically inform the mechanism of EWS::FLI1 activity. For example, the authors could discuss how the identified tyrosine-mediated interaction network might be targeted therapeutically or how it might be modulated by post-translational modifications.
  - **Required correction** Expand the discussion of the implications for EWS::FLI1 function, providing a more detailed and mechanistic link between the molecular findings and the oncogenic activity of the fusion protein.
  - **Concern ID** R1-m4
  - **Severity** Minor
  - **Axis** Terminology
  - **Affected element** Abstract and Introduction
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Introduction
  - **Issue** The term "molecular diversity" in the title and abstract is not clearly defined. The manuscript focuses on differences between EWS and FUS, but the concept of "diversity" within the FET family is not systematically addressed. The authors do not discuss TAF15 in detail, and the comparison is primarily between EWS and FUS.
  - **Required correction** Either define what is meant by "molecular diversity" in the context of this study, or revise the title and abstract to more accurately reflect the scope of the work, which is a detailed characterization of EWSLCD with comparisons to FUS.

- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** **Originality:** The study is original in its focus on EWS, which is understudied relative to FUS. The finding that tyrosine patterning, rather than just abundance, is important for phase separation is a novel contribution. However, the overall concept that aromatic residues drive phase separation in LCDs is well established. **Scientific importance:** The study addresses an important question about the molecular determinants of phase separation in a disease-relevant protein. The findings have implications for understanding the FET protein family and the oncogenic mechanism of EWS::FLI1. The importance is moderate to high, but the impact would be greater if the dilute-to-condensed phase link were more firmly established. **Interdisciplinary readership:** The topic is of interest to biophysicists, biochemists, cell biologists, and cancer biologists. The methods used are diverse and would appeal to a broad readership. However, the manuscript is written in a way that assumes significant background knowledge in NMR and simulation techniques, which may limit accessibility. **Technical soundness:** The experimental and computational methods are generally appropriate and well-executed. The combination of techniques is a strength. However, the lack of quantitative detail and the indirect nature of the evidence for the central claim are weaknesses. **Readability for nonspecialists:** The manuscript is clearly written but is dense and technical. The abstract is accessible, but the results and discussion sections would be challenging for nonspecialists. The authors could improve readability by providing more context and explaining technical terms.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The study addresses an important question and presents a comprehensive dataset. However, the central claim regarding the relationship between dilute and condensed phase interactions needs to be either strengthened with additional evidence or appropriately qualified. The lack of quantitative detail needs to be addressed. If these concerns are resolved, the manuscript would be a valuable contribution to the field.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "interactions observed in the dilute phase inform on atomic interactions driving phase separation" is not directly supported by experimental evidence and relies on correlative simulation data.
- The claim that "tyrosine residues primarily drive the interactions vital for EWSLCD phase separation" is supported by the mutant data, but the relative contribution of other residue types is not systematically assessed.
- The statement that the C-terminal region of EWSLCD is "the most FUS-like in terms of sequence composition and amino acid patterning" is made without a quantitative sequence comparison.
- The discussion of the implications for EWS::FLI1 oncogenic function is speculative and not directly tested in this study.
- The claim that the study provides "one of the pioneering efforts to probe the structural details of EWSLCD at atomic resolution" is difficult to evaluate without a thorough literature search, but the statement is appropriately cautious.
