# Experimental NOE, Chemical
Shift, and Proline Isomerization
Data Provide Detailed Insights into Amelotin Oligomerization


## Abstract
  

  Amelotin is an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) rich in Pro residues and is involved in hydroxyapatite mineralization. It rapidly oligomerizes under physiological conditions of pH and pressure but reverts to its monomeric IDP state at elevated pressure. We identified a 105-residue segment of the protein that becomes ordered upon oligomerization, and we used pressure-jump NMR spectroscopy to measure long-range NOE contacts that exist exclusively in the oligomeric NMR-invisible state. The kinetics of oligomerization and dissociation were probed at the residue-specific level, revealing that the oligomerization process is initiated in the C-terminal half of the segment. Using pressure-jump NMR, the degree of order in the oligomer at the sites of Pro residues was probed by monitoring changes in cis/trans equilibria relative to the IDP state after long-term equilibration under oligomerizing conditions. Whereas most Pro residues revert to trans in the oligomeric state, Pro-49 favors a cis configuration and three Pro residues retain an unchanged cis fraction, pointing to their local lack of order in the oligomeric state. NOE contacts and secondary 13C chemical shifts in the oligomeric state indicate the presence of an 11-residue α-helix, preceded by a small intramolecular antiparallel β-sheet, with slower formation of long-range intermolecular interactions to N-terminal residues. Although none of the models generated by AlphaFold2 for the amelotin monomer was consistent with experimental data, subunits of a hexamer generated by AlphaFold-Multimer satisfied intramolecular NOE and chemical shift data and may provide a starting point for developing atomic models for the oligomeric state.


## Introduction
  Due to their dynamic and heterogeneous nature, large oligomeric protein aggregates usually are not amenable to structural studies at the residue-specific level. Such oligomerization is considered key to the etiology of numerous amyloid diseases, including Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, diabetes, and many others.1−3 In contrast to protein fibrils, which typically are highly stable, tightly packed species, protein oligomers are often thermodynamically less stable and can be rapidly reverted to their monomeric state by changing sample conditions such as ionic strength, pH, or hydrostatic pressure, p.4−6 The latter is a particularly powerful variable because it allows reversible oligomerization processes to be studied repeatedly without altering the sample composition. With the void volume, ΔV, in oligomeric states increasing nearly linearly with oligomer size, the pΔV term of the Gibbs free energy makes the oligomers quite sensitive to pressure-induced denaturation. Using pressure-jump NMR spectroscopy,7,8 we demonstrated that in their oligomeric state, Aβ peptides adopt a highly ordered structure for about two-thirds of their residues while retaining dynamic disorder for the remainder.9 Unfortunately, the reversible Aβ oligomerization process competes with the autocatalytic accumulation of pressure-resistant fibrils, which limits the number of pressure cycles to a few thousand and thereby interferes with studies of the oligomer structure.9

  Here, we introduce a new pressure-jump NMR technology for probing the structure of an oligomeric protein species, amelotin, an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) that rapidly oligomerizes but is much less prone to forming pressure-resistant fibrils than Aβ peptides. We demonstrate that residue-specific secondary and tertiary structure information on the oligomers can be obtained from 13C chemical shifts and 1H–1H nuclear Overhauser enhancements (NOE), measured during the early phase of the oligomerization process. In addition, 15N relaxation rates report local backbone dynamics. We also show that the degree of local order that occurs later during the oligomerization process, on the time scale of minutes or hours, can be derived from the change in the cis–trans equilibrium of X-Pro peptide bonds in the oligomeric state relative to that seen in the dynamically disordered random coil.

  Novel approaches for predicting a protein’s structure from its amino acid sequence frequently can generate atomic resolution models that rival or exceed the quality of experimentally determined structures.10−12 An extension of one of these computational methods, AlphaFold, yielded the powerful AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold-Multimer programs,10,13 which have been shown successful at correctly predicting the atomic resolution structures of well-defined oligomeric complexes.14−18 However, no unique predictions are generally available for proteins that do not adopt a distinct oligomeric state of a well-defined size, as is often seen during the initial aggregation process of proteins implicated in amyloid diseases, prior to the formation of highly ordered fibril species.

  Even though the structural information derived from our pressure-jump NMR experiments does not suffice to build atomic resolution oligomer models, we show that the experimental restraints provide secondary structural information about the monomeric subunit and elucidate the intermolecular interactions between them. Such analyses therefore present a first and pivotal step for further iterative studies, such as mutagenesis, paramagnetic spin labeling, or fluorescent tagging for FRET experiments to bootstrap the study of oligomers to increasing levels of structural detail.


## Experimental Section

### Protein
Expression and Purification
  Gene constructs for human amelotin (UniProt Q6UX39; AMTN and AMTN25–130) were codon-optimized and cloned into the pET-28a gene vector (Genscript). Residue numbering used in our study does not include the N-terminal 16-residue signal peptide but contains an N-terminal Met residue required for protein expression and a C-terminal His6-tag connected by a 5-residue Trp-containing linker (Figure S1A). Protein expression followed standard protocols (SI Text). Purification of the construct was carried out using Ni-nitriloacetic acid affinity chromatography followed by size exclusion chromatography using 30 mM sodium acetate at pH 4.6 and 70 mM NaCl buffer that also contained 1 mM benzamidine HCl as a broad-spectrum protease inhibitor. Perdeuteration, needed for NOE measurement and providing improved 1HN resolution for other measurements, was achieved by expression in >99% D2O, containing perdeuterated glucose and 15NH4Cl as the sole sources of carbon and nitrogen, respectively. For backbone assignment experiments and those requiring measurement of 13C, a sample prepared using U–13C/2H glucose (2 g/L) and 15NH4Cl (1 g/L) was used, supplemented by 1g/L U–13C/2H/15N-Isogro (Sigma-Aldrich). For 13C-detected CON measurements, a sample prepared by using protonated precursors in H2O was used.


### NMR Spectroscopy
  NMR experiments were carried out at field strengths ranging from 700 to 900 MHz, equipped with Bruker Avance-III (700 MHz) or Neo (800 and 900 MHz) consoles, using a zirconium oxide NMR sample cell,19 rated at 3 kbar of pressure, with an inner diameter of 2.8 mm, and filled with 205 μL of protein solution, using mineral spirits (Sigma-Aldrich) as the hydraulic fluid. Experiments at static pressure were carried out using a commercial Daedalus syringe pump (Daedalus Innovations, Aston, PA) for sample pressurization. Measurements utilizing pressure jumps used a home-built device.8 Details regarding the various measurements are included in the Supporting Information.


### Backbone Resonance Assignments
  Resonance assignments were carried out by standard 3D triple resonance methods using the U–13C/15N/2H AMTN and AMTN25–130 samples in 97% H2O, 3% D2O, at 15 °C and 2 kbar on a 700 MHz Bruker Avance III spectrometer equipped with a triple-axis gradient TCI cryogenic probe. All 3D spectra were acquired at high digital resolution using sparse sampling (ca. 12%) of the nonuniformly sampled (NUS)20,21 data. Spectra were processed using NMRPipe software,22 using the SMILE module23 for reconstruction of NUS data, and analyzed using CCPNMR software.24 Assignments were transferred to other pressures and temperatures by stepwise changing conditions and tracking cross-peak positions. See the SI for details.


### Cis-Pro Assignments
  Resonance assignments of the minor components in the spectrum for residues adjacent to Pro residues that correspond to the X-Pro cis isomers were also carried out using standard triple resonance methodology, using a more concentrated sample of 600 μM [13C/15N/2H]-AMTN25–130 at 2 kbar and −8 °C in 30 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.6) containing 20 mM NaCl. Assignments were transferred to standard conditions by stepwise changes in the temperature on a more dilute sample (50 μM).

  13C-detected CON spectra were recorded on a 700 MHz spectrometer equipped with a Bruker TCI pulsed-field-gradient probehead using methods described previously.25 Measurements were carried out at 2000 bar and 5 °C in 20 mM HCl (pH 2) using a 1000 μM [13C/15N]-AMTN25–130 sample.


### 15N Relaxation Measurements
  Pressure-jump experiments require rather long (ca. 9 s) interscan delays for the sample to re-equilibrate after each pressure cycle and for the heating generated by the fast flow of hydraulic fluid to dissipate sufficiently. Consequently, the number of free induction decays that can be sampled in a reasonable amount of time is limited, and all relaxation measurements were based on the sampling of just two time points, with the assumption of exponential decay (SI Text).


### Oligomer Chemical Shifts
  Chemical shifts in the oligomeric state were measured 15, 30, and 300 ms after the pressure drop from 2 kbar to 1 bar, using stroboscopic observation and the pulse schemes of Figure S7. Chemical shifts during the dissociation phase were measured at 15, 30, and 300 ms after jumping the pressure back from 1 bar to 2 kbar. See the SI for details.


## Results and Discussion

### Pressure Dissociation of
Amelotin Oligomers
  We show that pressure-jump NMR spectroscopy provides detailed structural information on an oligomerizing species, amelotin (AMTN), a protein that plays a key role in the formation of enamel and hydroxyapatite mineralization.26,27 Amelotin also has been implicated in the formation of hydroxyapatite deposits in age-related macular degeneration.28 While the protein is soluble and, at concentrations below ca. 5 μM, yields an NMR 15N–1H HSQC spectrum that is characteristic of an IDP, it oligomerizes to form nanospheres with diameters in the 10–20 nm range at higher concentrations (Supporting Information Figure S1).29 AlphaFold210 predicts the protein to be highly disordered but also suggests the presence of two helical segments for residues P49–L53 and E103–L116 with no contacts between them (Figure S2). However, NMR secondary chemical shifts for these two segments are found to be close to random coil values and provide no evidence of significant helix population (Figure S2). Indeed, AlphaFold2’s strategy to “bet on the winning horse”30 can predict helices for sequences that are prone to adopt helical conformation; in contrast, observations show that these sequences are often disordered.31,32

  At atmospheric pressure, the 1H–15N heteronuclear single quantum correlation (HSQC) NMR spectrum of a 500 μM solution of AMTN reveals far fewer resonances than expected for a 205-residue IDP that includes 24 Pro residues (Figure 1B, Figure S3). On the other hand, under dilute sample conditions (5 μM), the HSQC spectrum is essentially complete, and narrow chemical shift dispersion together with sharp resonances confirms not only the IDP nature of AMTN (Figure 1A) but also that it oligomerizes when the protein concentration is increased. The observation of ca. 90 sharp resonances in the NMR spectrum of the 500 μM sample shows that many residues retain a high degree of dynamic disorder in the aggregated state.

  Due to their size and associated large intersubunit contact area, protein oligomers have a large total interfacial void volume, making them prone to disassembly when hydrostatic pressure is applied.6,33 Indeed, application of 2 kbar of pressure suffices to dissociate these oligomers and yields a complete, highly resolved HSQC spectrum (Figure S3; see SI Table S1 for chemical shifts) with sharp resonances, characteristic of a dynamically disordered state that is essentially indistinguishable from the spectrum recorded under atmospheric pressure at dilute concentrations (5 μM). The small chemical shift differences at 2 kbar relative to the IDP state of the protein observed at 1 bar under dilute conditions (Figure 1) are consistent with the known 1HN and 15N chemical shift dependencies on pressure in disordered peptides.34−37 Moreover, chemical shifts for the polypeptide backbone 15N, 13C’, and 13Cα resonances are very close to random coil values, confirming the absence of significantly populated, ordered secondary structure elements (Figure S2).

  Resonances that remain visible, and are therefore from disordered regions in the oligomeric state, correspond to the N-terminal segment, Q4-N30, and the C-terminal region, A129-Q194, plus its C-terminal 5-residue Trp-containing linker (for peptide quantification) and His6-tag. Shortening the polypeptide by removing these flexible terminal regions but retaining the Trp-containing linker and His6-tag yielded the truncated AMTN25–130 construct. Resonance positions of the shorter polypeptide at high pressure are essentially unchanged relative to full length amelotin, and their attenuation at 1 bar of pressure closely follows that seen for the full-length protein (Figure 1). Further analysis focuses on this shorter construct.


### Longitudinal
and Transverse Relaxation in the Aggregated State
  15N nuclear spin relaxation rates are robust probes for the motional characteristics of backbone amide groups in proteins.41,42 The reversible effect of pressure on protein oligomerization has long been recognized6 and permits the measurement of residue-specific 15N relaxation in the NMR-invisible oligomeric state by reading out the relaxation decay in the NMR-visible state at high pressure.8 Such experiments require rapid jumps in hydrostatic pressure of the NMR sample and oligomerization and deoligomerization times that are short relative to the 15N longitudinal relaxation times, T1, of the monomeric species. Rapid jumps of multiple kilobars in hydrostatic pressure of an NMR sample, on the time scale of a few milliseconds, while retaining high NMR sensitivity, have become possible by the introduction of specialized hardware7,8 combined with high-strength NMR sample tubes constructed of zirconium oxide.19 Such pressure-jump NMR experiments were previously used for probing the 15N relaxation rates in oligomerized Aβ peptides.9 The Aβ oligomeric state was shown to exhibit a high degree of order for many of the residues, as reflected in 15N T1 values longer than 5 s at 14 T magnetic field strength, compared to ca. 0.6 s in the monomeric, dynamically disordered state.

  Here, we use a very similar scheme (Figure 2A) to measure 15N T1 values (Figure 2B), as well as Hahn-echo and spin-locked transverse relaxation times, shown as their inverse, R2 and R1ρ relaxation rates (Figure 2C). In contrast to Aβ, upon oligomerization, the AMTN25–130T1 relaxation times, measured at 900 MHz 1H frequency, show only a moderate increase from their dynamically disordered monomeric state (T1 ∼800 ms) to ca. 2 s for the slowest relaxing amides (Figure 2B), whereas many of the T1 relaxation times remain intermediate between the short values expected for random coil disorder and the many seconds expected for a slowly tumbling, well ordered species.

  R2 and R1ρ relaxation rates in the oligomeric state (Figure 2C) follow a pattern that resembles that of longitudinal relaxation. Rates near the termini remain close to their pressure-denatured, random coil values (ca. 3 s–1), but very rapid transverse relaxation (≥∼100 s–1) is observed for two segments, extending from ca. V32–N55 and H83–T124. Remarkably, however, for many of these rapidly relaxing backbone 15N resonances, the Hahn-echo transverse relaxation rate, R2, is considerably higher than the spin-locked relaxation rate, R1ρ, pointing to the presence of additional contributions to R2.

  While transverse 15N relaxation in globular proteins is dominated by J(0) spectral density, it also can contain a component that results from conformational exchange, commonly referred to as Rex. For the simplest case, where Rex can be attributed to exchange between two states, A and B, with angular 15N resonance frequencies ωA and ωB, Rex is given by1where kex = kA→B + kB→A, population pA = (1 – pB) = (kB→A)/(kB→A + kA→B), and ω1eff is the effective strength of the RF spin lock field in the unit of radians per second. For cases where ω1eff ≪ kex, Rex only depends weakly on ω1eff, as is here observed for the fast-relaxing regions of AMTN25–130 (Figure 2C), indicating that the exchange is fast on the kHz time scale. A measure of the rotational correlation time that is independent of conformational exchange contributions can be obtained from the relaxation interference rates between the 15N chemical shift anisotropy and the 15N–1H dipolar terms.43 For globular proteins, these relaxation interference rates can be conveniently measured with the one-dimensional TRACT scheme;44 however, this is not feasible for oligomerized AMTN25–130, whose 1D 1H spectrum exhibits an extensive overlap between resonances corresponding to ordered and highly disordered regions. Instead, residue-specific TRACT rates can be extracted from differences between separate 2D measurements of the Hahn-echo transverse relaxation rates of the 15N TROSY and anti-TROSY components (Figure S5). These rates, measured at a 1H frequency of 900 MHz, are ca. 100 s–1, corresponding to a S2τc value of ca. 35 ns.

  The difference between R2 and R1ρ rates does not show a significant correlation with the change in 15N chemical shift between the disordered and partially oligomerized states (Figure S6; vide infra). It therefore appears plausible that the conformational exchange contribution does not reflect the formation of secondary structure but instead reports on the slower rearrangement of secondary structure elements during the oligomerization process.

  For a well-ordered protein, longitudinal 15N relaxation times of ca. 2 s at 21 T correspond to a rotational correlation time, τc, of ca. 15 ns, whereas hydrodynamic modeling predicts a rotational correlation time of several hundred ns for an oligomeric particle with a radius of 5–10 nm, observed by dynamic light scattering (Figure S1). The TRACT-derived rates correspond to S2τc ∼ 35 ns, where S2 refers to the generalized order parameter in the model-free Lipari–Szabo formalism.45 Together, the light scattering and NMR relaxation data therefore indicate that in the oligomeric state, the protein backbone retains considerable dynamic disorder and/or that the particle has not reached its final size at the short time (∼1.4 s) after the pressure drop at which the relaxation rates are measured. Our observation that the 15N T1 relaxation times in the oligomeric state remain relatively short (ca. 2 s) is consistent with the presence of substantial internal motions that contribute to J(ωN) spectral density. Pressure-jump relaxation data measured at multiple magnetic fields46 will be required for a detailed characterization of the internal dynamics of the oligomeric species. Although it would be desirable to obtain an independent measure of particle size by NMR measurement of translational diffusion of the oligomer by DOSY,47 we note that such measurements are incompatible with pressure-jumps due to the strong convection effects they induce. The pressure change creates not only movement of fluid but also a strong temperature gradient, and thereby convection, across the sample when the adiabatic compression or expansion of solvent during the jump changes the solvent temperature by ca. 2 °C, whereas the sample wall temperature remains unchanged.


### Oligomer Secondary Structure from Chemical
Shifts
  The very rapid transverse relaxation of NMR signals in the oligomeric state precludes the use of conventional 3D NMR experiments for determining site-specific chemical shift values. However, these chemical shifts can be measured by stroboscopic observation using pressure-jump NMR. This method limits the evolution of the nuclei of interest to a short, fixed duration κ of less than ∼1 ms while encoding the evolution of adjacent nuclei at high pressure and correspondingly high resolution in orthogonal frequency dimensions.48

  The stroboscopic observation method is depicted in Figure 3A, with details of the pulse schemes for measurement of 15N, 13Cα, and 13C’ chemical shifts shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S7).

  The most sensitive NMR experiment for the stroboscopic measurement of the backbone chemical shifts is the pseudo-3D HSQC-{15N} experiment, where {} brackets mark the nucleus for which chemical shifts are observed stroboscopically. For 15N, this stroboscopic element, consisting of two 90° pulses separated by κ (κ = 0.55 ms), is inserted at time το after an initial refocused INEPT magnetization transfer element has generated in-phase Nz magnetization, immediately prior to the drop in pressure from high (several kbar) to atmospheric pressure (Figure 3A; Figure S7A). This measurement probes the chemical shift evolution at time το after the pressure drop has started the oligomerization process. At the end of the κ interval, either the sine or cosine component of the transverse 15N magnetization is stored along the z axis, followed by a jump to a high hydrostatic pressure. After waiting for a duration, τd, during which the oligomers dissociate, 15N evolution and 1H detection in the standard HSQC manner yield the 15N and 1H frequencies at high resolution. The observed signal intensity is modulated by either cos(ωoκ) or sin(ωoκ), depending on the phase of the first stroboscopic 90° pulse, and the oligomer 15N angular frequency is obtained from ωo = κ–1 tan–1(Ix/Iy), where Ix and Iy refer to the intensities observed in the two steps of the phase cycle, for which signals are stored separately. A fully analogous approach yields stroboscopic observation of 13C’ and 13Cα chemical shifts but involves additional steps to transfer magnetization to and from these nuclei while the sample is at high pressure (Figure S7B,C). Under these denaturing conditions, transverse relaxation is slow, and consequently, the efficiency and sensitivity of such experiments are high.

  15N, 13Cα, and 13C’ chemical shifts at low pressure show a strong α-helical signature for Q106-L116 (Figure 3B–D), which overlaps with the E103-S115 segment for which AlphaFold2 predicted an α-helix in the monomeric state. Further, the stroboscopic chemical shift measurements for AMTN25–130 correlate closely with those measured for full-length AMTN (Figure S8), indicating that the ordered regions in the two constructs adopt very similar structures in the oligomeric state, with the terminal regions, M1-P34 and P131-Q194 of full-length AMTN remaining dynamically highly disordered. The deviations from random coil chemical shifts also point to the presence of four short β-strands, but secondary shifts for both the helical and β-strand segments are ca. 50% smaller than typically seen in secondary structure of globular proteins.50 When evaluated in terms of a generalized order parameter, S2, based on the random coil index (RCI) program,51 these chemical shifts point to considerable internal flexibility (Figure 3E). However, the presence of a mixture of highly disordered random coils and well-ordered oligomers cannot be excluded because stroboscopically observed chemical shifts are the ensemble-averaged values and do not report on the heterogeneity among different protomers. The above-noted observation that 15N T1 relaxation times remain shorter than expected for a highly ordered species favors the interpretation that residual dynamic disorder is primarily responsible for the RCI-S2 values being smaller than typically seen in globular proteins. In such a scenario, movement of intact units of secondary structure, such as the Q106-L116 α-helix, does not impact the chemical shifts or the RCI-S2 values but provides J(ωN) spectral density that shortens the 15N T1.


### Oligomerization and Dissociation Time Dependence
  Assuming that the transverse relaxation times of oligomeric AMTN are long relative to the ≤1 ms duration of the stroboscopic observation window, κ, the above measured chemical shifts report on the ensemble average of peptide chains determined 300 ms after the pressure drop that initiates the oligomerization. By shifting the position of the stroboscopic window to an earlier time point during the oligomerization delay (Figure 3A), the kinetics of the oligomerization process can be probed in a residue-specific manner by measuring this ensemble average as a function of τo during the low-pressure oligomerization interval. For the C-terminal residues, I87–F117, just 15 ms after the pressure drop, the 15N chemical shift changes relative to their random coil values have grown to ∼27% of the value seen at 300 ms and increase to ∼84% at 30 ms (Figure 4B). These results indicate rapid initial oligomerization for this region of the peptide chain, with relatively little further oligomerization occurring after 30 ms. Ordering of the N-terminal residues, S35-G48, for which small secondary chemical shifts point to β-sheet formation, appears to initiate somewhat later, with chemical shifts at 15 ms being only ∼9% of those seen at 300 ms (Figure 4C), but reaching ∼86% at 30 ms, which is comparable to those of I87–F117. These results suggest that most of the structural changes take place between 15 and 30 ms after the pressure drop but with the folding of residues I87–F117 driving the subsequent ordering of the S35-G48 segment.

  Similar to oligomerization, the dissociation of the oligomers can be monitored by stroboscopic measurements of the 15N chemical shifts. Here, the stroboscopic window is positioned 15, 30, or 300 ms after the jump to high pressure, and the measured chemical shifts are compared to those observed as part of the regular 1H–15N HSQC readout dimensions. Results indicate that the oligomers dissociate very rapidly after returning to high pressure and are essentially fully dissociated at the 30 ms time point, with only minor differences in the kinetics of residues S35-G48 and residues I87–F117 (Figure 4D–F). A root-mean-square difference of ca. 0.05 ppm between the values measured with τd = 300 ms and the direct HSQC readout chemical shifts confirms the robustness of these stroboscopic measurements.


### Structural
Information from Pressure-Jump NOE
  As applies for all dynamically disordered peptides and proteins, AMTN 1H chemical shift dispersion is poor. Therefore, amide 1H–1H NOEs are typically most easily analyzed when recorded as 15N–15N–1HN 3D NOESY spectra.52,53 In the disordered state, at high pressure, NOE buildup is slow, and only the nearest neighbor NOEs are observed (Figure 5B). However, when a period of low pressure is inserted during the first fraction of the NOE mixing period (Figure 5A, Figure S9), rapid 1H–1H cross-relaxation in the slowly tumbling oligomeric protein fraction gives rise to intense NOE cross peaks (Figure 5C). NOE contacts that extend well beyond nearest neighbors, in particular for the α-helix (Q106-L116), identified above based on chemical shifts, indicate the presence of substantial spin diffusion, despite the relatively short duration (100 ms) of the 150 ms NOE mixing period for which the sample was under oligomerizing conditions at low pressure. We note that because NOEs only build up rapidly in the oligomeric state, the NOESY experiment does not require insertion of a low-pressure delay prior to NOE mixing. Only the fraction of the NOE mixing period where the protein is oligomeric contributes significantly to cross peaks.

  The pressure-jump NOE spectrum shows a set of unambiguous long-range NOEs between the amides of F88 and S100; T90 and I98; and L92 and G96, which in combination with their secondary 13Cα and 13C’ chemical shifts point to the presence of an antiparallel β-sheet (Figure 5D, Figure S10). However, considering that the protein is oligomeric under these low-pressure conditions, these data cannot distinguish between intra- and intermolecular β-sheet formation. To resolve this issue, we performed NOE measurements on a sample with 50%/50% mixed isotopomers: U–2H/15N-AMTN25–130 and U–2H/14N-AMTN25–130. Intermolecular NOE cross peak to diagonal peak intensity ratios will be attenuated in the mixed sample relative to those in the U–2H/15N-AMTN25–130 sample at the same total protein concentration. Comparison of the corresponding strips of the 3D 15N-separated pressure-jump NOESY spectra reveals six 1HN–1HN NOE interactions that are substantially attenuated in the mixed-labeled sample (Figure 6) and are therefore likely to be of intermolecular origin. By contrast, the abovementioned NOE interactions between F88/T90/L92 and G96/I98/S100 remain of comparable intensities relative to their diagonals, indicative of intramolecular interactions.

  When additionally preparing a mixed sample for which one-half is completely unlabeled (i.e., fully protonated and natural abundance 14N), intermolecular spin diffusion during the NOE mixing time of 1HN magnetization originating from the U–2H/15N-AMTN25–130 isotopomers to spatially close, natural abundance isotopomers that carry a much denser network of dipolar coupled 1H nuclei will decrease diagonal peak signal intensities. Strong attenuation of diagonal resonances is observed for nearly all residues that become ordered at 1 bar in the 3D pressure-jump NOESY spectrum of such a mixed labeled sample, indicating that nearly the entire ordered part of the polypeptide chain is exposed to intermolecular contacts (Figure S11).


### Use of Pro Residues to Monitor Oligomerization
on Long Time
Scales
  All of the above and prior pressure-jump NMR observations relied on the slow longitudinal relaxation of 15N nuclei to shuttle magnetization between the low-pressure period, where direct NMR signal observation is not possible, and the high-pressure state, where sharp 15N and 1H signals can be observed. Aided by the increase in 15N longitudinal relaxation time upon oligomerization,9 this approach permits detailed studies of the aggregation process on a time scale of up to a few seconds. Here, we demonstrate that this time scale can be extended further by taking advantage of the much smaller peptide bond isomerization rates of the Pro residues.

  In the disordered state, cis and trans X-Pro peptide bonds are in a slow equilibrium, with the cis fraction typically substantially smaller and dependent on the residue types neighboring the Pro residue.54Cis percentages in the disordered state range from the midsingle digits to as high as 20% or more when Pro is adjacent to an aromatic residue.55,56 On the other hand, in folded proteins, a given X-Pro peptide bond generally is either 100% cis or, more often, 100% trans. Only in longer, conformationally flexible loop regions an equilibrium between cis and trans is commonly observed, sometimes with functional consequences.57,58 Therefore, we expect that any Pro residue located in a well-ordered region of the oligomeric state will be either 100% trans or 100% cis, but Pro residues located in regions that remain disordered will retain a cis fraction similar to that seen in the high-pressure disordered coil state of the protein.

  The cis fraction for each of the 11 X-Pro peptide bonds in the AMTN25–130 construct was established from the cis/trans intensity ratios of the corresponding HSQC intensities for residues neighboring each of these Pro residues, as well as from corresponding 13C’–15N cross peak intensities in 13C-detected CON spectra25,56 recorded at 2 kbar pressure (Figure 7A, Figure S12). Assignment of the resonances corresponding to cis isomers was carried out using conventional triple resonance methods and showed the same trends for the effect on backbone chemical shifts of neighboring residues (Figure S13, Table S7) as noted previously,59 with the most pronounced effect being an upfield change in chemical shift for 1HN of residue i-1, and upfield chemical shift changes for both 13C’i-2 and cis Pro 13C’i.

  For detecting the cis fraction at later times during the oligomerization process, the sample was kept at oligomerizing conditions, 750 μM AMTN25–130, 22 °C, for 2 h outside the NMR magnet. With the temperature of the NMR probe precooled to −8 °C, the NMR sample was then inserted into the probehead, and the pressure was jumped to 2 kbar immediately thereafter (Figure S14A). The activation energy for cis–trans isomerization is high, ca. 20 kcal/mol,60 and isomerization becomes very slow at temperatures below 0 °C, allowing it to be monitored by recording 1H–15N HSQC spectra. Hence, after denaturing the oligomers by high pressure at −8 °C, the cis–trans equilibrium at each Pro position was monitored by the HSQC intensities of the cis and trans isomer resonances of the immediately adjacent amides. For example, the intensity of the S35 resonance corresponding to cis Phe33-Pro34 increases from 11% to its random coil value of 21%, whereas the corresponding trans isomer resonance intensity decreases from 89 to 79% (Figure 7B). This result indicates that about half the peptide chains were fully ordered in the vicinity of Pro34 or, alternatively, that partial ordering at this location decreased the cis propensity by 2-fold. By contrast, the cis/trans intensity ratio for Thr61, reporting on the Thr61-Pro62 peptide bond, remains unchanged from its random coil value of ∼7%, indicating that this region of AMTN remains disordered in the oligomeric state (Figure 7D). The latter conclusion is consistent with the minimal increase in transverse relaxation rates and near-zero chemical shift changes for this region of the chain observed at much shorter delays after the pressure drop (Figure 2C, Figure 3). The retention of high diagonal peak intensity in the pressure-jump 3D NOESY experiment also confirms the absence of very rapid 1H–1H cross relaxation, i.e., the absence of a high degree of ordering for these residues in the oligomeric state (Figure S11B).

  The ca. 2-fold reduction in the cis conformer population seen for Phe33-Pro34 is comparable to reductions seen for Pro86 and Pro118 (Figure S14, Table S8), the latter terminating the α-helix that forms very rapidly after the pressure drop. Interestingly, Pro49 shows an increase in cis fraction upon oligomerization, which therefore is likely to cause a kinetic barrier for the 86% majority of chains that have a trans isomer in the disordered state (Figure 7C). However, the above-reported pressure-jump NOE and chemical shift observations that occur on time scales faster than 100 ms suggest that a trans isomer at Pro49 does not block oligomerization.


### Exploration of AMTN25–130 Structural Models
with AlphaFold
  Light scattering data indicate that the AMTN25–130 oligomers have, on average, diameters of ca. 13 nm, with the oligomer size of the full-length construct being about 50% larger (Figure S1). Assuming that these particles are about 50% hydrated, consistent with extensive rapid internal dynamics for a substantial fraction of residues, each oligomer contains ca. 40 polypeptide chains. Current implementations of AlphaFold-Multimer13 are not yet capable of generating atomic models for such large oligomeric species. Therefore, we searched whether AlphaFold can generate monomer and low-order oligomeric structural models that are consistent with both the intramolecular NOEs and secondary structure information derived from chemical shifts while potentially revealing intermolecular NOE contacts that are observed in the larger oligomeric species present in solution.

  AlphaFold2 monomer calculations for the segment exhibiting the highest degree of order, H83-L122, generated five structural models. While one of the models predicts a secondary structure that is consistent with the chemical shifts, intramolecular distances in the β-sheet disagree with NOEs (Figure S15). Out of many different oligomer sizes and protein segments explored by us using the AlphaFold-Multimer program, only calculations carried out for a hexameric arrangement of the H83-L122 segment yielded a model that is consistent with the intramolecular NOEs. However, several intermolecular NOEs (e.g., L85-V89 and Q91-S100; Figure 5E) are violated in this hexamer (Figure S16). Also, intermolecular NOE contacts (L38-I87, I39–I98, L41-L92, L45-F88) between residues in the N-terminal segment (L38-L45) and the β-sheet residues (F88–S100) point to their close proximity (Figure 6), but we were unable to generate AlphaFold-Multimer models for any larger segments of the protein, extending to the N-terminal region, that showed good quality packing, Ramachandran statistics, and other structurally favorable indicators while also satisfying these NOE contacts. However, the types of long-range intermolecular contacts seen in several of such AlphaFold-Multimer oligomers suggest new avenues for probing such interactions, both through site-directed mutagenesis and by mixed isotopic labeling approaches.61,62


## Concluding Remarks
  By their very nature, oligomeric protein species have large intermolecular contact areas. The high total void volume at these interfaces makes them exquisitely sensitive to dissociation by the application of hydrostatic pressure.6,33,63 Oligomerization prior to fibril formation is a characteristic property of many proteins associated with a wide range of neurodegenerative diseases,64−66 but their detailed structural characterization has remained elusive, in part because the variable size of such oligomers thwarts standard techniques such as cryo-electron microscopy and X-ray crystallography.

  Our results demonstrate that detailed atomic level structural information on oligomers can be accessed by pressure-jump NMR spectroscopy. Residue-specific 15N and 13C chemical shifts are readily measured at high sensitivity by using previously introduced stroboscopic observation technology67 and can both provide secondary structure information68,69 and assist in generating atomistic models.70,71 Importantly, the measurement of NOEs in such an oligomeric arrangement also becomes feasible, providing pivotal structural information that complements the secondary 13C chemical shifts. The crowded nature of the NMR spectrum of the intrinsically disordered state observed at high pressure, which encodes the NOEs present during oligomerization at low pressure, requires such 3D NOE spectra to be recorded at a very high spectral resolution. These measurements therefore require long recording times and a concomitant very large number (ca. 20,000) of cycles between high and low pressure. Only by extensive use of nonuniform sampling technology20,21,23 at a high level of sparsity (≤16%; SI Text) were we able to complete such measurements in less than 2.5 days on a 900 MHz instrument.

  A prerequisite for accessing structural details of the oligomeric state, in addition to chemical shift and NOE data, is that the interconversion between the oligomeric and monomeric states is fast on the time scale of 15N longitudinal relaxation (ca. 1 s). Whereas the kinetics of oligomerization usually can be increased to the required level by increasing the protein concentration and optimizing solution conditions, including ionic strength and pH, the kinetics of dissociation then can become limiting. In practice, we find that the dissociation kinetics of oligomeric species increases with hydrostatic pressure. However, with our current setup, mechanical wear of the system rapidly increases at pressures above ca. 2–2.5 kbar, which may pose practical limits for the types of oligomers that can be studied by pressure-jump NMR.

  The opportunity to probe the oligomeric state through changes in the cis–trans equilibrium of X-Pro peptide bonds is not limited by the above temporal considerations and requires only that the dissociation kinetics be fast relative to the cis–trans isomerization rates. As the dissociation takes place at high pressure where the freezing point of solvent is depressed (ca. 252 K at 2 kbar), measurements can be carried out at temperatures well below 273 K. Dissociation at low temperature takes advantage of the high activation energy (ca. 20 kcal/mol)60 of X-Pro cis–trans isomerization, slowing down the isomerization rates to less than 1 h–1 and thereby preserving the isomeric state that is present in the oligomers at the temperature where they are formed. Such measurements also remove the requirement for rapid pressure-jumps and can therefore be carried out by using commercially available equipment that relies on manually changing the pressure.
