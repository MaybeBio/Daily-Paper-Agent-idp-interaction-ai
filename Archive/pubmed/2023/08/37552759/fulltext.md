# Analysis of the complex between MBD2 and the histone deacetylase core of NuRD reveals key interactions critical for gene silencing


## Significance
  Gene regulation involves large regulatory complexes, often including intrinsically disordered proteins and protein regions. Yet, interactions involving intrinsically disordered regions have proven challenging to study. We previously established that disrupting the interaction between MBD2-IDR (methyl-CpG-binding domain protein 2-intrinsically disordered region) and the NuRD (nucleosome remodeling and deacetylase) complex blocks fetal hemoglobin silencing, a therapeutic goal for treating β-hemoglobinopathies. Here, we use AlphaFold2 to generate a model of the MBD2-IDR bound to the histone deacetylase core of NuRD, which reveals critical binding pockets that could be targets for developing molecular inhibitors. Hence, we used a machine learning structure prediction algorithm to determine a model of a complex involving a large intrinsically disordered domain that is borne out by experimental evidence.


## Results

### The Structural Propensity of the MBD2-IDR.
  The MBD2-IDR is necessary and sufficient for MBD2 to interact with the HDCC (11); however, the structure of this region, when bound to the HDCC, has proven challenging to study. In NMR experiments, 15N-HSQC NMR spectra of the MBD2-IDR (amino acids 228 to 356, Fig. 1A) have poor dispersion and very sharp amide peaks, indicating an unstructured peptide (Fig. 1B). In previous studies, chemical shift analyses showed that several short sequences in the MBD2-IDR have a propensity to form helices in isolation, suggesting that these regions could adopt a stable structure upon binding to the HDCC (11). Therefore, we decided to investigate the structural propensity of the isolated MBD2-IDR to gain insight into how the protein may fold upon binding. First, we collected solvent paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (sPRE) to measure solvent accessibility per residue. Adding a paramagnetic ion to the NMR sample enhances the proton transverse relaxation rate (1HN-R2) in a strongly distance-dependent manner (25). Solvent-exposed protein regions show increased relaxation enhancement than those protected by secondary and tertiary structure formation. The measured relaxation rate enhancement of all amide bonds in the MBD2-IDR backbone revealed segments with differing solvent accessibility (Fig. 1C). We compared the wild type (blue symbols) with the R286E/L287A double-mutant (red symbols) to detect whether this mutation disrupted the formation of any transient structure. The difference is plotted in Fig. 1C (open black symbols).

  As shown in Fig. 1C, sPRE ranges from approximately 10 to 90 s−1, varying on a residue-by-residue basis across the MBD2-IDR. In the first third of the MBD2-IDR, the wild type and mutant show similar relaxation rates, including some of the lowest and highest values we measured. The middle third contains a stretch of residues with similar relaxation rates in the wild-type (WT) protein (residues 280 to 305), which corresponds with the helical propensity of this region from NMR chemical shift indexing (11). Residues within the first of these nascent helices (280 to 292) show nearly identical relaxation rates (23 to 26 s−1), suggesting they behave as a cooperatively folding unit. The R286E/L287A mutation, located at the end of this first helical region, markedly increases relaxation rates for this region and moderately so throughout the middle and C-terminal thirds of the MBD2-IDR. Notably, the first third of the MBD2-IDR does not show any changes in solvent exposure in the mutant. However, the 2nd and 3rd regions show increased relaxation rates in the R286E/L287A mutant, indicating that this change induces more solvent exposure. Finally, the short stretch from 316 to 330 shows similar relaxation rates, consistent with its helical propensity.

  To probe whether this transient structure involves long-range interactions, we used traditional site-specific PRE (26–28). Two independent serine residues (S269 and S316), N- and C-terminal to the R286E/L287A site, were individually mutated to cysteine. We attached the paramagnetic group MTSL to these cysteine residues and measured relaxation enhancement by comparing the intensity of amide resonances in 15N-HSQC spectra between the paramagnetic and diamagnetic states. As seen in Fig. 2, site-specific labeling induced substantial relaxation enhancement near the label. In addition, relaxation is enhanced throughout the MBD2-IDR, most notably in the C-terminal half. Hence, the measured PRE indicates transient interactions between the N-terminal label (S269) and residues 300 to 308 and 325 to 330 (Fig. 2A). Site-specific labeling at S316 induces more substantial relaxation enhancement, completely line-broadening the peaks for residues 280 to 330. We then introduced the R286E/L287A double mutation and measured PRE for the same labeled sites. As shown in Fig. 2B, this mutation modestly reduces the long-range effects of the S316 label at residues 290 to 300, indicating that it disrupts the structural propensity of the MBD2-IDR in this region. Together, these results indicate transient long-range interactions surrounding the nascent helices of the MBD2-IDR in the C-terminal two-thirds of the MBD2-IDR, suggesting that this region may adopt a folded structure upon binding to the HDCC.


### Intracellular Interaction between MBD2 and the HDCC.
  To study how MBD2 associates with the HDCC complex in cells, we used a bioluminescent resonance energy transfer protein-protein interaction assay (NanoBRET). For this analysis, we cloned the donor [NanoLuc (NL)] and acceptor [HaloTag (HT)] domains at the N or C termini of various MBD2 and MTA2 constructs, respectively (Fig. 3 A–C). For constructs that included the MBD2 coiled-coil domain (CC), we incorporated the coiled-coil domain from GATAD2A (CR1) (29) as a C-terminal fusion. We have previously shown that this C-terminal single-chain construct forms a highly stable intramolecular coiled-coil complex that replicates the native interaction between GATAD2A and MBD2 and prevents interaction with the native GATAD2A (7, 11). The highest NanoBRET signal was consistently obtained when the donor was attached to the C terminus of the MBD2-IDR single-chain construct (MBD2-IDRsc, Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The associated acceptor construct that gave the highest signal consists of a C-terminal HT attached to the ELM-SANT domains of MTA2. Neither the BAH nor the zinc finger domains of MTA2 were necessary for the interaction. In addition, the R286E and L287A mutations previously shown to disrupt binding consistently reduced the NanoBRET across all the constructs we tested (Fig. 3B).

  Using the MBD2-IDRsc and MTA2-ELM-SANT constructs as the reference, we tested whether the individual R286E and L287A mutations disrupted binding. We normalized the data to the wild type (1.0) and R286E/L287A mutation (0.0) intensities to facilitate comparison across different experiments. As shown in Fig. 3D, the individual mutations (R286E or L287A) decrease binding but not to the same extent as both. Next, we successively truncated the MBD2-IDRsc from its N terminus (maintaining the same placement of the C-terminal acceptor). These truncations progressively decreased the NanoBRET signal (Fig. 3D) indicating that the MBD2-IDRsc N-terminal region is essential for binding to the HDCC.


### AlphaFold2 Generates a High-Confidence Model of the Complex between MBD2 and the HDCC.
  To study how the MBD2-IDR folds upon binding, we generated a de novo prediction of the MBD2-HDCC interaction using the AlphaFold2 machine-learning program (20, 21). Based on the well-established stoichiometry of the NuRD complex (7, 22, 23), prior structural studies of the complex between MTA1 and HDAC1 (30–32), and our initial BRET analysis, we built a model that includes two molecules each of HDAC1 and MTA2 and one molecule of MBD2. For the MBD2 sequence, we included the C-terminal single-chain construct that incorporates the coiled-coil domain from GATAD2A and the N-terminal methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD). For the HDCC, we included the ELM-SANT region of MTA2 and the entire HDAC1. This analysis produced nearly identical structures for 22 of the 25 models with a high pLDDT confidence score and low predicted alignment error for the interface between the MBD2-IDR and HDCC (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The interface pLDDT, calculated for all IDRsc residues within 4 Å of the HDCC, is 81.3, which falls within the medium to high accuracy range (33). The MBD2-IDR maintains a consistent fold with an RMSD of 1.04 Å across these 22 models. The remaining three models were the worst-ranked of the 25. These three differ in that the C-terminal portion of MBD2, including the coiled-coil domain and R286/L287 residues, do not contact the HDCC, while the N-terminal portion retains the same structure and contacts as the top 22 models (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

  Not surprisingly, the resulting model of the MTA2(ELM-SANT)- HDAC1 dimer closely matches the previously determined structure of the MTA1(ELM-SANT)-HDAC1 complex (PDB ID 4BKX, RMSD = 0.44 Å) (34). The two MTA2 ELM domains dimerize while the MTA2 SANT domains wrap around two separate HDAC1 proteins for a 2:2 stoichiometry. MBD2 binds to the MTA2-HDAC1 dimer through a large interface involving the entire MBD2-IDR and the coiled-coil domain of GATAD2A, burying approximately 5,271 Å2. The N terminus of the MBD2-IDR (237 to 279) interacts with an extended region of the HDCC surface and has minimal secondary structure formation (Fig. 4A). The MBD2-IDR extends across one of the MTA2 proteins and to the opposite side of the complex, breaking the HDCC two-fold symmetry. The MBD2-IDR 2nd and 3rd regions contact both MTA2 and HDAC1 and form a loosely collapsed domain involving residues 318 to 356. The coiled-coil domain interacts with MTA2 through residues on the exposed surface of the GATAD2A alpha-helix such that the C termini of MTA2 and GATAD2A are in proximity.

  The critical MBD2 R286 and L287 residues cap a predicted alpha-helix positioning the arginine sidechain in a pocket formed between HDCA1 and MTA2. MBD2 R286 makes a favorable salt bridge with HDAC residue D332, hydrogen bonding to the MTA S226 backbone, and the MBD2 Q280 sidechain (Fig. 4B). Additionally, MBD2 L287 makes hydrophobic interactions with both proteins of the MTA homodimer, involving MTA residues A222 and L207. Interestingly, MBD2 interacts with the symmetrically related binding pocket equivalent to the R286-binding site formed between HDAC1 and MTA2. This interaction involves MBD2 R246 (Fig. 4B), which interacts with the HDAC1 residue D332 and MTA2 residues T312 and R227.


### Mutagenesis Confirms Critical Conserved Interactions in the MBD2-HDCC Complex.
  Given that the AlphaFold2 model fits well with our previous structural and mutagenesis analysis of the MBD2-IDR, we decided to validate the model further using the in-cell NanoBRET assay to measure binding. We introduced mutations that disrupt specific interactions in the MBD2-HDCC complex (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). For these studies, we used the same MBD2-IDRsc and MTA2-ELM-SANT constructs and normalized the signals as before. First, we tested residues directly interacting with the HDCC from the N-terminal portion of the MBD2-IDR (R246, V256, and K268) and the C-terminal MBD2 coiled-coil domain (R380). Mutating each of these residues to possibly unfavorable substituents reduced the NanoBRET signal by at least 50%, with the smallest reduction in signal involving the more solvent-exposed K268. As discussed above, R246 binds in the pocket symmetrically equivalent to the one occupied by R286.

  In addition, we introduced mutations that involved the more globular region of the MBD2-IDR (S323 and F362). The S323R and F362R mutations have a modest impact on binding, whereas F362D has a more considerable effect. F362 contributes to a hydrophobic cluster formed by MBD2 residues L309, C359, and V364, and GATAD2A coiled-coil domain residues L165 and Q169 (Fig. 4B). The results suggest that this cluster can more readily accommodate the longer positively charged arginine sidechain than the negative aspartate sidechain. The S323 residue is within an alpha-helix that packs against the first alpha-helix of the MBD2-IDR. While we expected the large positively charged arginine sidechain to disrupt this packing, a potential favorable charge interaction with E319 may compensate. For comparison, we mutated three solvent-exposed residues (E374, W351, and M274), none of which significantly impacted binding as expected. Next, we mutated residues near the pocket occupied by R286 (L290 and W283R). While L290 did not have a large impact on binding, W283R had a moderate impact. The latter residue makes a potential sulfur–pi interaction with M220 of MTA2 and an anion–pi interaction with MBD2 residue E284 (Fig. 4B).

  One of the more interesting aspects of the AlphaFold2 model is that the coiled-coil domain of GATAD2A contacts MTA2 at the interface between the ELM and SANT domains. This interaction helps explain why incorporating the GATAD2A coiled-coil domain gives the largest NanoBRET signal. To disrupt this interface, we mutated residues A157 and V160 (Fig. 4B). The A157R mutation had a modest impact, while V160W did not. Notably, the V160W mutant shows a signal equivalent to the WT protein, raising the possibility that the tryptophan sidechain can maintain the same interactions with MTA2. In contrast, A157R reduces the signal by approximately 50%, supporting the role of this region in binding to MTA2.

  Based on these individual mutations, we conclude that many of the interactions identified by the AlphaFold2 model contribute to complex formation, yet no single mutation is sufficient to eliminate binding. The most considerable impact on binding involved residues R246, V256, W283, L287, and R380. Therefore, we tested a series of double mutations to identify combinations of these residues that would more significantly disrupt binding (V256R/R380A, R246D/V256R, W283R/L287A, and R246D/W283R). As shown in Fig. 5B, the W283R/L287A and R246D/W283R double mutants reduced the NanoBRET signal to that of R286E/L287A. Notably, the two most effective combinations include at least one mutation that targets the pocket occupied by R286. Moreover, the R246D/W283R double-mutation targets both symmetrically related pockets. These studies underscore the importance of the binding pocket formed at the interface between MTA2 and HDAC1. Yet, the binding interface involves many small contributions by individual residues throughout the MBD2-IDR region and coiled-coil domain of GATAD2A.


### A Complementary Mutation in MTA2 Restores Binding to Mutant MBD2-IDR.
  To further validate the model, we sought to identify a complementary mutation in MTA2 that would restore binding to a mutant MBD2-IDR. The W283R mutation disrupts a sulfur–pi interaction with M220 of MTA2. Hence, we mutated this methionine to glutamate which replaces the cation–pi interaction with a favorable charge interaction (Fig. 5C). As expected, MTA2 M220E restores interaction with MBD2-IDRsc W283R as detected by NanoBRET (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, we anticipated that MTA2 M220E might disrupt a favorable interaction with I245 of MBD2-IDRsc at the symmetrically related interface of the dimer. Hence, we introduced a second mutation, I245R, in the MBD2-IDRsc, to complement MTA2 M220E with a favorable charge interaction. As shown in Fig. 5B, the MBD2-IDRsc W283R/I245R double mutant further increases the NanoBRET signal with MTA2 M220E to a level slightly greater than the WT complex. Hence, these mutations provide strong evidence that M220 of MTA2 interacts directly with W283 and I245 of MBD2.


### Disrupting Critical Interactions between MBD2-IDR and the HDCC Abrogates Gene Silencing by the Associated NuRD Complex.
  Having identified individual and combinations of mutations in the MBD2 IDR domain that disrupt its binding to the HDCC core of the NuRD complex, as measured by NanoBRET, we next tested whether these mutations would diminish gene silencing of the γ-globin gene. We previously showed that the R286E/L287A double mutation prevents the silencing of γ-globin gene expression in a human-derived erythroid progenitor (HUDEP-2) cell line that faithfully recapitulates normal fetal hemoglobin regulation. Knockout of MBD2 (MBD2KO) in the HUDEP-2 cell line robustly induces expression of γ-globin RNA and protein (Fig. 6A) (14). Enforced expression of WT MBD2a protein in the MBD2KO cells inhibits expression of γ-globin RNA. In contrast, enforced expression of the W283R and R246D/W283R mutants fail to inhibit and slightly increase the expression of γ-globin. The MBD2 S323R mutant, which only modestly disrupts binding as measured by NanoBRET, does not inhibit silencing of the γ-globin gene. The effect of the MBD2 R246D single mutation is variable, with gene expression not significantly different from MBD2KO, and less than that of the W283R single mutation. The R246D/W283R dual mutant add-back results in the highest level of γ-globin gene expression compared to the single mutant add-backs. An immunoblot assay showed that the level of MBD2 protein in each add-back group is equivalent to or greater than the endogenous level in HUDEP-2 parental cells (Fig. 6B).


## Discussion
  NuRD plays a critical role in gene silencing during cell differentiation and development. Hence, extensive research effort has been directed toward understanding the structure of this macromolecular complex, including high-resolution crystal structures (31, 34, 35), cryogenic electron microscopy (10, 36), and NMR analyses (12, 37–39). Work by the Schwabe group has provided critical information about the structure of the MTA-HDAC complex, which plays a central role in forming NuRD (10, 30, 31, 34). In previous studies, we demonstrated that the MBD2/3 proteins form a bridge between the histone deacetylase and chromatin remodeling halves, which involves a key interaction between the intrinsically disordered region of MBD2/3 and the HDCC (11). However, the molecular details driving this interaction have remained elusive. The previous cryo-EM analysis of NuRD (10) did not have sufficient resolution to locate and provide the molecular details of the MBD2-IDR when bound to the HDCC. In unpublished studies, we have likewise been unable to identify the MBD2-IDR in cryo-EM maps of the complex with the HDCC. Hence, we decided to focus on the transient structure in the MBD2-IDR and compare that information with a model of the MBD2-MTA2(ELM-SANT)-HDAC1 complex generated by AlphaFold2.

  The results of our PRE studies agree quite well with the structural model we generated. First, the N-terminal one-third of the MBD2-IDR shows little evidence of interaction with the C-terminal two-thirds. Likewise, this same region adopts an extended structure, wrapping around the HDCC in the complex without significant secondary structure formation (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In contrast, the PRE data indicate long-range interactions involving the C-terminal two-thirds of the MBD2-IDR, which adopts a more globular structure in the complex. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S6A, the paramagnetic label on residue S316 enhances relaxation for a large backbone region (highlighted in blue and magenta). In contrast, the paramagnetic label on residue S269 is in the more extended region, enhancing relaxation for only the sequentially adjacent residues (highlighted in cyan). The R286E/L287A mutation involves the C terminus of the first helix, such that this mutation disrupts transient structure formation, which reduces the relaxation enhancement of residues 291 to 301 (highlighted in magenta). Plotting the distances between the labeled sites and backbone amides in the bound MBD2-IDR structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B) correlates with the PRE data (Fig. 2). This correlation suggests that the MBD2-IDR transiently adopts the collapsed structure in the unbound state. The MBD2-IDR does not adopt a regular structure in isolation yet appears to sample conformations with long-range contacts like the bound state. These long-range contacts involve the region of the MBD2-IDR that forms helices and a more globular structure in the complex.

  In addition, the structural model of the complex corroborates many of the previously published observations. As we described, the MBD2-IDR interacts with both sides of the MTA2 dimer, breaking the twofold symmetry and preventing a second MBD2 from binding simultaneously. Hence, this model explains the established 1:2:2 molar ratio between MBD2:MTA2:HDAC1 (22, 23). Notably, the GATAD2A coiled-coil domain makes significant contributions to the interface. The role of GATAD2A agrees with the previous observation by Low et al. (7) that in the absence of GATAD2A, two copies of MBD2 can bind to the HDCC for a 2:2:2 molar ratio. The GATAD2A coiled-coil domain stabilizes the interaction of MBD2-IDR across both sides of the dimer, thereby helping to block interaction with a second MBD2 molecule.

  Furthermore, the model places R286 and L287 in a deep pocket between MTA2 and HDAC1. This interaction, therefore, explains why the R286E/L287A double-mutation disrupts NuRD complex formation and blocks NuRD-dependent gene silencing (14, 40). We first identified this mutation based on evolutionary conservation and immunoprecipitation studies. This concordance provides strong independent support for this model of the complex. Notably, the N-terminal portion of the MBD2-IDR binds to the symmetrically related equivalent pocket on the opposite side of the MTA2 dimer. Our previous studies found that the first two-thirds of the MBD2-IDR was necessary and sufficient for binding to the HDCC as detected by immunoprecipitation (11). Consistent with that observation, the first two-thirds of the MBD2-IDR includes residues that interact with both pockets.

  To probe the structural model further, we used the NanoBRET assay to measure the interaction between MBD2 and MTA2 in cells. In our experience, this assay is robust and faithfully reports on protein–protein interactions in cells. However, the signal depends on the relative positioning of the donor and acceptor tags and the expression levels of the constructs. Hence, we placed the luciferase donor on the MBD2 protein such that we could monitor whether any of the mutations adversely affected expression levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Initial studies showed that placing the donor and acceptor tags on the C terminus of MBD2-IDRsc and MTA2 ELM-SANT constructs yielded the largest signal. This result is consistent with the structural model, which places both C-termini in proximity. For example, the C terminus of the IDRsc is approximately 30 Å and 70 Å from the C and N termini of MTA2 ELM-SANT, respectively. Likewise, when NL is on the C terminus of MBD2-IDRsc, the NanoBRET signal is more than twice the intensity when HT is on the C terminus as compared to the N terminus of MTA2 ELM-SANT (Fig. 3C). We then introduced a series of truncations and point mutations, which nearly all moderately decrease the NanoBRET signal, but no individual mutation appears to eliminate binding. This result indicates that the binding energy is distributed across the extended protein–protein interface. Importantly, we introduced a complementary mutation in MTA2 that restored binding to a mutant MBD2. This result strongly supports the model of the interaction between these residues.

  The NanoBRET experiments corroborate the importance of the symmetrically related equivalent pockets occupied by R286 on one side and R246 on the other. Double mutations involving one or both pockets largely eliminate binding. Given these results, we tested the relevance of these mutations on the function of MBD2-NuRD in gene silencing. As we described previously, blocking the interaction between MBD2 and the HDCC disrupts the silencing of fetal hemoglobin in cell models of globin regulation (11, 14). Mutating residues that bind these two pockets, W283 and R246, disrupted gene silencing. Unlike the NanoBRET results, the R246D mutation only moderately and variably disrupted silencing. However, R246 is in a region of the IDR that interacts with the C-terminal coiled-coil domain in the complex, which suggests that the mutation may destabilize binding by the C terminus and lower the NanoBRET signal without entirely disrupting the complex. Importantly, we found that mutating W283R, alone or with R246D, prevents silencing and mildly augments γ-globin gene expression. This latter result suggests that this mutant MBD2 may act as a dominant negative inhibitor of γ-globin silencing. Furthermore, it shows that the R286 pocket is critical in recruiting the HDCC and gene silencing. A molecular inhibitor targeting this region could sufficiently induce fetal hemoglobin levels for treating sickle cell anemia or β-thalassemia. Hence, our structural analysis has helped us identify mutations that block gene silencing and reveals the molecular details that drive complex formation and function.

  AlphaFold2 has successfully predicted structures of isolated domains; however, applying it to protein complexes is more challenging and does not always yield reliable models (33). Our studies generated a remarkably consistent model of the MBD2-HDAC1- MTA2 complex. We used the appropriate stoichiometry and protein domains based on experimental data for this model. The resulting structure is consistent with published observations, our PRE analysis, and mutagenesis studies. Yet, we recognize potential limitations to the model. In a recent publication, Arvindekar et al. (41) used extensive bioinformatic analyses of available data, including chemical crosslinking, to build coarse models of NuRD. The authors noted that the crosslinking data did not fully agree with their generated AlphaFold2 model. Given this observation and the absence of apparent density in cryo-EM maps of the complex, we suggest that the MBD2-IDR maintains significant dynamic flexibility when bound to the HDCC. Therefore, the AlphaFold2 model may represent only one, possibly the lowest energy state. Nonetheless, the model successfully predicts important protein–protein contacts and highlights a critical pocket involved in complex formation. Hence, we find that AlphaFold2 provides a credible model that yields unique insight into the formation of NuRD. Furthermore, AlphaFold2 provides molecular details of a dynamic complex that has eluded characterization by techniques requiring well-defined folded domains.


## Conclusions
  The MBD2-IDR is an essential component of the NuRD complex that binds to the HDCC. This interaction involves most of the MBD2-IDR, so individual mutations and small truncations throughout the region decrease binding. A molecular model of the complex shows that key residues insert into two symmetrically related equivalent pockets on opposite sides of the dimer. Mutating combinations of residues that interact with these pockets effectively prevents binding and blocks NuRD-dependent gene silencing. These data suggest that disrupting this critical interface pocket could be a viable target for drug development to treat β-hemoglobinopathies and cancer.


## Materials and Methods

### Protein Expression and Purification.
  Human MBD2-IDR (amino acids 238 to 356), and mutants, were cloned into a modified plasmid expression vector (pET32a) with N-terminal thioredoxin and hexahistidine tags and TEV protease site. The plasmid was transformed into Rosetta2 (DE3) Escherichia coli and cultured at 37 °C in M9 minimal media (15N-enriched). At an optical density at 600 nm of ~0.8, we induced protein expression with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside for 2.5 h. Pelleted cells were resuspended and lysed by sonication in 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, and complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail EDTA-free (Roche). We purified the protein by nickel affinity chromatography and TEV protease digestion overnight. The TEV protease and the thioredoxin tag were removed by a second nickel affinity purification step followed by size exclusion chromatography (Superdex-75, GE Life Sciences). Protein purity was >95%, as estimated by SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).


### Nitroxide Spin-Labeling.
  MBD2-IDR cysteine mutants (S269C and S316C) were purified as described for the WT protein. During the final protein concentration step, we added 2.5 mM DTT to fully reduce the cysteine before passing through a 7 KDa Zeba desalting column (ThermoFisher) into labeling buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl) with a 20-fold molar excess of MTSL. The reaction was allowed to proceed overnight at 4 °C before exchanging into NMR buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 6.0,10% 2H2O) for a final concentration of 300 μM protein.


### NMR Spectroscopy.
  We collected spectra of purified MBD2-IDR constructs on a Bruker Avance 850 MHz NMR spectrometer equipped with a cryoprobe. For sPRE measurements, we added 2 mM gadodiamide (Omniscan™) to the NMR sample (300 μM MBD2-IDR). 2D 15N-HSQC interleaved spectra were collected with a custom NMR pulse program and varying transverse relaxation delays as described by Kooshapur et al. (25) and Iwahara et al. (42). We measured six randomly ordered delays and fit the corresponding relaxation curves to an exponential decay function by the NMRPipe program (43). For traditional PRE, we collected a 2D 15N-HSQC spectrum of MTSL labeled protein in the paramagnetic state, reduced the label with a fivefold molar excess of ascorbate, and collected a spectrum of the diamagnetic state. We calculated the PRE as the ratio of paramagnetic to diamagnetic peak intensities. We performed all measurements in the NMR buffer.


### Plasmid Generation and Mutagenesis.
  We cloned the MBD2-IDRsc and MTA2(ELM-SANT) proteins into a NanoBRET expression vector with N- or C-terminal containing NL or HT tags, respectively, and confirmed by sequencing. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, we mutated the IDR with the Quikchange Lightning mutagenesis kit (Agilent). We acquired the lentiviral packaging plasmids psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259), lentiviral pLV203, and CRISPR plasmid LentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene #98290) from Addgene.


### HEK293T Cell Culture and NanoBRET Assay.
  Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T) were routinely cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HEK293T cells were added to a six- or twelve-well plate at 25 to 30% confluency and allowed to adhere overnight in either two or one mL of media, respectively. DNA plasmids at a 1:10 ratio of NL to HT were incubated with FuGENE HD (Promega) for 10 min according to the supplied protocol before adding to the cells and allowed to express for approximately 20 h. We resuspended the transfected cells in assay media (OptiMEM 1 with 4% FBS) and diluted them to 0.2 × 106 cells per mL before adding ligand or DMSO. We plated 100 or 50 μL in quadruplicate in a Nunc 96- or 384-well white plate, respectively (ThermoFisher). After 18 h, we added NL substrate and measured fluorescence donor and acceptor emission at 460 nm and 618 nm, respectively, on a ClarioStar plate reader (BMG Labtech). The reported mBRET was calculated as the ratio between acceptor and donor emission (x1000) and corrected by subtracting the DMSO (no ligand) control. We measured all assays in quadruplicate. For comparison between experiments, we normalized the values to WT MBD2-IDRsc and the R286E/L287A double mutant as 1.0 and 0.0, respectively.


### AlphaFold2 Model Generation.
  We generated a model of the multimeric 2:2:1 HDAC1:MTA2(ELM-SANT):MBD2sc protein complex with AlphaFold-multimer version 2.0.0 (20, 21), with a maximum template date of 01/01/2020. With a total of 1,536 residues, computation, using an A100 40GB GPU (NVIDIA) on a local computing cluster, took approximately 12 h to generate 25 models. Of note, we repeated the calculation using AlphaFold-multimer version 2.2.0 with a maximum template date of 04/17/23 which yielded essentially identical results. We visualized the models in UCSF ChimeraX 1.15 (44) and calculated the RMSD and SASA using the Biopython package (45) compared with the crystal structure (PDBID 4BKX) (34). The coordinates for the lowest-ranked model are provided in the SI Appendix, Dataset.


### Antibodies and Reagents.
  We purchased MBD2a, b, and Flag antibodies from Sigma-Aldrich (Catalog numbers M7318 and F1804), StemSpan and SFEM II media from StemCell Technologies, and recombinant human stem cell factor (SCF) and recombinant human Thrombopoietin (TPO) from R&D systems. In addition, we obtained doxycycline (DOX), dexamethasone (DEX), recombinant human insulin, heparin, and human AB serum from Sigma Aldrich and human AB plasma from Seracare. We purchased recombinant Erythropoietin (EPO) from Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) hospital pharmacy, holo-human transferrin (HTF) from Prospec, and human Flt3-ligand from PeproTECH.


### Lentiviral Preparation.
  We seeded 5 × 106 293T cells in 10-cm culture dishes with 10 mL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% HEPES, 1% nonessential amino acid (NEAA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine. After 24 h, we added fresh media without antibiotics and cotransfected the cells with 8 µg of the lentiviral expression plasmid, 6 µg of psPAX2, and 4 µg of pMD2.G using polyethyleneimine. Then, 12 to 16 h posttransfection, we replaced the media with 5 mL of DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% HEPES, and 1% NEAA. We collected viral supernatants at 48 and 72 h posttransfection and passed them through 0.45-µm sterile filters. We used freshly prepared viral supernatants to infect HUDEP-2 cells.


### Lentiviral-Mediated “Add-Back” of MBD2a in MBD2 Null Cells.
  pLV203 vectors containing sequences encoding MBD2a-TAPtag or MBD2a mutant-TAPtag were packaged as described previously (14) and used to infect MBD2KO HUDEP-2 cells. All MBD2a or mutant coding sequences contain silent mutations that confer resistance to MBD2 CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNA. We used western immunoblotting to assess exogenous MBD2 expression in HUDEP-2 MBD2KO cells.


### HUDEP-2 Cell Culture and Differentiation.
  We maintained and expanded HUDEP-2 cells in StemSpan SFEM II supplemented with 50 ng/mL SCF, 3 IU/mL EPO, 10−6 M of DEX, 1 µg/mL of DOX, 1% L-glutamine and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (expansion medium). To induce erythroid differentiation, we cultured HUDEP-2 cells for 3 d in IMDM supplemented with 3 IU/mL EPO, 1 µg/mL DOX, 10 µg/mL human recombinant insulin, 3 IU/mL heparin, 0.5 mg/mL of HTF, 5% human AB serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine (differentiation medium). We cultured cells at 37 °C in 5% CO2.
