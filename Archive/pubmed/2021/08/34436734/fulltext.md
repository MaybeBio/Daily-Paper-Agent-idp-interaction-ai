# The 1H, 15N and 13C resonance assignments of the C-terminal domain of Serpine mRNA binding protein 1 (SERBP1)


## Abstract
  SERBP1 is a multifunctional mRNA-binding protein that has been shown to play a regulatory role in a number of biological processes such as thrombosis, DNA damage repair, and the cellular response to nutrient deprivation. Additionally, SERBP1 is upregulated in glioblastoma, leukemia as well as liver, prostrate and ovarian cancers where it has been implicated in metastatic disease and poor patient outcomes. SERBP1 binds target mRNA, stabilizing and regulating the post-translational expression of the transcript. Since SERBP1 lacks canonical RNA-binding motifs such as RRM domains or zinc fingers, its target recognition and binding mechanisms are not well understood. Recent reports suggest that it is capable of recognizing both RNA sequence motifs and structured domains. Here we report the production and purification of the intrinsically disordered C-terminal domain of SERBP1, the assignment of the 1H, 13C, 15N backbone resonances of the protein by solution-state NMR, and secondary structure predictions. We show that the protein is not entirely disordered and identify an α-helix that was stable under the experimental conditions. This work is the first step toward understanding the structural basis underpinning the molecular mechanisms of SERBP1 functions, particularly interactions with mRNA targets.


## Biological context
  SERBP1 (Serpine1 mRNA-Binding Protein 1 or Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor 1 RNA-binding protein) is a multifunctional RNA-binding protein that regulates mRNA translation. It was initially identified as an interacting partner of the cyclic nucleotide-responsive sequence (CRS) found in the 3’-UTR of plasminogen activator inhibitor type-1 (PAI-1), a serine protease inhibitor that acts to prevent fibrinolysis (Heaton et al. 2001). Binding of the 134-nucleotide CRS by SERBP1 stabilizes PAI-1 mRNA transcripts and serves as a post-transcriptional regulator of PIA-1 expression (Tillmann-Bogush et al. 1999; Iwaki et al. 2012). Subsequent studies implicated SERBP1 in DNA double strand break repair through controlling the expression of the C-terminal interacting protein (CtIP) in S phase through direct binding to CtIP mRNA (Ahn et al. 2015). CryoEM structures of inactive 80S ribosomes revealed SERBP1 blocking the mRNA entrance tunnel, functioning as a global translational repressor in response to nutrient deprivation (Anger et al. 2013; Brown et al. 2018). Aside from these diverse regulatory roles, SERBP1 may be a key contributor to cell migration, invasion and the development of metastasis in hepato-cellular carcinoma (Wang et al. 2017), ovarian (Koensgen et al. 2007), breast (Serce et al. 2012) and androgen-independent prostate cancers (Guo et al. 2016) Most recently, SERBP1 was identified as a novel regulator of glioblastoma development where high expression levels correlate with chemo- and radiotherapy resistance and poor patient outcomes (Kosti et al. 2020).

  Human SERBP1 is a 408 residue protein containing an N-terminal intracellular hyaluronan binding domain (5–152) and a C-terminal hyaluronan/mRNA-binding protein domain (189–314). Two RGG boxes (163–182, 366–381) flank the C-terminal domain. Notably, SERBP1 lacks canonical RNA-recognition motifs (RRM) or other common RNA-interaction structural motifs such as zinc-fingers. The RGG boxes modulate the cellular localization of SERBP1 through asymmetrical methylation of the arginine guanidinium group (Lee et al. 2012), and are important mediators of binding to structured RNAs such as G4 quadruplexes (Takahama et al. 2011; Huang et al. 2018; Masuzawa and Oyoshi 2020). SERBP1 is predicted to be intrinsically disordered (Colleti et al. 2019), a common feature of RNA binding proteins, and appears in an extended conformation in the 80S ribosome cryoEM structures (Brown et al. 2018). We have initiated a structural study of SERBP1 to identify and describe the basis for its RNA binding and stabilization activity, and to determine if the heterogeneous nature of SERBP1 target recognition proceeds through unconventional mechanisms. Here, we report the backbone resonance assignment of a N-terminal truncation construct comprising residues 189–400.


## Methods and experiments

### 189–400 SERBP1 protein expression and purification
  The SERBP1 189–400 gene construct was amplified by PCR from a synthetic, codon optimized (for E. coli expression) gene encoding full-length (1–408) human SERBP1 that was a kind gift from Dr. Luiz Penalva. Forward (TTTCAGGGCGCCGGTAAGCGTGAGTTCGACCG) and reverse (CGCGGATCCTTAATCATCAACATCCGGAGCGC) primers were designed to introduce KasI and BamHI restriction sites, respectively. PCR products were digested by KasI and BamHI restriction enzymes and ligated into a custom vector to introduce a His8 tag and Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) cleavage site N-terminal to the protein. This strategy leaves an N-terminal two residue (Gly-Ala) scar after TEV cleavage that is not included in the numbering scheme used below. Correct cloning was confirmed by DNA sequencing. The plasmid, pAG8Ha-SERBP1(189–400), expressing His8-tagged SERBP1 189–400 was deposited in Addgene accession # 172315.

  Plasmids were transformed by heat shock into the E. coli BL21 (DE3) Star strain (Invitrogen), one colony was picked and used to inoculate a 2 mL LB starter culture supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin. The starter culture was grown for 5–6 h and 1 mL was used to inoculate 100 mL of M9 medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 15NH4Cl or 15NH4Cl and 13C6-glucose (Sigma Millipore) and grown overnight at 37 °C. The overnight culture was added to 900 mL M9 medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 1 g/L 15NH4Cl or 1 g/L 15NH4Cl and 2 g/L 13C6-glucose (Sigma Millipore), for 15N or 15N, 13C labelled samples respectively. Once the O D600 of the culture reached 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM of isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to the culture medium. After 5 h, the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation for 20 min at 3500 g and stored at −80 °C. Bacteria pellets were thawed on ice and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole) containing Complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Roche), and lysed by 12 cycles (5 s on, 55 s off) of sonication. Cell lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 45,000×g for 30 min, and the supernatant was filtered with a 0.45 μm Millex filter (MilliporeSigma) before being loaded on a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (Cytiva), equilibrated with the lysis buffer. The column was washed with 20 column volumes of 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole. SERBP1 was eluted with 15 mL of elution buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole). The sample was concentrated, and buffer exchanged to 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1% hexane-1,6-diol using Amicon Ultra 15 10 kDa centrifugal filters (Merck), followed by removal of the His8 tag using recombinant TEV (1 OD280 TEV per 100 OD280 target recombinant protein) incubated overnight at room temperature. Subsequently, the sample was cleared by centrifugation and loaded on a 5 mL HisTrap HP column to remove uncut SERBP1, TEV and the free H is8 tag. The flow-through containing SERBP1 189–400 was collected and concentrated. Size exclusion chromatography was performed as a final polishing step using a Superdex 75 16/60 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with NMR buffer (20 mM Na2HPO4 pH 7.0, 60 mM NaCl). 2 mL fractions containing 189–400 SERBP1 were collected, concentrated and aliquots were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Protein purity and integrity were confirmed by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry.


### NMR experiments
  NMR experiments were recorded at 278 K on a Bruker NEO spectrometer operating at a 1H frequency of 700.13 MHz equipped with a Bruker 5 mm TCI z-axis gradient cryogenic probe. An 80 μM 13C, 15N-labelled SERBP1 189–400 sample in 20 mM Na2HPO4 pH 7.0, 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF and 0.2 mM EDTA, supplemented with 10% D2O was used to record triple resonance experiments. 1H, 15N chemical shifts were obtained from a 2D 1H-15N HSQC recorded with 64* × 1024* complex data points in the indirect (15N) and direct (1H) dimensions respectively, corresponding to acquisition times of 37.6 and 112.6 ms. Time domain data in the 15N and 1H dimensions were apodized with a 90 degree-shifted sine function and zero filled to twice the size of the recorded matrix. Chemical shifts of the backbone carbon atoms (13Cα, 13Cβ and 13C’) were derived from an analysis of standard three-dimensional triple resonance experiments (Grzesiek and Bax 1992; Yamazaki et al. 1994) HNCO, HNCACO, CBCACONH, HNCACB, and HNHA (Bruker Topspin 4.1 pulse programs) recorded in non-uniform sampling (NUS) mode (Delaglio et al. 2017) with a sampling density of 15% for all 3D experiments. Spectra were setup with the following parameters: HNCO and HN(CA)CO, 32* × 64* × 1024* complex points in the indirect (F1, 13C), (F2, 15N) and direct (F3 1H) dimensions corresponding to acquisition times of 16.5, 37.6, 112.6 ms, respectively; CBCA(CO)NH and HNCACB, 128* × 64* × 1024* complex points in the indirect (F1, 13C), (F2, 15N) and direct (F3 1H) dimensions corresponding to acquisition times of 11, 37.6, 112.6 ms, respectively. 1Hα chemical shifts were obtained from a 3D HNHA experiment(Vuister and Bax 1993) recorded on a 100 μM 15N-labelled SERBP1 189–400 sample in the same buffer using 32* × 55* × 1024* complex points in the indirect (F1, 15N), (F2 1Hα), and direct (F3 1H) dimensions corresponding to acquisition times of 18.8, 5.7, and 106.5 ms, respectively. Water suppression was achieved through a WATERGATE pulse sequence scheme (Piotto et al. 1992). The NUS data were reconstructed using the SMILE (Ying et al. 2017) algorithm implemented in NMR-Pipe (Delaglio et al. 1995). The spectra were apodized with a shifted sine bell function and zero filled to twice the number of acquisition points for subsequent analysis. All data were processed with the tools available in the NMRPipe software suite (Delaglio et al. 1995). Visualization, analysis and resonance assignments were achieved with CCPNMR Analysis 2.5 software (Skinner et al. 2016).


### Assignment and data deposition
  Since SERBP1 lacks recognizable nucleic acid binding domains, we employed an inter-species sequence alignment and determined that the C-terminal half of SERBP1, containing the hyaluronan/mRNA binding domain was well conserved among species. We note that the sequence conservation did not extend into the first RGG repeat element (163–182) but did include the second RGG box (366–381). From this alignment we designed an expression construct that encompasses residues 189–408 for NMR studies. Mass spectroscopy and initial NMR spectra revealed that the last 8 residues of the 189–408 construct were systematically degraded, resulting in protein instability. We thus designed a shorter construct (189–400) that dramatically increased the stability of the protein.

  The SERBP1 189–400 contains 9 prolines and therefore we expect to observe 203 peaks in the 1H, 15N HSQC spectrum. Analysis of the 3D NMR data enabled the identification and unambiguous assignment of 188 residues (93%) of the 203 expected peaks (Fig. 1). Of the 15 peaks that could not be unambiguously assigned 13 were identified as arginine or glycine comprising the degenerate RGG repeat (G369-R381). Four of the resonances were classified as a glycine connected to a glycine (G369, G372, G375, G380) and were tentatively assigned to a single peak in the glycine region of the 1H, 15N HSQC (Fig. 1). The remaining four unidentified glycine residues (G371, G374, G377, G379) are the likely source for two unassigned peaks in the glycine region (Fig. 1). An additional seven peaks appear overlapped in the 1H, 15N HSQC were identified from the 3D experiments (HNCO) and are denoted with question marks (Fig. 1). These peaks likely arise from the 5 arginine (R370, R373, R376, R378, R381), one histidine (H206) and one tryptophan (W293), residues that could not be uniquely assigned due to sequence degeneracy, spectral overlap and/or signal broadening in the 13C dimension. A further four unidentified peaks appear at < 10% of the intensity of the other peaks in the 1H, 15N HSQC that could not be identified because of a lack of corresponding spin systems in the 3D experiments. These peaks, marked with asterisks, could arise from the GA cloning scar, or potentially slow conformational exchange process of yet unidentified residues (Fig. 1). We consider mutagenesis as a possible strategy to resolve the tentatively assigned glycine peaks as well as the other resonances arising from the resides comprising the RGG motif. Overall, the backbone 13Cα, 13Cβ,13C’, and 1Hα resonances were assigned at 93%, 96%, 93%, and 80%, respectively.

  Using the completed backbone resonance assignments and backbone chemical shift data, the secondary structure of SERBP1 189–400 was calculated using two different approaches (Fig. 2). First, the secondary chemical shift values, derived by subtracting the 13Cα and 13Cβ chemical shifts from corresponding random coil chemical shift values were obtained from the Poulsen IDP/IUP random coil chemical shifts web server (Kjaergaard and Poulsen 2011). The difference (Δδ13Cα–Δδ13Cβ) is an excellent indicator of secondary structure. From this analysis, most of the protein shows secondary chemical shift values Δδ around 0, suggesting no secondary structure, except for the 290–299 region, which displays strongly positive Δδ, over 3 ppm, indicative of a fully formed α-helix (Fig. 2A). These results were similar to those obtained by the SSP program (Marsh et al. 2006), an algorithm that uses experimentally determined chemical shifts and calculates their contribution to secondary structure conformations by weighting the residues propensity to participate in α- and β- secondary structure elements. SSP outputs a weighted score that can range from −1 (β-strand) to + 1 (α-helix). SSP returned a secondary structure score of at least 0.5 for residues 289–298, suggesting that 50% of the conformers are helical in this region, and over 0.7 for residues 290–297 (Fig. 2B), strongly indicating a fully formed α-helical structure. These results demonstrate that SERBP1 is not entirely disordered and that helical structure exists within the protein chain under the experimental conditions used for NMR. Further investigation will be necessary to decipher the importance of this α-helix for the function of SERBP1 and its potential involvement in mRNA recognition and binding.

  The assignments for the backbone resonances have been deposited at the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank under the Accession Number 50953.
