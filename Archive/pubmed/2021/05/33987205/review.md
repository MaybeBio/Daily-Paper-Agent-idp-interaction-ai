## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This is a competently written Mini Review that provides a broad, well-organized overview of how PTMs control RBP function and how dysregulation of these modifications contributes to human disease. The authors have selected illustrative examples that span multiple PTM types, multiple RBPs, and multiple disease categories, and they make a reasonable case for the importance of this regulatory layer. However, the manuscript is largely descriptive and does not offer a novel conceptual framework or critical synthesis that would distinguish it from existing reviews on similar topics. The scope is broad but shallow, and the lack of depth in mechanistic discussion, combined with the absence of a critical evaluation of conflicting evidence, limits its impact. For a Nature-style journal, the originality and conceptual advance are insufficient, and the review would be more suitable for a specialized or lower-tier journal.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of RNA biology, post-translational modification, and gene expression regulation would find this review useful as a concise entry point into the literature. Clinically oriented researchers studying the molecular basis of neurodegenerative diseases (ALS, FTLD) and cancer may also find the disease-focused sections relevant. However, specialists in any of the individual topics covered (e.g., HuR biology, FUS phase separation, KSRP function) would likely already be familiar with the primary literature cited, and the review does not provide sufficient depth or new perspectives to serve as a definitive reference.
- **Major strengths** The manuscript is clearly written and logically structured, progressing from general principles of RBP regulation to specific PTM effects and then to disease relevance. The selection of examples is appropriate and covers a diverse range of PTMs and RBPs. The authors make a commendable effort to highlight the interplay between different PTMs, which is an important and sometimes underappreciated concept. The concluding remarks effectively summarize the complexity of PTM-RBP regulation and point toward future research directions.

- **Minor Comments** The following comments are identified.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** No technical failings in the sense of experimental methodology are applicable, as this is a review article. However, the manuscript's reliance on secondary sources without critical evaluation, and the absence of a systematic or comprehensive literature search, limits the strength of the claims made.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - **Originality** The topic is not new, and multiple reviews on PTM-mediated regulation of RBPs and its disease relevance have been published in recent years. The authors do not present a novel hypothesis, a new synthesis of existing data, or a unique perspective. The manuscript is a summary of known findings rather than an original contribution.
  - **Scientific importance** The regulation of RBPs by PTMs is undoubtedly an important area of biology with clear disease relevance. However, the review does not elevate the importance of the topic beyond what is already established in the literature. The examples chosen are well-known, and the conclusions drawn are not surprising or transformative.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The manuscript is written for a specialized audience with prior knowledge of RNA biology and protein modifications. It is unlikely to appeal to a broad interdisciplinary readership, as the content is dense with protein names, modification sites, and disease associations without sufficient context for nonspecialists.
  - **Technical soundness** The manuscript is technically sound in that the descriptions of published findings appear accurate based on the text. However, the lack of primary data and the absence of a critical assessment of the quality or consistency of the cited studies mean that the technical robustness of the underlying evidence is not evaluated.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is readable for those with a background in molecular biology, but it assumes significant prior knowledge. Terms such as "RGG motifs," "membrane-less organelles," and "liquid-liquid phase separation" are used without adequate introduction for a general scientific audience. The figures, if provided, might help, but they are not available for assessment.
- **Recommendation posture** Not suitable for publication in a Nature-style journal in its current form. The manuscript is a competent but largely descriptive review that does not offer sufficient conceptual novelty or critical insight. It would be more appropriate for a specialized review journal. If the authors wish to strengthen the manuscript, they would need to provide a more critical and integrative synthesis of the literature, identify unresolved questions and controversies, and offer a forward-looking perspective that goes beyond summarizing known examples.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that "aberrant hyper-phosphorylation, ubiquitylation, acetylation, cysteine oxidation and caspase cleavage of TDP-43 have been related to a pathogenic behavior of this RBP in FTLD and/or ALS" is presented without critical evaluation of the strength or consistency of the evidence. The manuscript does not discuss conflicting findings or the extent to which these modifications are causative versus correlative.
- The statement that "the dysregulation of PTMs in RBPs has been associated with the pathophysiology of many different diseases" is a broad generalization. The examples provided are illustrative but not exhaustive, and the manuscript does not assess the overall strength of the evidence across the field.
- The claim that "a failure in this system was shown to enhance the pathological aggregation of this FUS mutant" (referring to the SUMO-targeted ubiquitin ligase pathway) is based on a single study and is presented without discussion of whether this mechanism is generalizable or specific to the P525L mutant.
- The manuscript states that "elevated levels of both COX-2 and cyclin-A proteins are associated to abnormal cellular proliferation" and that "Ser318-phosphorylated HuR has been proposed as a tumor marker for colon carcinoma." The evidence for this is not critically assessed, and the clinical utility of this marker is not discussed.
- The claim that "PTMs at low-complexity IDRs (e.g., arginine methylation of RGG regions) of some phase-separating RBPs such as FUS strongly influence the formation and characteristics of condensates" is presented as a general principle, but the manuscript does not discuss the quantitative or mechanistic details of how methylation alters phase behavior.
- The manuscript's statement that "the study of the post-translational regulation of this class of proteins could provide a better understanding of disease-related dysregulation" is a reasonable suggestion but is not supported by a specific research agenda or testable hypotheses.
