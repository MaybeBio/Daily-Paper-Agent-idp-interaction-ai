# A Data-driven Hydrophobicity Scale for Predicting Liquid-Liquid Phase Separation of Proteins


## Abstract
  An accurate model for macroscale disordered assemblies of biological macromolecules such as those formed in so-called membraneless organelles would greatly assist in studying their structure, function and dynamics. Recent evidence has suggested that liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) underlies the formation of membraneless organelles. While the general mechanism of exchange of macromolecule/water for macromolecule/macromolecule interactions is known to be the driving force for LLPS, the specific interactions involved are not well understood. One way that protein-water and protein-protein interactions have been understood historically is via hydrophobicity scales. However, these scales are typically optimized for describing these relative interactions in certain cases, such as protein folding or insertion of proteins into membranes. In order to better describe the relative interactions of proteins which undergo LLPS, we have developed a new, data-driven hydrophobicity scale. To determine the new scale, we used coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations using the hydrophobicity scale coarse-grained model, which relates the interactions between amino acids to their hydrophobicity. Hydrophobicity values were determined via the Force-Balance method, on a library of proteins which includes unfolded, intrinsically disordered, and phase separating proteins (PSP). The resulting hydrophobicity scale is able to better predict whether a given protein will undergo LLPS at physiological conditions using coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations than existing hydrophobicity scales. This new scale confirms the importance of π-π interactions between amino acids as important drivers of LLPS. This new hydrophobicity scale provides a convenient and compact description of protein-protein interactions for proteins that undergo LLPS and could be used to develop new models to describe interactions between PSP and other components, such as nucleic acids.


## Introduction
  Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) has emerged an area of interest in recent years due to its importance to cellular compartmentalization and the formation of membraneless organelles.1,2 Membraneless organelles are believed to regulate many biological processes such as stress response,3,4 chromatin organization,5 cellular signaling,6 and viral replication.7 Furthermore, proteins known to undergo LLPS have been found in brain inclusions associated with certain neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).8–11 A frequently observed feature in LLPS is the presence of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), or proteins with substantial intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), whose lack of a specific three-dimensional structure allows for a high degree of multivalancy in the interactions with themselves and other proteins or nucleic acids.3,12–14 Certain types of interactions have been proposed as drivers for LLPS such as electrostatic interactions, π-π interactions, and hydrophobic interactions.15–17

  Several theoretical and computational models have been developed to understand the driving forces of LLPS and how changes in primary sequence affect LLPS. Lin and Chan modeled electrostatics with the random phase approximation and other interactions by Flory-Huggins theory to predict critical temperature based on charge patterning.18 McCarty et al. were able to predict full phase diagrams by a field-theoretical approach.19 The sticker and spacers framework20 has been used to describe the role of non-electrostatic interactions in LLPS for prionlike domains.21 However, these methods in general lack the ability to describe the general sequence dependence on LLPS by lacking both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions simultaneously and residue-specific interactions, although some progress in this direction has recently been made.22 More generally, it is more difficult to treat systems containing both IDRs and folded regions using a purely theoretical approach. On the other hand, detailed all-atom molecular simulations of phase separation are still limited in the length and time scales that are accessible, although they provide very high resolution models of structure and dynamics.23–25

  Recently, an explicit coarse-grained molecular simulation framework has been developed that includes both residue-specific interactions and simultaneous electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, known as the Hydrophobicity Scale (HPS) model.26 This model uses a representation of one bead per residue, allowing for inclusion of sequence dependence, while also being amenable to simulations on large time and length scales. It relates the strength of short-range interactions to an existing hydrophobicity scale27 and the strength of electrostatic interactions to the total charge of a residue via screened electrostatics. Using a hydrophobicity scale to describe interactions between residues can be motivated in several ways. Firstly, it has been shown that the dominant eigenvectors of statistical pair interaction matrices are often correlated with hydrophobicity. In particular the Miyazawa-Jernigan pair interaction matrix for residue-residue interaction energies can be accurately reconstructed using only a vector of hydrophobicities.28 Secondly, mean charge and mean hydrophobicity have previously been used to predict whether a protein will be intrinsically disordered.29 The HPS model, along with the slab simulation methodology,30 has been successful at generating phase-diagrams of LAF-1 and the low-complexity domains of the protein FUS (Fused in Sarcoma).26 It has been used qualitatively to describe changes in LLPS as a result of mutations,31 the relation between single chain collapse and phase separation,32 and the effect of valency on LLPS.26,33 Similar insights have been obtained from a recent phenomenological lattice model.34

  In spite of its utility, the original HPS model is not sufficiently accurate to quantitatively predict the phase separation propensity of specific sequences under physiological conditions or features such as the critical conditions for phase-separation to occur, as we disucss below. This lack of predictive power suggests that the hydrophobicity scale chosen may not be optimal. Like other hydrophobicity scales in the literature, it was derived based on specific assumptions or definitions of the meaning of hydrophobicity, which are typically derived for specific domains of application.35,36 Indeed, there is considerable diversity amongst published scales for protein residue hydrophobicity, depending on their method of construction37

  There is a long history of study of hydrophobicity, driven by its importance as a driving force in biological self assembly processes, foremost amongst them being protein folding.38 From a theoretical perspective, hydrophobic forces for small, hard sphere solutes have been studied through the free energy for cavity formation within bulk water.39–42 Hydrophobic attractive forces among larger solutes has been described in terms of dewetting of the solute surface.43–46 Empirically, hydrophobicity scales have been determined in a number of ways, including from water-octanol partitioning,47 lipid bilayer insertion propensity,35 calculations of accessible surface area,48,49 chromatographic techniques,50 physical properties,36 or machine learning.51 The multitude of techniques has lead to over 100 different hydrophobicity scales, often without a strong correlation among them.52

  In this paper, we seek to derive a new hydrophobicity scale which is able to predict phase separation properties of a protein based on primary sequence. First, we derive a new hydrophobicity scale by optimizing the hydrophobicity parameters against the radius of gyration of unfolded and intrinsically disordered proteins. The resulting hydrophobicity scale is much better able to predict the single-chain properties of disordered and unfolded proteins than the hydrophobicity scale used in the original HPS model,26 but still fails to predict the propensity of proteins to phase separate, suggesting that the sequences present in the data set are not representative of certain interactions important to LLPS. We therefore perform a second fit in which the data set is augmented by a set of phase-separating proteins. The resulting fit is able to predict whether a protein will phase separate at ambient conditions with much higher accuracy than the original HPS hydrophobicity scale, as well as a variety of hydrophobicity scales described in the literature. The data suggests that the new hydrophobicity scale can be used to provide insight into important interactions of phase-separating proteins and to predict phase separation in a similar to prediction of disorder in previous studies.


## Methods

### Hydrophobicity scale (HPS) model
  Proteins were simulated using the Hydrophobicity Scale (HPS) model.26 This is a one-site per residue coarse-grained polymer model which relates interactions between residues to a hydrophobicity scale which is scaled from 0 to 1. Each residue has an associated hydrophobicity parameter, λi, which modulates the attractive part of a Lennard-Jones potential:  UHPS=λΦLJ,ifr≥σ21/6ΦLJ-(1-λ)ϵ,otherwise   (1) ΦLJ=4ϵσr12-σr6  The model utilizes a global ϵ of 0.2 kcal/mol. The sigma used is given in table S1. An arithmetic mixing rule is used to calculate λ and σ for unlike residues. Electrostatic interaction are modeled using a Coulombic term with Debye-Hückel screening to take into account charge screening from the ions:  (2) Eij=qiqj4πϵ0Drexp-r/κ  where κ is the screening length, ϵ0 the permittivity of free space and D the dielectric constant of the medium, here taken to be 80. The value of κ was set to 1.0 nm, corresponding to approximately 100 mM ionic strength, close to physiological conditions. All bonds have an equilibrium bond length of 3.8 Å and a spring constant of 2.39 kcal mol−1.Å−2.


### Optimization Procedure
  In order to better capture interactions necessary to describe liquid-liquid phase separation, the hydrophobicity parameters were optimized using a procedure inspired by the Force Balance approach53,54 In our approach, hydrophobicity parameters are iterated to create agreement with the simulated and experimental radius of gyration. As such, an objective function, Δ2, given by the square differences of the radii of gyration for the test set proteins is used:  (3) Δ2=1N∑n=1NRgn-Rgn¯2  where Rgn¯ indicates the experimental value for the radius of gyration for the protein n. We chose Δ2 as the optimization target rather than χ2,55 because of the difficulty of quantifying the error from the different experiments used, as well as for consistency with approach used for for phase-separating proteins, discussed below. Gradients are determined by taking the derivative of the objective function with respect to the hydrophobicity parameters,  (4) gi=dΔ2dλi=2N∑n=1NRgn-Rgn¯dRgdλi   (5) =2N∑n=1NRgn¯-RgnRg∂U∂λin-Rgn∂U∂λin  (in the above expressions U is understood to be in units of kBT, where kB is the Boltzmann constant and T the temperature). Once the gradient is computed, the hydrophobicity parameters are updated using gradient descent  (6) λi,j+1=λi,j-ηgi  where j represents the iteration number and η is the learning rate.

  One difficulty of including phase separating proteins into the above optimization is the difficulty in measuring their properties as isolated chains in experiments, owing to their tendency to self assemble. The lack of such single-chain measurements is circumvented by only calculating gradients for those phase-separating proteins which the model predicts to be more expanded than the theta condition. This is accomplished by adding to Δ2 a term ΔPSP2 given by  ΔPSP2=Rg-bN0.52,ifRg≥bN0.50,otherwise  for each protein in the PSP set, where N is the number of peptide bonds of the protein and b is a commonly used prefactor for unfolded or disordered protein chains (0.55 nm).56,57

  A protein was determined to phase separate if its Flory exponent ν was greater than 0.5. The Flory exponent was estimated by fitting the equation  <Rij2>|i-j|=δij1/2=bδijν,  in which Rij is the distance between residues i and j, the average is performed over all residue pairs in which the sequence separation of i and j is δij, and the prefactor b was set to 0.55 nm.

  Since the problem is under-determined, L2 regularization is used to limit the maximum drift of the parameters. This is accomplished by including an additional penalty term to the objective function  (7) Δreg2=Δ2+∑i=120γλi,j-λi,HPS2  where γ is the regularization strength factor and λHPS are the hydrophobicity parameters from the zeroth iteration (i.e. the original Kaptcha-Rossky parameters). An arithmetic mixing rule was utilized to determine λ for interactions between unlike residues. A subsequent optimization, starting from the end of the previous optimization, was performed where λ for unlike terms were fit directly instead of determined by mixing rules.


### Simulation Details
  All simulations were performed using the MD software LAMMPS.58 The systems were simulated using a timestep of 10 fs and used a non-bonded cutoff of 10.0 Ångstroms. Electrostatic interactions had a cutoff of 30.0 Angstroms. Proteins were initially configured in an alpha helical conformation. The system was equilibrated for 100 ns with the Langevin thermostat at 300K with a friction coefficient of 1 ps. Statistics were collected for a further 500 ns under the same conditions. Observables were calculated using the PLUMED plug-in,59 version 2.4.4.60


### Choice of Experimental Data
  Experimental data for the dimensions of unfolded and intrinsically disordered protein monomers was extracted from the literature, creating a “non-PSP” data set. Care was take to select sources where the original data was of high quality and where the analysis had been performed using methods that are unlikely to give systematic errors.61 For this purpose, SAXS data analyzed via recent methods such as ensemble fitting,62 the molecular form factor (MFF) method63 or extended Guinier analysis57 were used. These methods use a broader range of scattering angles to estimate the radius of gyration, and so are less sensitive to the noisy data at small scattering angles than traditional Guinier analysis. For FRET, interpretation using a fixed distance distribution function leads to artifacts.62 We therefore reanalyzed all FRET data using the SAW-ν model, which allows the form of the distribution function to adjust to the degree of collapse of the chain.56

  For the phase separating proteins, sequences were taken from the LLPSDB,64 to create a “PSP” data set. Although the LLPSDB database contains 536 distinct sequences, many of these are closely related. We reduced the number of sequences by choosing sequences which were entirely disordered (i.e. no folded domains), resulting in the set of 18 sequences which we split into training and validation sets.

  Each of the PSP and non-PSP data sets were then further split into training and validations sets.

  In Supporting Tables S1 and S2 we give details of each data set, and sources for the original data, while in Tables S3–S6 we provide their full sequences.


### Slab Simulations
  A slab simulation was performed on the HBP-pep sequence to validate the prediction of phase separation directly. The protocol is described in detail elsewhere,26,30 but briefly, 100 chains of HBP-pep were placed in cubic box of 10 nm sides. An initial NPT simulation with a temperature of 150K and a pressure of 1 atm was run until the box volume stabilized, about 100 ns. The z-dimension of the box was then extended to length of 70 nm. The system was then run using Langevin dynamics, ramping the temperature from 150K to 300K over 100 ns. Langevin dynamics at 300K were then run for 1μs, saving coordinates every 100 ps and removing the first 1000 frames. Equilibration was assessed by comparing density profiles for the first and second halves of the simulation.


## Results and Discussion

### Hydrophobicity scales
  In the original version of the HPS model,26 the hydrophobicity scale chosen was based on the atomistic scale proposed by Kapcha and Rossky,27 after performing averages over each residue. This was used to set the hydrophobicity parameters λ, after normalization to a [0, 1] scale. An optimization of the model’s global ϵ was then performed to approximately match the Rg to experimental values for a selected set of unfolded proteins and IDPs. This original model was successful at the qualitative description of phase-separation by the slab simulation methodology, notably predicting the effect of phosphomimetic mutations.31 It was also used to show that that there was a close correspondence between the Θ-temperature for collapse of a single protein chain (Tθ) and the critical temperature for phase separation (Tc), a result that was known to be true only for homopolymers.32 However, it was lacking in quantitative accuracy, for example the upper critical temperature of the intrinsically disordered region of LAF-1 to be ~ 160 K, although this protein is known to phase-separate at room temperature. While a coarse-grained model with a temperature-independent potential such as this may not capture the full temperature dependence of the phase diagram, it should ideally be able to predict whether proteins phase separate for the temperature it is parameterized for (i.e. ~ 300 K)

  In this work, we set out to refine the HPS model by optimizing the associated hydrophobicity scale with the goal of more quantitatively reproducing phase separation phenomena under ambient conditions. This would allow the model to be used in a more predictive fashion. We have exploited the fact that the collapse of a single protein chain is driven by the same types of protein-protein interactions driving homotypic phase separation, which underlies the above observation that Tc≈Tθ. Thus matching the radius of gyration of single protein chains to experimental data ought to be a good target for parameterizing a model for LLPS. However, it is critical to use reliable experimental data for this purpose: notably, it has recently been shown that esimating Rg from either single-molecule FRET or small-angle X-ray scattering requires some care in the data analysis.61,63,65,66 We therefore developed a carefully curated set of high quality estimates of Rg based on different types of experimental data (Tables S1 and S2) for intrinsically disordered or unfolded proteins in the absence of chemical denaturant. Note that none of these examples undergo phase separation under conditions near physiological temperature and ionic strength – making the experimental characterization of their Rg more straightforward. We therefore refer to this data set as “non-PSP”.

  We first evaluated the performance of the original HPS model on the non-PSP data set by comparing the predicted and experimental radii of gyration for our data set. In Figure 1a, the original HPS model using Kaptcha-Rossky hydrophobicities systematically underestimates the radius of gyration (which was also evident in the original parameterization26). A seemingly straightforward solution to this issue would be to modify the model’s global ϵ to optimize agreement with the data set, or to find an existing hydrophobicity scale that better predicts the radius of gyration. For instance, the agreement can be improved by reducing the global epsilon of the original HPS to 0.1 kcal/mol (Figure 1b) or by utilizing the Kyte-Doolittle hydrophobicity scale normalized to span the interval [0, 1] (Figure 1 c) and ϵ=0.2kcal/mol. Both of these corrections result in apparently promising models that predict protein compactness with a lower mean squared error than the original model.26

  However, these changes have the unfortunate side effect of reducing the model’s ability to predict phase-separation. In Figure 1 d–f, a protein’s Rg as a function of chain length is given along with reference lines showing the scaling expected for the poor-solvent limit (ν=1/3), the Θ-solvent limit (ν=1/2), and the good-solvent limit (ν≈0.588). By utilizing the observation of Dignon et al. that a protein’s critical temperature for phase-separation is closely approximated by the θ-temperature, defined here as where the Flory exponent ν is equal to 1/2, and the approximate scaling relation Rg(N,ν)=bNν, an IDP’s phase-separation propensity can be determined from whether it lies above or below the θ-temperature guide-line Rg(N,1/2). This allows for a protein’s phase-separation propensity to be estimated from a single chain simulation instead of a slab simulation,32 where the protein-rich phase is constructed containing many copies of a protein and then the box length is extended allowing the proteins to transfer the protein-rich and protein-poor phases. In Figure 1 d for the Kapcha-Rossky hydrophobicities, dots lie on both sides of the θ-temperature line indicating that some proteins phase separate while others do not. However, the model misclassifies many of the proteins. This is in contrast with fig1.e and f in which the model was optimized to reproduce Rg for disordered proteins, where all the dots lie above the θ-temperature line. These results seem to indicate an apparent trade-off between compactness prediction and phase-separation prediction. We postulated that this trade-off may be due to these hydrophobicity scales being parameterized with folded proteins in mind instead of disordered proteins, and that interactions important for stabilizing the phase separated state of IDPs may have been under-represented. We set out to produce a new hydrophobicity scale that can simultaneously predict compactness and phase-separation propensity.


### Optimization
  In order to create a model within the HPS framework that simultaneously describes compactness and phase-separation, a new hydrophobicity scale must be determined. Given the number of parameters that need to be optimized, an automatic and systematic approach must be adopted. We have chosen the Force Balance method described by Wang et al.53,54 since it allows for corrections to a model’s observables without adding additional parameters. This is advantageous since the resulting hydrophobicity could possibly be used in other contexts. The radius of gyration was chosen as an experimental target for the optimization of the new model. This was chosen because of the above mentioned connection between the conditions for collapse of a single chain and critical conditions for phase separation.

  We found that in order to achieve this goal it was essential to include in the training data both disordered proteins that phase-separate (PSP) and those that do not (non-PSP). To illustrate this, we performed optimizations utilizing a training set that only includes non-PSP and another training containing only PSP. In Figure 2, panel a shows the correlation plot of the validation set following a training that excluded the PSP from the training set (table S1). This optimization reduces the validation error for Rg prediction to values similar to what is seen using the Kapcha-Rossky scale with ϵ=0.1kcal/mol or the Kyte-Doolittle scale (Δ2=0.1921 and Δ2=0.2328, respectively). However, like those scales, this optimization also fails at predicting phase-separation. One the other hand, the exclusion of non-PSP from the training set results in a model that systematically underestimates the protein size, while still reducing the validation error from 1.4732 to 0.7249.

  Our final training set included both disordered proteins that did phase-separate and those that did not. The radius of gyration for each of these proteins was calculated either from SAXS measurement data or FRET measurement data. A collection of 17 non-PSP and 4 PSP were included in the training set (table S1). Since we desired to leave some of the PSP out of the training set to cross-validate our model, there is a much greater proportion of non-PSP within the training set. This was counteracted by weighting the PSP by a factor of 4.25 so that the relative contributions from the two groups to the optimization are equal.

  One problem that arises when one wants to use PSP within a training set is the lack (in most cases) of single-chain data for them. This lack of data is likely because of experimental challenges such as the need to avoid phase separation and still have sufficiently high concentrations to make measurements using SAXS, although some recent advances using in-line purification techniques have made started to make this possible.4 In order to include these PSP without knowledge of the single-chain radius of gyration, we assume that they must have Rg less than that for a chain under Θ-solvent conditions, assuming that that also marks the critical point for phase separation. We achieve this by adding a half-parabola term for their Rg to the optimization target, so there is no lower limit on their Rg.

  We optimized the hydrophobicity parameters, starting from the KR parameters, using gradient descent with gradients based on the Force Balance method. The optimization reaches a minimum error at iteration 23 (Figure S2), and the new hydrophobicity parameters (termed “FB-HPS”) were taken from that iteration. The resulting training error was similar to the training error found on the non-PSP optimization (Figure 2a), and was smaller than the error found on the training set when using the Kyte-Dolittle scale or the Kapcha-Rossky scale with ϵ=0.1kcal/mol. In Figure 3, the resulting validation errors can be seen. While the set of hydrophobicity parameters determined from Force-Balance resulted in a significantly smaller validation error than the original Kapcha-Rossky scale, the validation error on the non-PSP data set was slightly greater than the error on the same set using either the Kyte-Doolittle scale or the Kapcha-Rossky scale with ϵ=0.1kcal/mol. The validation error for the PSP data set is also greater than the non-PSP validation error. This suggests that a trade-off occurs upon the inclusion of phase-separating proteins into the training set. This could be due to an insufficiently representative training set, but also could be due the limited of resolution of the model.

  Regularization runs were also performed to test if the optimization was leading to over-fitting to the training set (Figure S2). It was determined that a regularization factor of 0.01 gave a fit which had the same training error as the unregularized fit. This regularized optimization resulted in a similar validation error to the unregularized run. Furthermore, the resulting hydrophobicity parameters were similar to those found in the unregularized optimization (Figure S3), suggesting that no over-fitting is occurring in the optimization.


### A New Hydrophobicity scale for Phase separating proteins
  The new hydrophobicity parameters’ ability to predict phase-separation is visualized in Figure 3. The optimization with a training set that includes both non-PSP and PSP proteins corrects five false positives observed with the original HPS model, and seven false negatives. One false positive still remains in the validation set, hTIA1. Additionally one new false negative is introduced by this optimization, the fused in sarcoma (FUS) protein, however both of these are very close to the dividing line indicating ν=1/2.

  A greater understanding of the important interactions within PSP can be gained by analyzing the new hydrophobicity parameters, plotted in Figure 4. Of the seven residues whose hydrophobicities increase (ASN, GLY, ARG, TYR, HIS, PHE, TRP), six are known to have side-chains which donors for π-π interactions. It has also been suggested by Vernon et al. that glycine may contribute π-π interactions via the backbone of the protein.67 Given the relatively large increase in the hydrophobicity for GLY, the role backbone π-π interactions may be larger than previously considered, consistent with collapse previously inferred for polyglycine chains.68,69 This seems to confirm the hypothesis provided that π-π donor interactions play an important role among PSP.

  Among the remaining residues whose hydrophobicity parameters decrease, SER, VAL, PRO, THR, and LEU decrease significantly. Most of these residues contain either a hydroxyl group or a hydrocarbon side chain. Given the large amount of SER and GLN in FUS, it is possible that the hydrophobicity of these residues is underestimated by the model. Notably, many of the residues whose hydrophobicity parameters decreased contain hydrocarbon side-chains.

  In the last panel of Figure 3, the FB-HPS hydrophobicities are compared (on a normalized scale) to a selection of other hydrophobicity scales. In general FB-HPS follows the trend of the other scales. Interestingly, it is most similar to the Wimley-White scale, derived from propensity to be at membrane interfaces,35 although still with some important differences. There are more significant differences from the Kyte-Doolittle scale, probably the most commonly used hydrophobicity scale, which was based on amino acid burial in folded proteins as well as hydration free energies.36 In particular the aliphatic side-chains appear more hydrophobic and aromatic side-chains less hydrophobic in the Kyte-Doolittle scale, relative to FB-HPS. Some of this difference may have to do with which hydrophobic interactions are most favorable as isolated residue-residue contacts (as most likely the case in LLPS), versus those which are most favorable in a dense hydrophobic environment, surrounded by many other hydrophobic groups. The planar structure of the aromatic and sp2-hybridized groups whose interactions are associated with LLPS may facilitate a better packing of those groups in pairwise interactions, where it would be harder to effectively pack an isolated pair of aliphatic side-chains. This is consistent with the increased abundance of π-π contacts associated with increased solvent exposure in folded protein structures.67

  As a demonstration of the utility of the newly determined hydrophobicity scale, the mean net charge and mean hydrophobicity were calculated utilizing the some hydrophobicty scale. Uversky has shown that folded proteins tend to be more hydrophobic and have less net charge than IDPs.70 To this a end, a useful hydrophobicty scale for PSPs should be able to differentiate between proteins that do and do not phase separate based on similar criteria. In Figure 5, the mean hydrophobicity and mean net charge for top8000,71 representing folded proteins and disprot,72,73 representing all intrinsically disordered proteins are plotted, together with a selection of PSP proteins from the LLPSDB64 that contain no folded domains. Firstly, the PSP data set clearly has lower net charge per residue than either folded or disordered proteins in general, a property which obviously favors phase separation. However, the Kaptcha-Rossky or Kyte-Doolittle hydrophobicities suggest that the phase-separating proteins have similar, or even slightly lower average hydrophobicity than the general set of disordered proteins from disprot – this explains why HPS models based on these hydrophobicities failed to predict phase separation even which Rg for non-PSP disordered proteins were well predicted.

  The rightmost panels of Figure 5 demonstrates that the FB-HPS predicts relatively higher hydrophobicities for PSP than for folded and intrisically disordered proteins, favoring their collapse as single chains in the HPS model. While it may seem counterintuitive that phase separating disordered proteins could be more “hydrophobic” than folded proteins, it is in fact consistent with the experimentally observed properties of isolated chains: disordered proteins that do not phase separate, or the unfolded states of foldable sequences (i.e. before they fold) tend to be close to the boundary between collapsed and expanded states, i.e. the so-called Θ-state with scaling exponent ν≈1/2,61–63,65,66,74,75 rather than being collapsed. This is in contrast to those phase-separating IDPs for which it has been possible to determine Rg for isolated molecules: these proteins have been shown to be more collapsed, with ν<1/2.4,21,63 It should be pointed out that using mean hydrophobicity as a proxy for collapse propensity is a simplification. Firstly, a mean field average over all possible pairs of residues would be expected to be better than a simple sequence average, and secondly sequence order matters, as can be captured by metrics such as SHD.22 Of course, all these effects are naturally included in simulations with the HPS model, which could also easily be adapted to incorporate folded and disordered domains in the same sequence.76

  Since all of our predictions of phase separation have been based on collapse of single chains, we performed a further validation using explicit simulations of phase separation on HBP-pep, a member of our PSP validation set. This peptide represents a good candidate for validation since it was not in the training set and was incorrectly predicted not to phase separate when using the original HPS model, and predicted to phase separate with FB-HPS. Furthermore, we would expect the Tc≈Tθ assumption based on theory for infinite chains to be the least valid on this peptide since it is a short chain of 25 residues. This peptide also has a positive net charge. In Figure 6, density profiles and simulation snapshots of the slab simulation are shown. The Kapcha-Rossky HPS model predicts a relatively uniform density of peptide throughout the box, while the FB-HPS predicts protein-rich phase density more than ten times greater than the protein-poor phase.


### Optimization of Off-Diagonal Interactions
  While the use of a hydrophobicity scale with mixing rules can be advantageous due to its compact nature, it makes the assumption that the full matrix of residue-residue interactions can be accurately constructed via a mixing rule. If this were not the case, the mixing rules could cause stronger interactions between certain residues than exist in reality (or vice versa). For instance, we have proposed that the increase of certain hydrophobicities is due to previously underestimated role of π-π interactions. However by increasing the hydrophobicities of π-π donors, the mixing rules will also increase the interaction among residues whose hydrophobicities remain the same but aren’t π-π donors. A solution to this issue is to optimize the entire interaction matrix instead of just the diagonal elements.

  To test this, an optimization was done in a similar fashion to the optimization of the diagonal elements, but all 210 interactions were adjustable instead of the 20 diagonal interactions. The optimization was started from end of the previous diagonal optimization. The resulting training error is only marginally reduced (Figure S5). The cross-validation error was also marginally reduced and is still greater than the cross-validation error for both the KR with ϵ=0.1 and the Kyte-Doolittle scale. This minimal improvement of the model considering the large increase in the number of parameters seems to indicate that the use of per-residue hydrophobicities with mixing rules is a reasonable approximation for generating all pair interactions, as previously suggested.28


## Conclusion
  We have determined a data-driven hydrophobicity scale for phase-separating proteins using the Force Balance method. In combination with hydrophobcity scale coarse-grained model, the FB-HPS is able to both predict both the degree of collapse of proteins and IDPs, and their phase-separation propensity. This new hydrophobicity scale is also able better describe phase-separation propensity through mean hydrophobicity, suggesting FB-HPS may also be of use in bioinformatic studies of PSP.

  While the 1-site per residue resolution currently employed has the advantage of computational efficiency and is is able to capture the properties of both PSP and non-PSP sequences, the model’s lack of resolution likely limits its expressiveness. In particular, the lack of distinction between backbone and sidechains of the residues limits the power of the model to distinguish different types of interactions. The relatively large increase in the hydrophobicity of the glycine residue in the FB-HPS scale suggests a likely important role for the backbone in interactions driving phase separation. A possible future direction would be to apply a similar optimization strategy to the one in this study to a two-site per residue model with (at least) a backbone and side-chain bead similar to the SOP-IDP model.77 Not only would this model likely be able to reproduce experimental results with higher fidelity, it could also directly elucidate the role of side-chain-backbone and side-chain-side-chain interactions.

  Possible future applications of this model could include the study of coacervates of mulitple types of proteins and of coacervates of RNA and proteins.7 The role of RNA-protein interactions has recently become an area of interest, though the driving forces of such interactions are an area of active investigation.78 Another future development of this model would be the inclusion of an explicit model for salt. It has recently been shown that Hofmeister effects modify the critical behavior of LLPS.17 Given that Hofmeister effects are thought to be a result of exchange of water-protein to ion-protein interactions, or hydrophobic effects, it seems natural that a model designed to capture a similar effect with protein-water and protein-protein interactions could potentially capture these effects.
