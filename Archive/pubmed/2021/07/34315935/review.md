## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, introduction, results, conclusions, methods) for the article "Size conservation emerges spontaneously in biomolecular condensates formed by scaffolds and surfactant clients" by Sanchez-Burgos et al., published in Scientific Reports (2021).
- **Assessment boundary** Evaluation is limited to the content provided in the manuscript text. Supplementary Information is referenced but not provided; its contents are therefore not directly assessable.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors use coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of a minimal patchy-particle model to show that binary mixtures of high-valency scaffold proteins and low-valency surfactant client proteins can form multiple coexisting condensates that maintain a conserved size, in contrast to pure scaffold systems which form a single growing droplet. They propose that surfactant-induced reduction of surface tension, combined with preferential surfactant localization at the interface, thermodynamically stabilizes this multidroplet emulsified state.
- **Visible evidence base** Results from Direct Coexistence (DC) simulations across three box geometries (Table 1, Fig. 2), surface tension measurements as a function of composition (Fig. 1c, Fig. 3), density and composition profiles (Fig. 2 bottom panels, Fig. 4a), and local binding-site engagement analysis (Fig. 4b). Methodological details for phase diagram calculation, surface tension, and binding-site analysis are stated to be in the Supporting Information, which was not provided.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** The Supporting Information document, which contains methodological details for phase diagram calculations, surface tension determination, and the local order parameter for binding-site analysis, was not provided. This limits verification of several technical procedures. Additionally, no simulation trajectory data, convergence tests, or finite-size scaling analyses are shown in the main text.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a conceptually interesting and potentially useful simulation study addressing a relevant question in the biology of biomolecular condensates: how can cells maintain multiple condensates of controlled size rather than one coalesced droplet? The authors employ a well-established minimal patchy-particle model and demonstrate, through systematic variation of box geometry and composition, that surfactant-like client proteins can stabilize multiple coexisting droplets of conserved size. The central idea, that surface-active clients can thermodynamically stabilize emulsified condensate states, is plausible and aligns with existing experimental observations of non-coalescing condensates. However, the manuscript as presented has several limitations that temper enthusiasm. The evidence is entirely computational and based on a highly simplified model; the connection to specific biological systems is largely speculative. The main text omits key methodological details that are relegated to the Supporting Information, making the work difficult to fully evaluate. The claim of "spontaneous emergence" of size conservation is supported by the simulations, but the thermodynamic analysis is somewhat superficial, and the manuscript would benefit from a more rigorous free-energy treatment to substantiate the proposed mechanism. Overall, this is a solid but incremental contribution that may be of interest to the computational biophysics community, though its broader biological impact is not fully established from the provided evidence.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in the fields of computational biophysics, soft matter physics, and the emerging area of biomolecular condensates and phase separation will find this work of interest. The study provides a mechanistic, simulation-based framework for understanding how condensate size can be regulated, which is a topic of active experimental investigation. The results may also appeal to those studying surfactant behavior in biological systems and to theorists developing coarse-grained models of intracellular organization. Experimentalists studying condensate biology may find the predictions testable, though the model's simplicity limits direct quantitative comparison.
- **Major strengths**
  1. The study addresses a timely and biologically relevant question: the mechanism of size control in biomolecular condensates.
  2. The use of a minimal, well-characterized patchy-particle model allows for systematic exploration of parameter space and clear identification of the physical determinants (valency, surface tension, composition).
  3. The demonstration that box geometry can be used to probe the system's ability to accommodate different surface-to-volume ratios is a clever and effective methodological approach.
  4. The observation of surfactant accumulation at the interface, with reduced binding-site engagement, provides a clear microscopic picture supporting the proposed mechanism.
  5. The manuscript is clearly written and the figures are generally informative.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the multidroplet state is "thermodynamically stable" and represents the equilibrium state of the system under the given conditions.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section "Size-conserved condensates can be regulated by surfactant proteins", Fig. 2, Table 2; location not provided for specific simulation times.
  - **Concern** The assertion of thermodynamic stability is a strong claim that requires rigorous evidence. The authors state that they "simulated over sufficiently long timescales to allow for multiple droplet fusion events and variations in droplet composition," but no quantitative data on simulation timescales, convergence criteria, or free-energy calculations are provided in the main text. The observation that droplets do not coalesce over the simulated time does not prove thermodynamic stability; it could indicate a kinetically trapped metastable state. A proper demonstration of thermodynamic stability would require, for example, calculation of the free energy as a function of droplet size and number, or demonstration that the system reaches the same equilibrium state from different initial conditions (e.g., starting from a single large droplet and showing it spontaneously splits into multiple droplets).
  - **Why it matters** The central claim of the paper is that size conservation emerges spontaneously as a thermodynamically favored state. If the observed multidroplet state is merely metastable or kinetically trapped, the biological relevance of the finding is substantially weakened, as cellular timescales may not permit equilibration. The distinction between thermodynamic stability and kinetic trapping is fundamental to the interpretation of the results.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should provide free-energy calculations (e.g., via umbrella sampling or thermodynamic integration) as a function of the number of droplets and their sizes, or demonstrate reversible transitions between single-droplet and multidroplet states by varying the surfactant concentration or temperature. They should also report the total simulation times in reduced units and show that the observed states are independent of the initial configuration.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Technical soundness
  - **Claim pointer** The authors claim that the surface tension of the condensate decreases with increasing surfactant concentration, and that this reduction is the key driver of the size-conserved multidroplet state.
  - **Evidence pointer** Results section, Fig. 1c (bottom panel) and Fig. 3; location not provided for the surface tension calculation method.
  - **Concern** The surface tension values are presented, but the methodology for their calculation is not described in the main text and is relegated to the Supporting Information. The standard method for computing surface tension in planar interfaces (via the pressure tensor) may not be directly applicable to the curved, small droplets studied here, where the Laplace pressure and curvature effects can be significant. The authors acknowledge that curvature effects are not considered, but this is a potentially important omission. If the surface tension is size-dependent (as expected for small droplets), the interpretation of the measured values and their role in stabilizing specific droplet sizes becomes more complex.
  - **Why it matters** The proposed mechanism hinges on the quantitative relationship between surfactant concentration, surface tension, and the resulting equilibrium droplet size. If the surface tension measurements are not reliable for the small droplets in question, the quantitative predictions of the model are questionable. The neglect of curvature-dependent surface tension could alter the predicted size distribution of droplets.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should describe the surface tension calculation method in detail, including how it was adapted for small droplets. They should also test the sensitivity of their results to curvature effects, for example by comparing surface tensions of planar interfaces with those of droplets of different sizes, or by using a method that accounts for the curvature dependence.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Scientific importance
  - **Claim pointer** The authors propose that their findings "shed light on the roles of protein connectivity, binding affinity, and droplet composition" in regulating condensate size, and suggest relevance to biological systems such as the nucleolus.
  - **Evidence pointer** Abstract, Introduction, Conclusions; location not provided.
  - **Concern** The connection between the model results and specific biological systems is made only in general terms. The model uses a highly simplified representation of proteins as patchy particles with fixed valency and isotropic interactions. Real proteins involved in condensate formation, such as those with intrinsically disordered regions, exhibit much more complex interaction landscapes, including sequence-specific effects, post-translational modifications, and dynamic binding. The authors do not discuss how their findings might translate to these more realistic scenarios, nor do they provide quantitative predictions that could be directly tested in experiments. The claim of biological relevance is therefore somewhat overstated relative to the evidence presented.
  - **Why it matters** For a broad readership, the significance of the work depends on its relevance to understanding biological phenomena. If the model is too abstract to make contact with experimental systems, the impact of the work is limited to a narrow computational audience. The authors should either strengthen the biological connection by discussing specific experimental systems and making testable predictions, or temper their claims of biological relevance.
  - **Resolution test** The authors should identify specific experimental systems where their predictions could be tested (e.g., in vitro reconstitution assays with controlled protein valency) and provide quantitative predictions (e.g., scaling of droplet size with surfactant concentration) that could be compared with experiments. Alternatively, they could discuss how their model could be extended to incorporate more realistic protein features.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established**
  - R1-M1: Lack of rigorous evidence for thermodynamic stability of the multidroplet state (no free-energy calculations, no demonstration of reversibility).
  - R1-M2: Inadequate description of surface tension calculation methodology and neglect of curvature effects, which are potentially important for small droplets.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria**
  - **Originality** The idea that surfactant-like client proteins can stabilize multiple coexisting condensates of conserved size is a novel contribution to the field. While the concept of surfactants in biology is not new, its application to the regulation of condensate size in the context of scaffolds and clients is a fresh perspective.
  - **Scientific importance** The question of how cells regulate the size and number of condensates is of significant current interest. The proposed mechanism, if validated, could have implications for understanding cellular organization. However, the highly simplified model and lack of direct experimental connection limit the immediate importance.
  - **Interdisciplinary readership** The work is likely to appeal primarily to computational biophysicists and soft matter physicists. Biologists may find the model too abstract, and the lack of experimental validation or clear experimental predictions will limit the broader appeal.
  - **Technical soundness** The simulation methodology is based on a well-established model, and the Direct Coexistence approach is appropriate. However, the lack of detail on surface tension calculations and the absence of free-energy analysis for the central claim of thermodynamic stability are significant technical weaknesses.
  - **Readability for nonspecialists** The manuscript is generally well-written, but the use of physics terminology without sufficient explanation may hinder accessibility for a biology-oriented audience. The figures are helpful but could be better annotated.
- **Recommendation posture** Currently not established from the provided evidence. The central claim of thermodynamically stable, size-conserved multidroplet states is plausible but not rigorously demonstrated. The technical concerns regarding surface tension calculations and the lack of free-energy analysis must be addressed. The biological relevance of the findings is also not sufficiently developed. I would be supportive of the work if the authors can provide stronger evidence for thermodynamic stability, address the technical concerns, and more clearly articulate the connection to biological systems.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the multidroplet state is "thermodynamically stable" is not fully supported by the evidence presented in the main text; the distinction between thermodynamic stability and kinetic trapping is not addressed.
- The claim that the findings "shed light on the roles of protein connectivity, binding affinity, and droplet composition" in biological systems is speculative, as the model is highly simplified and no direct experimental comparison is made.
- The statement that the observed behavior is "a consequence of the thermodynamic conditions of our system" is not a mechanistic explanation and is therefore not a supported claim.
- The quantitative relationship between surfactant concentration, surface tension, and droplet size is presented, but the reliability of the surface tension measurements for small droplets is not established, making the quantitative predictions uncertain.
