## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (Abstract, Introduction, Results, Discussion, Physiological implications, Funding, Disclosures, Author Contributions, Acknowledgements)
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific content, experimental design, data interpretation, and claims as presented in the provided text. Figures and supplemental materials are referenced but not directly examined.
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors propose that α-synuclein (αSyn) interacts with the three mammalian VDAC isoforms with markedly different kinetics (up to 100-fold differences in on-rates and equilibrium constants), and that this isoform specificity arises from differential interactions at the VDAC protein–lipid interface, not from differences in channel conductance, selectivity, or voltage gating. They further propose that cysteine residues, particularly in VDAC3, modulate αSyn binding, and that this isoform-specific interaction represents a previously unrecognized step in VDAC inhibition by αSyn, with implications for isoform-specific therapeutics.
- **Visible evidence base** Electrophysiological recordings of recombinant VDAC isoforms (WT and cysteine-less mutants) reconstituted into planar lipid membranes; comparison of on-rates, off-rates, and equilibrium constants; MD simulations of VDAC1 and VDAC2 in lipid bilayers; prior published work on VDAC2 substates and αSyn–VDAC interaction.
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Figures (including Figure 4E data points and error bars), Supplemental Figures S1–S3, MD simulation details (force field, simulation time, system setup), statistical analysis details, and raw data for kinetic parameters. The manuscript text alone does not permit independent verification of the quantitative claims.

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript addresses a significant and timely question: how do highly homologous VDAC isoforms achieve functional specificity? The use of αSyn as a sensitive kinetic probe is creative and well justified. The central observation—that on-rates and equilibrium constants differ by up to two orders of magnitude across isoforms despite nearly identical channel properties—is potentially important. However, the manuscript as presented has several weaknesses that prevent full confidence in the conclusions. The mechanistic model (isoform-specific interaction at the protein–lipid interface) is plausible but not directly tested; the role of cysteines is inferred from mutant comparisons without structural or biochemical validation; and the physiological extrapolations, while interesting, are speculative. The MD simulations are mentioned but not described in sufficient detail to assess their contribution. The writing is generally clear but the manuscript would benefit from a more rigorous quantitative framework and a clearer separation of established results from speculation.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers in mitochondrial biology, membrane protein biophysics, and neurodegeneration (particularly Parkinson's disease) would find this work relevant. The finding that VDAC isoforms differ dramatically in their interaction with αSyn despite similar channel properties could inform studies on isoform-specific mitochondrial regulation and may have implications for understanding αSyn toxicity. The proposed protein–lipid interface interaction mechanism would interest biophysicists studying membrane protein–lipid interactions. The potential for isoform-specific therapeutics targeting VDAC would appeal to drug discovery researchers.
- **Major strengths** 1) The comparative approach across three isoforms is systematic and well controlled, using the same lipid composition and experimental conditions. 2) The use of αSyn as a kinetic probe is elegant and leverages prior work from the same group. 3) The finding of up to 100-fold differences in kinetic parameters despite nearly identical channel properties is striking and likely to be of broad interest. 4) The inclusion of cysteine-less mutants provides a genetic handle on the molecular determinants of isoform specificity. 5) The MD simulations, while briefly described, add a structural dimension to the mechanistic proposal.
- **Major Concerns**
  - **Concern ID** R1-M1
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** Yes
  - **Axis** Evidence sufficiency
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that "the αSyn-induced blockage exhibits up to 100-fold differences in on-rates and equilibrium constants" between isoforms, and that this reflects isoform-specific interaction with the protein–lipid interface.
  - **Evidence pointer** Figure 4D–F; Supplemental Figures S1–S2; location not provided for raw data
  - **Concern** The quantitative basis for the 100-fold difference claim is not fully transparent. The on-rates are stated to differ by up to 100-fold, but the exact values, error bars, and statistical significance for each isoform at each voltage are not provided in the text. The equilibrium constants are derived from on-rates and off-rates, but the propagation of errors and the number of independent experiments (n) are not stated. Without these details, the reader cannot assess whether the differences are robust or whether they might be influenced by experimental variability, particularly given the acknowledged high standard deviations for VDAC2 samples. Furthermore, the claim that this difference arises from the protein–lipid interface is inferred from the observation that channel conductance and selectivity are similar across isoforms, but this is an indirect argument. The MD simulations are mentioned as supporting evidence, but the simulation details (system composition, force field, simulation length, analysis methods) are not provided, making it impossible to evaluate the validity of the structural conclusions.
  - **Why it matters** The central conclusion of the paper rests on the quantitative reliability of the kinetic measurements and the interpretation that the differences are due to protein–lipid interface interactions. If the kinetic differences are not robustly established, or if alternative explanations (e.g., differences in protein stability, aggregation state, or membrane partitioning) are not excluded, the mechanistic model is not supported.
  - **Resolution test** Provide full kinetic data (on-rates, off-rates, equilibrium constants) for each isoform and mutant at all voltages tested, with n values, error bars, and statistical tests. Include a detailed description of the MD simulations (system setup, force field, simulation time, convergence criteria, and analysis of lipid–protein contacts). Ideally, provide direct experimental evidence for the proposed protein–lipid interface interaction, such as mutagenesis of the interface residues (e.g., Glu73/Glu84/Gln73) or lipid composition dependence of the kinetic parameters.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M2
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Mechanistic interpretation
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that cysteine residues, particularly in VDAC3, modulate αSyn binding, and that the effect is due to cysteines on the loops at the IMS side of the channel.
  - **Evidence pointer** Figure 5A–F; Figure 6; Supplemental Figure S2; location not provided
  - **Concern** The interpretation of the cysteine mutant data is not fully convincing. For VDAC3, the on-rate increases ~10-fold upon cysteine removal, and the authors attribute this to cysteines on the IMS-facing loops. However, the manuscript does not provide a mechanistic explanation for how these cysteines, which are not in the pore or at the membrane interface, could affect αSyn capture. The authors speculate that the cysteines may act as redox sensors, but no redox manipulations were performed. For VDAC2, the effect is smaller (~5-fold) and of borderline significance, and the authors acknowledge the high variability. The lack of a clear structure–function relationship weakens the claim that cysteines are a primary determinant of isoform specificity. Additionally, the authors do not consider alternative explanations, such as differences in protein folding, stability, or membrane insertion efficiency of the cysteine-less mutants.
  - **Why it matters** The cysteine mutant data are used to support the model that isoform-specific interactions at the protein–lipid interface are mediated by specific residues. If the cysteine effects are not mechanistically understood or are confounded by other factors, the model is weakened.
  - **Resolution test** Perform redox experiments (e.g., oxidizing and reducing conditions) to test whether the cysteine effect is redox-dependent. Provide structural or computational evidence for how the IMS-facing cysteines could influence αSyn capture. Alternatively, perform mutagenesis of specific cysteine residues (rather than all cysteines) to identify which ones are responsible. Include control experiments to rule out effects of cysteine removal on protein stability or membrane insertion.
  - **Concern ID** R1-M3
  - **Severity** Major
  - **Blocking** No
  - **Axis** Physiological relevance
  - **Claim pointer** The claim that "the less efficiently blocked VDAC3 remains open for respiratory substrate transport, while VDAC1 remains blocked by αSyn" and that this differential interaction explains isoform-specific physiological roles.
  - **Evidence pointer** Section 4 (Physiological implications); location not provided
  - **Concern** The physiological extrapolation is speculative and not directly supported by the data presented. The in vitro electrophysiology experiments use purified recombinant proteins in artificial lipid bilayers, which may not reflect the complex environment of the mitochondrial outer membrane. The authors cite their previous work on VDAC isoform knockout cells, but no new cellular or in vivo data are presented here. The claim that VDAC3 is "the most important VDAC isoform for respiration" is based on the assumption that lower αSyn blockade translates to higher respiratory flux, but this is not tested. The authors also do not address the fact that VDAC3 is the least expressed isoform in most tissues, which would limit its overall contribution to mitochondrial metabolism regardless of its interaction with αSyn.
  - **Why it matters** The physiological implications section is likely to attract broad interest, but if the extrapolations are not clearly labeled as speculative, readers may overinterpret the findings. The manuscript would be strengthened by a more cautious framing of the physiological relevance.
  - **Resolution test** Either provide direct cellular or in vivo data supporting the differential regulation of VDAC isoforms by αSyn, or clearly label the physiological implications as speculative and discuss alternative interpretations. Consider discussing the quantitative contribution of VDAC3 given its low expression levels.

- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 1) The quantitative kinetic data (on-rates, off-rates, equilibrium constants) for all isoforms and mutants at all voltages are not fully presented in the text; the reader is referred to figures that are not available for inspection. 2) The MD simulation methods are not described, making it impossible to evaluate the structural claims. 3) The statistical treatment of the data (n values, error bars, tests) is incomplete. 4) The manuscript does not provide a clear definition of the "protein–lipid interface" interaction or how it is distinguished from other steps in the model.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** Originality: The comparative kinetic analysis of αSyn across three VDAC isoforms is novel and extends prior work from the same group. The proposal of an isoform-specific interaction at the protein–lipid interface is a new mechanistic idea. Scientific importance: The findings could have implications for understanding isoform-specific mitochondrial regulation and αSyn toxicity, which is relevant to Parkinson's disease. However, the physiological relevance is not directly demonstrated. Interdisciplinary readership: The work bridges membrane biophysics, mitochondrial biology, and neurodegeneration, which could attract readers from multiple fields. Technical soundness: The electrophysiology approach is well established, but the incomplete presentation of quantitative data and the lack of MD simulation details undermine confidence. Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is generally well written and accessible, but the mechanistic model and the significance of the kinetic differences could be explained more clearly for a broad audience.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The central observation of isoform-specific αSyn kinetics is potentially important, and the proposed mechanism is interesting. However, the manuscript as presented does not fully establish the quantitative basis for the claims, and the mechanistic model is not directly tested. The authors should provide complete kinetic data, detailed MD simulation methods, and a more cautious framing of the physiological implications.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that the isoform-specific interaction occurs at the protein–lipid interface is inferred from indirect evidence (similar channel properties, MD simulations) and is not directly tested experimentally.
- The claim that VDAC3 is "the most important VDAC isoform for respiration" is speculative and not supported by the data presented in this manuscript.
- The claim that cysteine residues on the IMS-facing loops of VDAC3 mediate the isoform-specific interaction is not mechanistically explained and is based on a single mutant comparison.
- The physiological implications section extrapolates from in vitro electrophysiology to cellular function without direct experimental support.
- The statement that the findings "open a new paradigm" and "guide a new strategy in the search for VDAC-targeting drugs" is overstated given the speculative nature of the mechanistic model.
