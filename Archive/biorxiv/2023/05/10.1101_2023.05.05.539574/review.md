## Review setup
- **Input scope** Full manuscript text (abstract, main text, methods, supplementary materials list) from bioRxiv preprint
- **Assessment boundary** Scientific validity, methodological soundness, claims vs. evidence, clarity, and suitability for a high-impact generalist journal
- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors present DeepAssembly, a domain-based assembly protocol for multi-domain protein and complex structure prediction. The method combines domain segmentation, single-domain structure prediction (via an AlphaFold2 extension with PAthreader templates), a deep learning network (AffineNet) that predicts inter-domain interactions, and a population-based assembly algorithm. Claims include improved inter-domain accuracy over AlphaFold2 on multi-domain proteins, ability to correct low-confidence AlphaFold database structures, and applicability to protein complex prediction using interactions learned from monomers.
- **Visible evidence base** Main text figures (Figs 1–8), Supplementary Tables 1–5, Supplementary Figures 1–6, Methods section, two case studies (ERdj5, sEH, complement factor B), complex prediction benchmark (247 heterodimers)
- **Missing materials affecting confidence** Supplementary figures and tables referenced but not provided in the submitted material; no detailed benchmark composition for the 247 heterodimer set; no comparison with AlphaFold-Multimer on the same heterodimer set with matched inputs; no runtime/memory benchmarks; no statistical significance tests reported; no ablation for the population-based assembly component alone

## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The manuscript addresses a timely and important problem: improving multi-domain protein structure prediction, which remains a weak point of current end-to-end methods. The core idea, using a dedicated deep learning model to predict inter-domain interactions and then assembling high-quality single-domain models, is sensible and potentially valuable. The results on the 219 multi-domain test set show modest but consistent improvements over AlphaFold2, and the case studies are illustrative. However, the evidence for the central claims is incomplete in several respects. The comparison with AlphaFold2 is not fully controlled (different single-domain models are used), the complex prediction results are presented without a strong baseline comparison, and the claimed generality of inter-domain interaction transfer from monomers to complexes is supported by limited evidence. The manuscript would benefit from clearer benchmarking, additional statistical analysis, and a more critical discussion of failure cases.

- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Computational structural biologists, protein prediction method developers, and researchers using AlphaFold database structures for multi-domain proteins or complexes. The work is also relevant to those interested in domain-level assembly strategies as a lightweight alternative to end-to-end prediction, and to experimentalists seeking to correct or improve low-confidence structural models.

- **Major strengths** 
  1. The problem addressed is important and clearly articulated: multi-domain protein and complex prediction remains challenging despite AlphaFold2's success on single domains.
  2. The method is well-motivated by a divide-and-conquer strategy, leveraging the strength of single-domain prediction while focusing deep learning efforts on the inter-domain interface.
  3. The use of affine transformations to represent inter-domain interactions is a novel and potentially powerful representation.
  4. The demonstration that interactions learned from monomers can be applied to complexes is conceptually interesting and supported by at least one clear example.
  5. The potential application to updating low-confidence AlphaFold database structures is practically valuable.

- **Major Concerns**

- **Concern ID** R1-M1
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Experimental design / fairness of comparison
- **Claim pointer** "On average, the models built by DeepAssembly achieve a TM-score of 0.916 and RMSD of 3.12 Å, which are both better than 0.900 and 3.58 Å of AlphaFold2."
- **Evidence pointer** Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 1
- **Concern** The comparison between DeepAssembly and AlphaFold2 is not controlled for the quality of single-domain models. DeepAssembly uses single-domain models generated by an extended AlphaFold2 with PAthreader templates, whereas the AlphaFold2 comparison presumably uses full-length AlphaFold2 predictions. The observed improvement could be partly due to better single-domain models rather than the assembly strategy itself. The authors do test a "DeepAssembly (AF2 domain)" variant, but the comparison is not fully disentangled.
- **Why it matters** Without a controlled comparison, the specific contribution of the inter-domain interaction prediction and assembly algorithm cannot be isolated. The central claim of the paper is that the assembly approach improves multi-domain prediction, and this requires a fair comparison where the only difference is the assembly strategy.
- **Resolution test** Provide a comparison where AlphaFold2 full-length predictions are compared to DeepAssembly using the same single-domain models (e.g., AlphaFold2-predicted domains) and where the only difference is the assembly algorithm. Report TM-score, RMSD, and inter-domain orientation errors for both.

- **Concern ID** R1-M2
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** Yes
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency for complex prediction claim
- **Claim pointer** "We apply DeepAssembly for the prediction of 247 heterodimers. We find that DeepAssembly generates models with acceptable quality (DockQ ≥ 0.23) for 32.4% of the dimers, suggesting a lighter way to assemble complex structures by treating domains as assembly units and using inter-domain interactions learned from monomer structures."
- **Evidence pointer** Fig. 5, Methods (Data collection)
- **Concern** The complex prediction results are presented without a comparison to a state-of-the-art complex prediction method (e.g., AlphaFold-Multimer) on the same test set with matched inputs. The authors mention AlphaFold-Multimer only in the context of a single example (4Q7J). The claim that DeepAssembly provides a "lighter way" is not supported by runtime or memory benchmarks. Additionally, the 32.4% success rate is modest, and it is unclear how this compares to expectations for this benchmark.
- **Why it matters** The claim that inter-domain interactions learned from monomers can be applied to complexes is a strong and interesting one. However, without a proper baseline comparison and resource usage analysis, the practical value of the approach for complex prediction is not established.
- **Resolution test** Run AlphaFold-Multimer on the same 247 heterodimers with the same input data (or a matched subset) and report DockQ distributions, success rates, and runtime/memory usage for both methods. Discuss cases where DeepAssembly succeeds and AlphaFold-Multimer fails, and vice versa.

- **Concern ID** R1-M3
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Statistical rigor
- **Claim pointer** "DeepAssembly has an improvement of 74.2% compared to RoseTTAFold (SR = 18.6%)" and "DeepAssembly improves accuracy by 11.8% for 164 multi-domain structures with low confidence deposited in AlphaFold database."
- **Evidence pointer** Fig. 5a, Fig. 4a
- **Concern** No statistical significance tests are reported for the observed improvements. Given the modest differences in some cases (e.g., TM-score 0.916 vs 0.900), it is unclear whether these improvements are statistically meaningful. The improvement on the AlphaFold database subset (11.8%) is reported as an average, but the distribution of improvements and the number of cases with substantial improvement are not shown.
- **Why it matters** For a method to be adopted, it is important to know not just the average improvement but also the reliability of the improvement across diverse targets. Statistical testing would strengthen the claims.
- **Resolution test** Perform paired statistical tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) on TM-score and DockQ differences between DeepAssembly and baselines. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals. Show the distribution of per-target improvements.

- **Concern ID** R1-M4
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Generalizability / benchmark composition
- **Claim pointer** "Experimental results show that on 219 multi-domain proteins, the average inter-domain distance precision by DeepAssembly is 22.7% higher than that of AlphaFold2."
- **Evidence pointer** Fig. 8b, Supplementary Table 3
- **Concern** The 219 test set is derived from the DEMO dataset, which may have specific characteristics (e.g., domain types, difficulty levels) that do not represent the general population of multi-domain proteins. The authors do not provide an analysis of the diversity of the test set or the distribution of results across different domain architectures. The improvement in inter-domain distance precision is reported as an average, but it is unclear how consistent this improvement is across different types of multi-domain proteins.
- **Why it matters** The generalizability of the method is a key question. If the test set is biased toward certain types of multi-domain proteins, the reported improvements may not translate to other targets.
- **Resolution test** Provide a breakdown of results by domain count, domain size, and functional class. Discuss any categories where DeepAssembly does not improve over AlphaFold2. Consider testing on an independent, more diverse set of multi-domain proteins.

- **Concern ID** R1-M5
- **Severity** Major
- **Blocking** No
- **Axis** Methodological detail / reproducibility
- **Claim pointer** "DeepAssembly uses a population-based evolutionary algorithm to assemble multi-domain proteins by inter-domain interactions inferred from a developed deep learning network."
- **Evidence pointer** Methods (Population-based domain assembly)
- **Concern** The description of the population-based assembly algorithm is brief. Key parameters (population size, mutation rate, crossover scheme, convergence criteria) are not specified. The initialization step mentions random rotation angles for four linker residues, but the rationale for this choice and the sensitivity of the results to these parameters are not discussed.
- **Why it matters** Reproducibility is a core requirement for a methods paper. Without sufficient detail, other groups cannot implement or fairly evaluate the method.
- **Resolution test** Provide a complete description of the algorithm, including all parameters and a sensitivity analysis for the most critical ones. Consider releasing the code and a configuration file.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that DeepAssembly provides a "lighter way" for complex assembly is not supported by runtime or memory benchmarks.
- The claim that inter-domain interactions learned from monomers can be applied to complexes is supported by only a few examples and a modest success rate (32.4%), without a strong baseline comparison.
- The improvement over AlphaFold2 on the 219 test set is not statistically tested, and the contribution of the assembly algorithm vs. improved single-domain models is not fully disentangled.
- The generalizability of the method to diverse multi-domain proteins is not established, as the test set composition is not analyzed in detail.
- The claim that DeepAssembly can improve low-confidence AlphaFold database structures is based on a single test set of 164 proteins, and the practical significance of the improvement (11.8% TM-score increase) is not discussed in terms of downstream utility.
