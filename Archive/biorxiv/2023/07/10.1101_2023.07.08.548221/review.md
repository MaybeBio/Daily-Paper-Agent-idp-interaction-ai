## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** The abstract presents a potentially significant advance in understanding α-carboxysome assembly by identifying specific conserved residues in a disordered protein that mediate shell interactions, and by establishing an in vitro reconstitution system. However, the abstract alone provides insufficient evidence to evaluate the robustness of the claims. The identification of VTG and Y as key residues is plausible but lacks mechanistic detail, and the reconstitution claim requires validation of fidelity to native carboxysome structure and function. The work is likely of interest to the carbon fixation and synthetic biology communities, but the current evidence base is too limited to assess technical soundness or reproducibility.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Researchers studying bacterial microcompartments, carbon fixation biochemistry, intrinsically disordered proteins in scaffolding roles, and synthetic biologists interested in engineering carboxysomes for heterologous CO2 fixation. The in vitro reconstitution approach may also appeal to those developing minimal cell-free systems for studying protein self-assembly.
- **Major strengths** The study addresses a fundamental open question about how a disordered protein can serve as an architectural scaffold. The identification of specific conserved residues (VTG and Y) provides a concrete molecular handle. The in vitro reconstitution approach is a potentially powerful tool that could enable mechanistic dissection and future engineering applications.

### Major Concerns

- **Concern ID** R1-M1  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Evidence sufficiency  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims that "highly conserved and repetitive residues, VTG and Y, contribute to the interaction between CsoS2 and shell proteins" and are "necessary for building functional α-carboxysomes in vivo."  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; location not provided  
- **Concern** The abstract provides no experimental data demonstrating that mutation or deletion of VTG and Y residues abolishes or impairs carboxysome assembly or function. No mutagenesis results, growth phenotypes, or interaction assays are described.  
- **Why it matters** The central mechanistic claim of the paper rests on the functional necessity of these specific residues. Without direct evidence, the claim is unsupported and cannot be distinguished from correlation or incidental conservation.  
- **Resolution test** Provide mutagenesis data showing that alteration of VTG or Y residues leads to loss of carboxysome formation or function in vivo, with appropriate controls and quantitative readouts (e.g., growth curves, microscopy, or biochemical assays).

- **Concern ID** R1-M2  
- **Severity** Major  
- **Blocking** Yes  
- **Axis** Technical validation  
- **Claim pointer** The abstract claims "in vitro reconstitution of the α-carboxysome into spherical condensates with CsoS2, Rubisco, and shell proteins" and that this "show[s] the utility of reconstitution as a biochemical tool."  
- **Evidence pointer** Abstract only; location not provided  
- **Concern** No details are given on the composition, size, morphology, or protein stoichiometry of the reconstituted condensates. It is unclear whether these condensates resemble native carboxysomes structurally or functionally, or whether they are merely nonspecific aggregates.  
- **Why it matters** The reconstitution claim is a major advance if validated, but without characterization data, the condensates could be artifacts of the in vitro conditions. The utility as a tool depends on the system faithfully recapitulating native assembly.  
- **Resolution test** Provide electron microscopy or light scattering data showing condensate size and morphology, protein composition analysis, and functional assays (e.g., CO2 fixation or shell permeability) to demonstrate relevance to native carboxysomes.
