- **Shared manuscript claim summary** The authors report structural, thermodynamic, and functional characterization of the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid N-terminal domain (N-NTD) and its SR-rich variant (N-NTD-SR) binding to single- and double-stranded DNA derived from transcriptional regulatory sequences (TRS). ## Reviewer
- **Overall assessment** This manuscript presents a comprehensive biophysical study of the SARS-CoV-2 N-NTD and N-NTD-SR interactions with TRS-derived DNA, combining experimental thermodynamics, NMR spectroscopy, computational modeling, and functional assays for melting and LLPS. The work is timely and relevant given the ongoing pandemic and the importance of N protein in viral replication. The authors provide novel insights into potential TRS specificity, thermodynamic signatures distinguishing ssDNA versus dsDNA binding, and SR-motif-dependent phase separation. However, several claims, particularly regarding specificity and the mechanistic link between structural positioning and melting activity, are inferred from indirect evidence and require stronger experimental support. The absence of key quantitative data in the visible text limits full assessment. The manuscript is technically sound in its methodology but would benefit from clearer presentation of controls, statistical analyses, and direct comparisons to RNA counterparts.
- **Who would be interested in the results, and why** Structural biologists and biophysicists studying nucleic acid-protein interactions, particularly those focused on coronavirus nucleocapsid proteins. Researchers investigating liquid-liquid phase separation in viral contexts. Drug discovery groups targeting N protein for antiviral development, given the proposed relevance for inhibitor and aptamer design. The thermodynamic and structural data may also interest computational biologists developing force fields or docking protocols for nucleic acid-protein complexes.
- **Major strengths** 1) Comprehensive multi-technique approach combining ITC-like thermodynamics (via fluorescence), NMR CSP mapping, docking, MD simulations, and functional LLPS assays. 2) Novel finding of TRS-specific thermodynamic patterns (ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0 for ssTRS; ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0 for dsTRS) that may inform specificity mechanisms. 3) Demonstration of SR-motif-dependent LLPS with nucleic acid sequence selectivity, providing new insights into N protein phase behavior. 4) Use of DNA as a stable RNA mimic is pragmatic and well-justified. 5) Integration of experimental and computational data to propose a structural model for melting activity.
- **Major Concerns** 
  - R1-M1: The claim of TRS-specific recognition is not fully supported by the affinity data presented. The authors report modest differences between TRS and NS binding affinities (Table S7) but do not provide statistical comparisons or effect sizes. The thermodynamic pattern differences are qualitative and based on van't Hoff analysis with assumptions of constant ΔH that may not hold. The specificity claim requires more rigorous quantitative evidence.
  - R1-M2: The mechanistic link between the proposed structural positioning of dsTRS (∼25° tilt difference) and melting activity is speculative. The authors do not provide direct experimental evidence (e.g., mutagenesis of E174 or TTT-interacting residues) to test this hypothesis. The MD simulations are used to infer causality without functional validation.
  - R1-M3: The LLPS experiments lack critical controls and quantitative characterization. The authors do not report saturation concentrations, critical concentration measurements, or rheological properties of the condensates. The claim that dsTRS maintains liquidity while dsNS leads to crystallization is based on qualitative microscopy observations without time-resolved or quantitative analysis. The role of PEG-4000 as a crowding agent is not systematically varied.
  - R1-M4: The use of DNA as a proxy for RNA is a significant limitation that is not adequately addressed. While the authors cite similar CSP profiles between DNA and RNA, they do not directly compare melting activity or LLPS between DNA and RNA substrates. The conclusions about biological relevance for viral transcription rely on RNA, yet all functional assays use DNA.
- **Minor Comments** 
  - R1-m1: The manuscript would benefit from a schematic figure summarizing the proposed model of TRS recognition and melting mechanism.
  - R1-m2: The authors should clarify the number of replicates for fluorescence and FRET experiments and provide error bars or confidence intervals in figures.
  - R1-m3: The discussion of thermodynamic parameters (ΔH, ΔS) from van't Hoff analysis should acknowledge the limitations of this approach compared to direct calorimetry.
  - R1-m4: The authors should specify the exact composition of the LLPS buffer and justify the choice of PEG-4000 concentration.
  - R1-m5: The statement that "N-NTD-SR exhibits the highest LLPS propensity" based on catGRANULE predictions should be tempered, as this algorithm has known limitations for structured domains.
- **Technical failings that need to be addressed before the case is established** 1) Lack of direct comparison between DNA and RNA substrates for melting and LLPS assays. 2) Absence of mutagenesis experiments to validate the proposed E174 and TTT motif interaction. 3) Insufficient statistical treatment of binding affinities and thermodynamic parameters. 4) Missing quantitative LLPS characterization (e.g., turbidity dose-response curves, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching). 5) The MD simulation methodology (force field accuracy for nucleic acid-protein complexes, convergence criteria) is not fully described.
- **Assessment against Nature-style criteria** 
  - Originality: Moderate to high. The application of combined thermodynamic, structural, and phase separation analyses to N-NTD with TRS DNA is novel, though individual techniques are established. The TRS-specific thermodynamic signature is a new observation.
  - Scientific importance: Moderate. The findings advance understanding of N protein nucleic acid interactions and phase behavior, but the direct relevance to viral replication mechanisms remains inferential. The potential for inhibitor design is speculative at this stage.
  - Interdisciplinary readership: Moderate. The work bridges structural biology, biophysics, and virology, but the technical depth may limit accessibility to a broad audience. The LLPS component broadens appeal to cell biology readers.
  - Technical soundness: Generally sound methodology, but the specificity claims and mechanistic interpretations are not fully validated. The reliance on DNA as a proxy for RNA is a notable weakness.
  - Readability for nonspecialists: The manuscript is clearly written but assumes familiarity with NMR, MD simulations, and phase separation concepts. A schematic model figure would improve accessibility.
- **Recommendation posture** Supportive if technical concerns are resolved. The core observations are potentially valuable, but the specificity claim, mechanistic hypothesis, and biological relevance require stronger experimental support. The authors should address the RNA versus DNA issue, provide mutagenesis validation, and strengthen quantitative analyses before the case is fully established.

## Risk / unsupported claims
- The claim that N-NTD/N-NTD-SR "specifically" recognizes TRS is not quantitatively supported; affinity differences are modest and not statistically validated.
- The proposal that E174 acts as a "trigger" for dsDNA/dsRNA melting is speculative without mutagenesis or direct binding energy decomposition experiments.
- The assertion that DNA binding is a valid proxy for RNA function in melting and LLPS is not experimentally demonstrated.
- The conclusion that TRS-induced LLPS has biological relevance for viral transcription is inferential and not tested in cellular or viral contexts.
- The thermodynamic pattern (ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0 for ssTRS; ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0 for dsTRS) is presented as a "well-defined pattern" but is based on van't Hoff analysis with assumptions that may not be valid across the temperature range studied.
- The claim that N-NTD-SR condensates are "liquid" is based on qualitative microscopy and limited turbidity data; no rheological or FRAP measurements are provided.
